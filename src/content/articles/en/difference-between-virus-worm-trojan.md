When a laptop starts acting strangely, someone usually says "it's got a virus." That's fine as everyday shorthand, but security experts use the word much more narrowly. The **difference between a virus, a worm and a Trojan** is really about how each one reaches you: a virus rides on a file and waits for you to open it, a worm spreads across networks on its own, and a Trojan tricks you into installing it yourself. Once you see that, it becomes clear why some malware can circle the globe in hours while other kinds sit still until somebody makes a mistake.

## Malware: the umbrella term

**Malware**, short for malicious software, covers any program built to damage a device or network, steal data, or take control of a system without the owner knowing. Viruses, worms and Trojans all sit under that umbrella. They aren't ranked by how much harm they do. What separates them is how they get in and how they spread.

## What is a computer virus?

The name is a good analogy. A biological virus can't survive alone; it has to get inside a cell. A computer virus works the same way, attaching itself to a legitimate file or program known as its host. Until someone opens that file or runs that program, the virus just sits there, dormant.

Since it can't move by itself, a virus depends on people to carry it around. An infected file gets emailed to a friend, copied onto a USB stick that travels between a home computer and the school lab, or shared online. Once it runs, it can corrupt files, slow the machine down or delete data.

## What is a computer worm?

A worm is **standalone malware**. It doesn't need a host file, and usually it doesn't need the user to do anything at all. Instead it exploits weaknesses in operating systems or networks and copies itself from one machine to the next automatically.

That independence is what makes worms the fastest spreaders. Picture an office network with hundreds of computers: one infected machine can pass the worm to hundreds of others in a short time. A worm doesn't even have to carry anything destructive to cause trouble, because all those copies generate enough traffic to clog the network.

## What is a Trojan horse?

The Trojan takes its name from the Greek legend of the wooden horse that carried hidden soldiers into the city of Troy, and the trick is the same. A Trojan is **disguised as something useful**: a free game, a "PC speed booster", or a pirated copy of a paid app.

Unlike viruses and worms, a Trojan usually doesn't copy itself. It doesn't need to, because the user installs it willingly, believing it's legitimate. Once it's on the device it opens a back door, which can be used to steal passwords or banking details, give an attacker remote control, or download more malware.

## Virus vs worm vs Trojan at a glance

| | Virus | Worm | Trojan |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Needs a host file? | Yes | No | No (it poses as a program) |
| Copies itself? | Yes | Yes, automatically | Usually not |
| Needs user action? | Yes (opening the file) | Usually not | Yes (installing it) |
| Spreading speed | Moderate | Very fast | Limited |
| Typical goal | Damaging files | Spreading and paving the way for other attacks | Spying, theft and remote control |

> **Did you know?** The Morris worm, released on 2 November 1988, was one of the first worms to spread across the internet. It's estimated to have infected about 6,000 machines, roughly a tenth of the computers online at the time, and it led to the creation of the first Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT).

## Famous examples from history

Self-spreading programs didn't start out as weapons. Creeper, which appeared in 1971, was one of the first programs to move between computers by itself, but it was an experiment, not an attack. All it did was display a cheeky message.

The Morris worm of 1988 had a harmless goal too: it was meant to measure the size of the internet. A programming error, however, made it reinfect the same machines over and over until they ground to a halt. It's a useful reminder that a worm can do real damage even when nobody intended it to.

In May 2000 the ILOVEYOU worm arrived by email, dressed up as a love letter, and reached millions of users worldwide within days. It combined two tricks at once: fooling people into opening the attachment, then spreading on its own.

WannaCry, in May 2017, was ransomware that spread like a worm through a Windows vulnerability and hit computers in more than 150 countries. The frustrating part is that a security update fixing the flaw had been released about a month earlier. The machines that got hit were mainly the ones that hadn't installed it.

## How to protect your devices

WannaCry makes the case for the first habit on this list better than any warning could. The others cover the remaining ways in:

1. Keep your operating system and apps updated. Most worms exploit old vulnerabilities that already have fixes.
2. Don't open unexpected attachments or links, even when they seem to come from someone you know.
3. Download software only from official sources, and avoid pirated copies and suspicious "free" tools.
4. Use reputable security software and keep your firewall on.
5. Back up important files somewhere separate from your device, so you can recover from ransomware.
6. Turn on two-step verification for important accounts, so a stolen password alone isn't enough.

Notice how the habits line up with the three types: updates shut worms out, caution with attachments stops viruses, and sticking to official downloads keeps Trojans away.

## Questions students ask about malware

### Is all malware a virus?

People often talk as if it is. Technically, though, a virus is just one type of malware, alongside worms, Trojans, ransomware, spyware and others.

### Can phones get malware too?

Yes. Smartphones are targets as well, especially when apps are installed from outside the official stores. Trojans are the most common type on phones, since a fake app is the easiest way to fool someone into letting malware in.

### What is ransomware?

Ransomware encrypts your files and demands payment to decrypt them. It can arrive through a worm, a Trojan or an infected email attachment. Security agencies generally advise against paying, since payment doesn't guarantee you'll get your files back.

### Are Macs and Linux computers completely safe?

No system is completely safe. There is more malware aimed at Windows because it's so widely used, but macOS and Linux are attacked too, especially internet-facing [servers](/en/articles/what-is-a-server/). And as [artificial intelligence](/en/articles/what-is-artificial-intelligence/) advances, good security habits matter more every day, whatever system you use.
