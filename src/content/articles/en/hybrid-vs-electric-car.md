Every time petrol prices climb, the same debate comes back around the family dinner table: hybrid vs electric car, which one should we buy? The honest answer is that **it depends on where you can charge and how far you drive each day**. Neither type wins for everyone. To see why, it helps to know what is actually going on under the bonnet of each one.

## Three kinds of car, not two

People often lump these cars together, but there are really three distinct designs, and the difference comes down to where the energy comes from.

A **regular hybrid (HEV)** pairs a petrol engine with a small electric motor and a battery. The battery tops itself up from the engine and from braking, so you never plug it in. The car that made the idea famous was the Toyota Prius, launched in Japan in 1997.

A plug-in hybrid (PHEV) carries a bigger battery that you charge from the mains. That lets it run on electricity alone for anywhere from a few dozen to around a hundred kilometres, depending on the model. Once the battery is flat, the petrol engine simply takes over and it behaves like a regular hybrid, so you are never stranded.

A fully electric car (BEV) has no petrol engine at all. It runs only on electricity and is charged at home or at public charging stations. Most modern models cover roughly 300 to 600 km on a single charge.

## Hybrid vs electric: the comparison at a glance

Since we are comparing three cars on the same points, a table is the clearest way to lay it out:

| | Regular hybrid | Plug-in hybrid | Fully electric |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Energy | Petrol + self-charging electric | Electric + petrol | Electric only |
| Needs charging? | No | Yes (optional) | Yes |
| Running cost | Low | Very low if charged regularly | Usually the lowest |
| Maintenance | Similar to petrol | Similar to petrol | Less: no engine oil, far fewer moving parts |
| Long trips | Excellent | Excellent | Need to plan charging stops |
| Purchase price | Medium | Higher | Varies widely by model and taxes |

Notice that both hybrids cost about the same to maintain as a petrol car. That makes sense: the engine, its oil and its parts are all still there. An electric car saves on servicing mainly because it has so few moving parts.

## When an electric car makes sense

Start with one question: can you charge at home or at work? If you have your own parking space with power, the car charges overnight much like your phone does, and you leave every morning with a full battery.

Then think about your daily driving. Someone who commutes to the office and drops the children at school on the way usually knows their mileage, and it tends to be far below the car's range. If that sounds like you, and you want the lowest running and maintenance costs, an electric car is a strong fit, provided there are chargers in the places you travel to.

## When a hybrid is the easier choice

Picture a family in an apartment block with no private parking, leaving the car on the street each night. They have nowhere to plug in, and an electric car would make life harder for them. A hybrid needs no plug, so it suits them naturally.

The same goes for people who regularly drive long distances, or into areas with few or no chargers. A hybrid fills up at any petrol station and keeps going. It is also the right pick for anyone who wants to use less fuel without changing how they drive or refuel.

> **Did you know?** Electric cars recover some energy when braking, recharging the battery and relying less on the friction brakes. That's why their brake pads often last longer.

## How long does charging take?

It depends on the type of charger. Ordinary home charging takes anywhere from several hours to overnight, depending on battery size and charger power, which is why it works best while you sleep. Fast charging gets many modern cars to about 80% in roughly 20 to 40 minutes, about the length of a coffee stop on a long drive.

## What to check before you buy

Work through these one at a time before you hand over any money:

1. Check battery health on a used car; the battery is the most expensive part of an electric car.
2. Ask about the battery warranty. Many manufacturers cover it for many years or a high mileage.
3. Look at the charging port type, such as CCS2 or GB/T, and make sure it suits the chargers in your country.
4. Remember the heat: air conditioning and hot weather reduce range somewhat.
5. Compare the total cost over several years, including purchase, fuel or electricity, maintenance and insurance, not just the price tag.
6. Make sure servicing and spare parts for the brand are available where you live.

Point five is the one people skip most often, yet the cheapest car on the showroom floor can turn out to be the most expensive after a few years of fuel and repairs.

## Questions families ask before choosing a hybrid or electric car

### Does an electric car battery need replacing soon?

Not usually. Modern car batteries are designed to last many years, though their capacity slowly drops with use and age, so the range shrinks a little over time.

### Is a hybrid cheaper to run than a petrol car?

Usually, yes. The gap is biggest in city traffic, where the electric motor does more of the work and all that stopping and starting feeds energy back into the battery.

### Which is better for city driving and frequent long trips?

If you can charge at home and mostly drive around town, an electric car is usually cheaper to run. If you can't charge, or you often drive long distances between cities, a hybrid is more convenient, and a plug-in hybrid sits in between for people who want a bit of both. Before visiting a showroom, it is worth answering two questions honestly: can I charge at home, and how far do I drive each day?
