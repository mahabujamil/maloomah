Ask an older relative what summers were like when they were young, and many will tell you the heat used to be shorter and the rain more dependable. Today rain in much of the Arab world seems to arrive late or all at once, and heatwaves drag on. Scientists have a name for this long-term shift: **climate change**. It isn't a far-off news story any more. Here is what it is, why it's happening, and how it affects Jordan and the region around it.

## What is climate change?

Climate change means long-term shifts in temperatures and weather patterns on Earth. The planet's climate has changed naturally many times, but the change since the 19th century is different, because it is mainly caused by **human activity**, above all burning fossil fuels: coal, oil and gas.

Students often mix up weather and climate, so it helps to pin them down. Weather is what you see out of the window this morning. Climate is the pattern that repeats year after year:

| | Weather | Climate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time span | Hours, days or a week | An average over 30 years or more |
| Example | "Rain in Amman tomorrow" | "Jordan has cool, rainy winters and hot, dry summers" |
| How fast it changes | From one day to the next | Slowly, over decades |

## How does the greenhouse effect work?

Picture sleeping under a light blanket on a winter night, then someone throws two more on top. Your body makes the same heat as before, but more of it is trapped, so you go from cosy to too hot. Earth is in a similar position.

The planet is wrapped in a layer of gases that lets sunlight in and traps some of the heat the ground gives off. This natural process is the **greenhouse effect**, and we need it: without it, Earth would be too cold for life. The trouble is that people have added a great deal more of these greenhouse gases, thickening the blanket and warming the planet.

Carbon dioxide (CO₂) matters most. It comes from burning fuel and clearing forests, and its concentration has climbed from about 280 parts per million before the Industrial Revolution to about 423 parts per million in 2024. That is the highest level on record, and 2024's rise was the largest since modern measurements began in the 1950s. Methane is next, released by farming, livestock, landfills and gas leaks. Nitrous oxide, from fertilisers and some industries, is the third.

Methane is known to be a powerful gas, so why all the attention on carbon dioxide? Because CO₂ is responsible for the largest share of human-caused warming. We release enormous amounts of it, and it lingers in the air for a long time. Methane comes second.

## Causes of climate change close to home

"Emissions" can sound like something that happens in distant factories, but the causes of climate change are close to home. Around the world, much of the electricity that runs air conditioners and fridges is generated from coal, oil and gas. Transport burns fuel too: the school bus, family cars, trucks, planes and ships.

Industry adds its share, especially cement and steel, the materials our homes and schools are built from. Cutting down forests does double damage, since trees absorb carbon dioxide and losing them means losing that sponge. Agriculture and waste are major sources of methane; food scraps thrown in the bin rot in landfills and give the gas off.

## How much has Earth warmed?

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) found that global average temperature in 2011–2020 was about 1.1 °C higher than before the Industrial Revolution. More recent figures from the World Meteorological Organization fill in the picture:

| Fact | Figure |
| --- | --- |
| Hottest year on record | 2024, about 1.55 °C above pre-industrial levels |
| Where 2025 ranks | Second or third hottest, depending on the dataset |
| Hottest years on record | The eleven years from 2015 to 2025 |
| Estimated long-term warming | About 1.3–1.4 °C |

A common mistake: one year above 1.5 °C doesn't mean the Paris Agreement goal has been missed. That goal is measured as an average over about twenty years, not a single year.

One and a half degrees sounds tiny, but it is an average for the whole planet, oceans and ice sheets included, and a shift that size is enough to cause major changes in weather systems around the world.

> **Did you know?** Scientific studies suggest the Eastern Mediterranean and Middle East is warming at nearly **twice the global average rate**, making it one of the regions most exposed to climate change.

## Effects of climate change

The effect people feel first is heat: heatwaves are getting longer and more intense. Rainfall is shifting as well. Some places face repeated droughts, while others are hit by sudden floods and downpours that can swamp streets within hours.

At the poles and in the mountains, glaciers and sea ice are melting. Sea level is rising for two reasons: meltwater pours into the oceans, and seawater expands as it warms. Since 1900 the sea has risen by about 20 cm.

The damage reaches people directly. When farming and water sources suffer, food and water security are threatened. Heat stress and the spread of some diseases are health risks. Wildlife is under pressure too, and coral bleaching in warming seas is one of the clearest examples of the threat to biodiversity.

## Climate change in Jordan

Jordan is one of the most water-scarce countries in the world, and that is exactly why it is so sensitive to a changing climate. Rainfall is declining and becoming more erratic, which strains dams and groundwater. Temperatures are rising and summer heatwaves are more frequent, which hurts agriculture, rain-fed crops most of all. And summer is when demand for water and electricity peaks, since people need more of both just as supplies tighten.

In response, Jordan's 2021 climate plan update raised its emissions-reduction target to **31% by 2030** (part of it conditional on international support). The country has expanded solar and wind power significantly and is working on the National Water Carrier project, which will desalinate Red Sea water in Aqaba and pump it to Amman and other governorates.

## What is the world doing?

Greenhouse gases ignore borders, so no country can solve this alone. In 2015 countries signed the **Paris Agreement**, committing to cut emissions to keep warming well below 2 °C while pursuing efforts to limit it to 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels.

Arab countries have hosted the UN climate summits several times: Qatar in Doha in 2012, Morocco in Marrakech in 2016 and Egypt in Sharm El-Sheikh in 2022.

## Questions students ask about climate change

### Are global warming and climate change the same thing?

They're linked but not identical. Global warming is the rise in Earth's temperature caused by greenhouse gases. Climate change is the bigger picture: the warming plus everything it sets off, from changing rainfall and wind to extreme events.

### Is it too late to stop climate change?

No. Every fraction of a degree avoided makes a big difference, and the shift to clean energy is speeding up worldwide.

## How can you help?

What one student does may seem small next to a planet-sized problem, but habits repeated in millions of homes add up. A few things within your reach:

- Save electricity: switch off unused devices and use LED bulbs.
- Use water wisely; it's precious in our region.
- Drive less when you can, or share rides with classmates and neighbours.
- Talk with your family about solar power for your home if possible.
- Reduce waste and recycle, and don't waste food.
- Plant a tree, and raise awareness among family and friends.

People caused this problem by releasing these gases, which also means people can fix it. In a country like Jordan, where water is scarce and sunshine is plentiful, solar power and careful water use aren't luxuries. They're necessities.
