When someone says "I've got a headache", the first useful question is not "how bad?" but "where, and what does it feel like?" There are several **types of headaches**, and the location and character of the pain are the first clues to what's behind it. They also help you separate an ordinary headache that rest will fix from one that needs a doctor, or an emergency department, right away.

Headaches are about as common as health complaints get. The World Health Organization estimates that about 40% of people worldwide live with a headache disorder, roughly 3.1 billion people in 2021, and that these disorders are more common in women.

## Types of headaches and causes: primary vs secondary

Doctors begin by sorting headaches into two groups. In a **primary headache**, the headache itself is the condition rather than a symptom of something else; tension headache, migraine and cluster headache are the best known. A **secondary headache** is a symptom of another cause, such as sinusitis, a cold, a head injury or overusing painkillers. Only rarely does it signal something serious.

### Tension headache

This is the most common type, and people describe it in remarkably similar ways: pressure or tightness on both sides of the head, like a band being pulled tight, sometimes spreading into the neck and shoulders. It's usually mild to moderate, so most people can carry on with their day. Stress, poor sleep and long hours of bad posture are the usual companions. Think of a student hunched over revision notes late into the night during exam week, and you have the classic setting.

### Migraine

A migraine feels different. The pain throbs or pulses, usually on one side of the head, and it often brings nausea or vomiting along with sensitivity to light and sound, which is why people with a migraine tend to want a dark, quiet room. An attack commonly lasts from about 4 hours to 3 days. Some people get an "aura" beforehand, such as flashing lights or zigzag lines across their vision. Migraine is a real medical condition that deserves a proper diagnosis, not just "a bad headache".

### Cluster headache

Cluster headache is rare, but it ranks among the most painful conditions known. The pain is sharp, burning or piercing, centred **around one eye** or on one side of the head, and attacks usually last between 15 minutes and 3 hours. On the same side, the eye may water and turn red and the nostril may run or block. The name comes from the pattern: attacks arrive in "clusters" that can last weeks or months. Where someone with a migraine wants to lie still, a person in a cluster attack often feels restless and can't sit still at all.

### Sinus headache

This one brings pain and pressure around the cheeks, eyes and forehead, usually with a blocked or runny nose, and it often gets worse when you bend forward to pick something up. It's also one of the most over-diagnosed labels. Many people who think they have "sinus headaches" actually have migraine, because the symptoms overlap.

### Medication-overuse headache

It sounds backwards: the tablets you take for headaches can end up causing them. Taking headache painkillers too often for too long can lead to near-daily headaches, and UK guidance says this typically happens when they're taken on more than about half the days of the month. If you notice you're reaching for painkillers that often, treat it as a sign to see a doctor.

## Types of headaches and location

Where the pain sits gives a first clue only, and it's never enough for a diagnosis on its own. Still, it narrows things down:

| Where it hurts | Likely types |
| --- | --- |
| Around the head like a band (sides and forehead) | Usually tension headache |
| Front (forehead, eyes, cheeks) | Tension headache, sinus headache, eye strain |
| One side of the head | Often migraine |
| Around or behind one eye | Cluster headache, sometimes migraine |
| Back of the head and neck | Tension headache, tight neck muscles, poor screen posture |
| The whole head | Tension headache, dehydration, fever or a cold |

### Headache at the back of the head

The usual culprit is tight neck and shoulder muscles after long hours looking down at a phone or laptop, often helped along by poor sleep or stress. A sudden, severe pain at the back of the head with a stiff neck is a completely different matter. That is an emergency, and it's covered in the warning signs below.

### Headache at the front of the head

This is most often a tension headache or eye strain from screens or long stretches of reading. A cold or sinusitis can do it too. Sometimes the cause is simpler still: not drinking or eating enough, like a long school day that started without breakfast.

> **Did you know?** Brain tissue itself has no pain receptors, so the brain can't "feel" pain directly. Headache pain comes from the membranes around the brain, the blood vessels, and the nerves and muscles of the head and neck.

## Common triggers of frequent headaches

When headaches keep coming back, the reason is often an everyday habit that's easy to overlook. Sleep is the first suspect: too little of it, or an irregular schedule such as late nights at the weekend followed by early school mornings. (See also [how much sleep do you need](/en/articles/how-much-sleep-do-you-need/).) Water comes next. Not drinking enough is a well-known trigger, especially in hot weather, so it's worth checking [how much water should you drink a day](/en/articles/how-much-water-should-you-drink/).

Food matters as well, since skipping meals and going hungry for long stretches is enough to set off a headache in many people. Stress plays its part, and so do long screen sessions with poor posture and no breaks. Caffeine cuts both ways: too much can cause headaches, and so can stopping it suddenly. Then there's painkiller overuse, described above.

If headaches still keep coming back or getting worse after you've looked at these, see a doctor to find the actual cause.

## Everyday habits that help prevent headaches

No single routine works for everyone, and none of this replaces medical advice, but a few general habits help a lot of people. The steadiest one is going to bed and waking up at regular times where you can, including at weekends. Drinking water through the day, rather than all at once, helps too, as does not skipping meals and eating a balanced diet.

If you spend long hours at a screen, take short breaks and stretch your neck and shoulders. Staying active, with regular walks for example, along with finding ways to relax and manage stress, does the head as much good as the rest of the body.

One habit people often skip is keeping a headache diary. Write down when each headache started, how long it lasted and what came before it. Short night? Missed lunch? After a few weeks a pattern often appears, and that helps both you and your doctor spot the triggers.

## When is a headache an emergency?

Most headaches aren't serious, which is reassuring. But the NHS and other health services advise **calling an ambulance or going to the emergency department immediately** if a headache comes with any of these:

- It is sudden and extremely severe, peaking within seconds or minutes, like "the worst headache of your life".
- A high temperature and a stiff neck, or discomfort from bright light.
- A rash that doesn't fade when you press a glass against it.
- It follows a blow or injury to the head.
- Weakness or numbness on one side of the body, a drooping face or trouble speaking.
- Confusion, severe drowsiness, fainting or a seizure.
- Sudden loss of vision or double vision.

Don't drive yourself to hospital in these situations. Ask someone to take you, or call an ambulance.

Between an ordinary headache and an emergency there's a middle ground that calls for seeing a doctor soon, though not necessarily the emergency department: headaches that keep returning or getting worse, that don't improve, that start for the first time after age 50, that wake you from sleep, or that come with jaw pain when chewing or a tender scalp.

## Questions people ask about headache types

### What is the difference between a tension headache and a migraine?

The easiest way to tell them apart is how they feel. A tension headache is pressure or tightness on both sides of the head, usually mild to moderate, so you can often keep going with school or work. A migraine is a throbbing pain, usually on one side, often with nausea and sensitivity to light and sound that make you want to stop everything.

### What are the most common types of headaches?

Tension headache is the most common, followed by migraine. Cluster headache is rare but extremely painful.
