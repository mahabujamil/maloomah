Almost everyone gets a headache now and then, but not all headaches are the same. Knowing the main **types of headaches**, what causes them and where the pain sits helps you make sense of what is going on, and helps you tell an ordinary headache from one that needs a doctor or an emergency department right away. Here are the main types, a location chart and the warning signs to take seriously.

The **World Health Organization** estimates that about 40% of people worldwide live with a headache disorder, roughly 3.1 billion people in 2021, and that these disorders are more common in women.

## Types of headaches and causes: primary vs secondary

Doctors split headaches into two broad groups:

- **Primary headaches:** the headache itself is the condition, not a symptom of something else. The best known are tension headache, migraine and cluster headache.
- **Secondary headaches:** the headache is a symptom of another cause, such as sinusitis, a cold, a head injury or overusing painkillers. Rarely, it signals something serious.

### 1. Tension headache

The most common type. It usually feels like pressure or tightness on both sides of the head, **like a band squeezing your head**, and can spread to the neck and shoulders. It is typically mild to moderate and doesn't usually stop you from getting on with your day. Stress, poor sleep and long hours of bad posture are common links.

### 2. Migraine

A throbbing or pulsing pain, usually on **one side of the head**, often with nausea or vomiting and sensitivity to light and sound. An attack commonly lasts from about 4 hours to 3 days. Some people get an "aura" first, such as flashing lights or zigzag lines. Migraine is a real medical condition that deserves a proper diagnosis, not just "a bad headache".

### 3. Cluster headache

Rare, but one of the most painful conditions known. It causes a sharp, burning or piercing pain **around one eye** or on one side of the head, and attacks usually last between 15 minutes and 3 hours. The eye on that side may water and turn red, and the nostril may run or block. Attacks come in "clusters" that can last weeks or months, and people often feel restless and unable to sit still during one.

### 4. Sinus headache

Pain and pressure around the cheeks, eyes and forehead, usually with a blocked or runny nose, and often worse when you bend forward. Many people who think they have "sinus headaches" actually have migraine, as the symptoms overlap.

### 5. Medication-overuse headache

It sounds odd, but taking headache painkillers **too often for too long** can cause near-daily headaches. UK guidance says this typically happens when they are taken on more than about half the days of the month. If you find yourself reaching for painkillers that often, it's a sign to see a doctor.

## Types of headaches and location

Where the pain sits gives a first clue only. It is not enough for a diagnosis:

| Where it hurts | Likely types |
| --- | --- |
| **Around the head like a band (sides and forehead)** | Usually tension headache |
| **Front (forehead, eyes, cheeks)** | Tension headache, sinus headache, eye strain |
| **One side of the head** | Often migraine |
| **Around or behind one eye** | Cluster headache, sometimes migraine |
| **Back of the head and neck** | Tension headache, tight neck muscles, poor screen posture |
| **The whole head** | Tension headache, dehydration, fever or a cold |

### Headache at the back of the head

Most often it comes from tight neck and shoulder muscles after long hours looking down at a phone or laptop, poor sleep or stress. A sudden, severe pain at the back of the head with a stiff neck is a different matter: that is an emergency (see the warning signs below).

### Headache at the front of the head

Usually a tension headache, eye strain from screens or long reading, a cold or sinusitis, or simply not drinking or eating enough.

> **Did you know?** Brain tissue itself has no pain receptors, so the brain can't "feel" pain directly. Headache pain comes from the membranes around the brain, the blood vessels, and the nerves and muscles of the head and neck.

## Common triggers of frequent headaches

Recurring or daily headaches often have everyday triggers:

- **Too little sleep or an irregular sleep schedule.** See also [how much sleep do you need](/en/articles/how-much-sleep-do-you-need/).
- **Not drinking enough water**, especially in hot weather. See [how much water should you drink a day](/en/articles/how-much-water-should-you-drink/).
- **Skipping meals** and going hungry for long stretches.
- **Stress.**
- **Long screen sessions** with poor posture and no breaks.
- **Too much caffeine, or stopping it suddenly.**
- **Overusing painkillers**, as above.

If headaches keep coming back or getting worse, see a doctor to find the cause.

## Everyday habits that help prevent headaches

General habits that help many people (not a substitute for medical advice):

1. **Go to bed and wake up at regular times** where you can.
2. **Drink water steadily** through the day.
3. **Don't skip meals**, and eat a balanced diet.
4. **Take short screen breaks** and stretch your neck and shoulders.
5. **Stay active**, for example with regular walks, and find ways to relax and manage stress.
6. **Keep a headache diary:** note the time, how long it lasted and what came before. It helps you and your doctor spot triggers.

## When is a headache an emergency?

Most headaches aren't serious. But the NHS and other health services advise **calling an ambulance or going to the emergency department immediately** if a headache comes with any of these:

- It is **sudden and extremely severe**, peaking within seconds or minutes, like "the worst headache of your life".
- A **high temperature and a stiff neck**, or discomfort from bright light.
- A **rash that doesn't fade** when you press a glass against it.
- It follows a **blow or injury to the head**.
- **Weakness or numbness** on one side of the body, a **drooping face** or **trouble speaking**.
- **Confusion, severe drowsiness, fainting or a seizure**.
- **Sudden loss of vision or double vision**.

Don't drive yourself to hospital in these situations. Ask someone to take you, or call an ambulance.

See a doctor **soon** (not necessarily the emergency department) if headaches keep returning or getting worse, don't improve, start for the first time after age 50, wake you from sleep, or come with jaw pain when chewing or a tender scalp.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the most common types of headaches?

Tension headache is the most common, followed by migraine. Cluster headache is rare but extremely painful.

### What is the difference between a tension headache and a migraine?

A tension headache is pressure or tightness on both sides of the head and is usually mild to moderate. A migraine is a throbbing pain, usually on one side, often with nausea and sensitivity to light and sound.

### When is a headache an emergency?

When it is sudden and extremely severe, comes with fever and a stiff neck, follows a head injury, or comes with weakness, confusion, or problems with vision or speech. Go to the emergency department straight away.

## Key takeaway

There are many types of headaches, and the most common are tension headache, migraine, cluster headache, sinus headache and medication-overuse headache. The location of the pain gives a first clue, but diagnosis is a doctor's job. Simple habits like regular sleep, enough water, regular meals and screen breaks help reduce attacks. A sudden severe headache, or one with fever and a stiff neck, weakness or confusion, needs emergency care right away.
