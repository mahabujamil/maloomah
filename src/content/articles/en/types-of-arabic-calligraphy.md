The same Arabic letter can look completely different on a mosque wall, in a schoolchild's notebook and on a book cover. That's because there are many **types of Arabic calligraphy**, each with its own rules, beauty and purpose. In this guide we look at the main Arabic calligraphy styles, how they evolved from angular Kufic to modern digital fonts, and which script is usually recommended for beginners.

## From Kufic to "proportioned script": how the styles evolved

In the first centuries of Islam, **Kufic** dominated. Named after the city of **Kufa** in Iraq, it is an angular, rectilinear script used in many early Qur'an manuscripts and carved on buildings, mosques and coins.

The big leap came in the Abbasid era with **Ibn Muqla** (died about 940 CE), a vizier and calligrapher in Baghdad. He is credited with the system of **proportioned script** (*al-khatt al-mansub*): the basic unit is the **rhombic dot** made by the pen's nib, the height of the letter alif is measured in a set number of these dots, and every other letter is sized relative to the alif. For the first time, the rounded scripts had geometric rules that could be taught and passed on.

Next came **Ibn al-Bawwab** (died about 1022), who refined these rules and gave the scripts new elegance. The proportioned scripts later reached a peak of perfection with **Yaqut al-Musta'simi** in the 13th century.

## The six pens: the 6 types of Arabic calligraphy

The classical rounded scripts settled into six styles known as **al-aqlam al-sitta**, "the six pens". This is the traditional answer when people ask about the 6 types of Arabic calligraphy:

1. **Thuluth**
2. **Naskh**
3. **Muhaqqaq**
4. **Rayhani**
5. **Tawqi'**
6. **Riqa'**

They were often used in pairs, a large script for headings with a smaller companion for the text. Over time **Thuluth** and **Naskh** remained the most widely used, while Muhaqqaq and Rayhani faded.

> **Did you know?** The Chester Beatty Library in Dublin, Ireland, holds a Qur'an copied by **Ibn al-Bawwab** himself in 391 AH (about 1000–1001 CE). It is the only Qur'an known to survive in his hand, and it is written in Naskh on paper.

## The main Arabic calligraphy styles today

These are the scripts most people mean when they talk about the kinds of Arabic calligraphy:

- **Kufic:** geometric and angular, with many branches such as floriated, knotted and square Kufic. Still popular in decoration and logos.
- **Thuluth:** often called the "king of scripts" and the hardest to master. Its large, curving, interlaced letters fill mosque inscriptions, calligraphy panels and book titles.
- **Naskh:** clear, balanced and easy to read. It was used to copy Qur'ans and books for centuries, and most Arabic print typefaces today are based on it.
- **Ruq'ah:** simple, fast and compact. Ottoman calligraphers developed it for everyday writing, and its rules were standardised in the 19th century, a feat usually credited to Mumtaz Efendi. It is the script most people use for handwriting today.
- **Diwani:** born in the Ottoman **divan** (chancery) for decrees and official documents, and usually credited to the calligrapher Ibrahim Munif. Its letters are slanted, interlocking and flowing.
- **Nastaliq (called "Farisi" in Arabic):** emerged in Iran around the late 14th century and is traditionally attributed to Mir Ali Tabrizi. Its long, sloping letters seem to hang, and it is widely used for Persian, Urdu and poetry.
- **Maghribi:** used across North Africa and al-Andalus. Scholars see it as a descendant of Kufic, but it is much rounder and more sweeping, and it is still used in Qur'ans and manuscripts in North and West Africa.

Note that the classical Riqa' of the six pens is a different script from modern Ruq'ah, despite the similar names.

## Modern Arabic calligraphy

Arabic calligraphy didn't stay in manuscripts. Today it underpins the **digital fonts** on computers and phones, many of them based on Naskh or Kufic. Artists also blend Arabic letters with contemporary painting, street art and graphic design, so calligraphy now appears in logos, posters and modern architecture.

In **December 2021**, **UNESCO** inscribed "Arabic calligraphy: knowledge, skills and practices" on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, recognising its artistic value and its role in passing on knowledge. The nomination noted that one of the threats it faces is the shrinking number of specialist calligraphers.

## Which Arabic script should you learn first?

The common advice from calligraphy teachers:

- **Start with Ruq'ah** if you want better everyday handwriting. It is quick and has relatively few rules.
- **Learn Naskh** if you want to understand printed letterforms and write long, clear texts.
- **Leave Thuluth for later.** It takes years of practice, ideally under a teacher.

Beginners usually need a reed pen (qalam) or a broad-edged calligraphy pen, a practice workbook and short daily practice.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many types of Arabic calligraphy are there?

There is no single agreed number, because the scripts have many branches. The best-known are seven or eight: Kufic, Thuluth, Naskh, Ruq'ah, Diwani, Nastaliq and Maghribi, alongside the classical six pens.

### What is the oldest Arabic calligraphy style?

Kufic is one of the oldest and best-known Arabic scripts from the early Islamic centuries, though other, less famous writing styles came before it and alongside it.

### What is the hardest Arabic calligraphy script?

Many calligraphers consider **Thuluth** the hardest, which is why mastering it is seen as the true test of a calligrapher.

### What is the difference between Naskh and Ruq'ah?

Naskh has full, clear letters with extensions and curves, and is used for books and print. Ruq'ah has short, simplified letters written quickly, which makes it ideal for everyday handwriting.

## Key takeaway

The **types of Arabic calligraphy** tell a long story: angular Kufic gave way, through Ibn Muqla and Ibn al-Bawwab, to rounded proportioned scripts and the six pens, including Thuluth and Naskh. The Ottomans added Ruq'ah and Diwani, Iran produced Nastaliq, and North Africa and al-Andalus developed Maghribi. If you want to start, pick Ruq'ah for handwriting and Naskh for clear, readable text.
