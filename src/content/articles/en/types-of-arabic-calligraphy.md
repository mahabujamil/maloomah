Write the word "salam" in Arabic three times: once carved above a mosque door, once in a student's homework, once on a book cover. Same letters, three very different looks. That variety is what people mean by the **types of Arabic calligraphy**. The best-known scripts are Kufic, Thuluth, Naskh, Ruq'ah, Diwani, Nastaliq and Maghribi, plus a classical group called the six pens. Each grew out of a particular time, place and job, and the story of how they appeared explains why they look the way they do.

## From Kufic to "proportioned script": how the styles evolved

For the first few centuries of Islam, the dominant hand was **Kufic**, named after the city of Kufa in Iraq. It is built from straight lines and sharp angles, which made it ideal for carving. You find it in many early Qur'an manuscripts and on buildings, mosques and coins.

Rounded, flowing scripts existed too, but they had a problem: every scribe shaped the letters a little differently. The fix came in Abbasid Baghdad from Ibn Muqla (died about 940 CE), a vizier who was also a calligrapher. He is credited with **proportioned script** (*al-khatt al-mansub*). The unit of measurement is the rhombic dot that the tip of a reed pen leaves on paper. The letter alif is a fixed number of dots tall, and every other letter is sized against the alif. With that one idea, calligraphy became something a teacher could explain and a student could copy accurately.

Ibn al-Bawwab (died about 1022) polished the system and gave the letters a new lightness. The proportioned scripts reached their peak with Yaqut al-Musta'simi in the 13th century.

## The six pens: the 6 types of Arabic calligraphy

If someone asks about the 6 types of Arabic calligraphy, they usually mean *al-aqlam al-sitta*, "the six pens". These are the classical rounded scripts that settled into shape after Ibn Muqla:

1. Thuluth
2. Naskh
3. Muhaqqaq
4. Rayhani
5. Tawqi'
6. Riqa'

Calligraphers tended to pair them, using a large script for headings and a smaller partner for the main text. Thuluth and Naskh have stayed in constant use ever since. Muhaqqaq and Rayhani are rarely seen today.

> **Did you know?** The Chester Beatty Library in Dublin, Ireland, holds a Qur'an copied by Ibn al-Bawwab himself in 391 AH (about 1000–1001 CE). It is the only Qur'an known to survive in his hand, and it is written in Naskh on paper.

## The main Arabic calligraphy styles today

Kufic is still the oldest of the group. It's geometric and angular, and it branched into floriated, knotted and square forms. Designers still borrow it for logos and decoration.

**Thuluth** is often called the "king of scripts", and it's the hardest to master. Its big, curving letters loop over and under each other, so you see it in mosque inscriptions, framed calligraphy panels and book titles rather than in anything written quickly.

Naskh is its practical cousin. It's clear and balanced, it was used to copy Qur'ans and books for centuries, and most Arabic print typefaces, including the ones in school textbooks, are based on it.

If you write Arabic by hand, your own writing is probably closest to **Ruq'ah**. Ottoman calligraphers developed this compact, fast script for everyday use, and its rules were standardised in the 19th century, usually credited to Mumtaz Efendi. More people write in it today than in any other style.

Diwani also came from the Ottomans. It was born in the state chancery, the divan, for decrees and official documents, and is usually credited to Ibrahim Munif. Its slanted, interlocking letters flow together, which is why it's still a favourite for wedding invitations.

Nastaliq, known in Arabic as "Farisi" (Persian script), emerged in Iran around the late 14th century and is traditionally attributed to Mir Ali Tabrizi. Its long letters slope downward as if hanging from a line, and it's the standard script for Persian and Urdu as well as much poetry.

Maghribi spread across North Africa and al-Andalus. Scholars see it as a descendant of Kufic, but it's far rounder and more sweeping, and it's still used in Qur'ans and manuscripts in North and West Africa.

One trap to watch for: the classical Riqa' of the six pens and the modern Ruq'ah are different scripts, even though the names sound almost the same.

## Modern Arabic calligraphy

Calligraphy didn't stay on parchment. The Arabic letters on your phone and laptop are **digital fonts**, and many of them are drawn from Naskh or Kufic. Artists also mix Arabic letters with contemporary painting, street art and graphic design, so calligraphy turns up on posters, brand logos and the fronts of modern buildings.

In December 2021, UNESCO inscribed "Arabic calligraphy: knowledge, skills and practices" on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, recognising both its artistic value and its role in passing on knowledge. The nomination also flagged a real risk: the number of specialist calligraphers is shrinking.

## Which Arabic script should you learn first?

Calligraphy teachers usually suggest a sensible order. If you want neater handwriting in your notebook and exams, start with Ruq'ah, because it's quick and has relatively few rules. If you want to understand how printed letters are built and write long passages clearly, move on to Naskh. Save Thuluth for later; it takes years of practice, ideally with a teacher watching your pen.

You don't need much to begin: a reed pen (qalam) or a broad-edged calligraphy pen and a practice workbook. Habit matters more than equipment. Ten minutes every day will teach you more than one long session a week.

## Questions students ask about Arabic calligraphy

### How many types of Arabic calligraphy are there?

There's no single agreed number, because each script has branches. The best-known are seven or eight: Kufic, Thuluth, Naskh, Ruq'ah, Diwani, Nastaliq and Maghribi, alongside the classical six pens.

### What is the oldest Arabic calligraphy style?

Kufic is one of the oldest and best-known scripts of the early Islamic centuries, although other, less famous writing styles came before it and alongside it.

### What is the difference between Naskh and Ruq'ah?

Naskh has full, clear letters with extensions and curves, so it suits books and print. Ruq'ah shortens and simplifies the letters so they can be written fast, which makes it the everyday handwriting script.
