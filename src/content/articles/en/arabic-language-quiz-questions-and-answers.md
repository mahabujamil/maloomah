Why is Arabic called "the language of the ḍād"? Most Arab students have heard the nickname, but far fewer can explain it when a teacher springs the question in a class quiz. That's what makes an **Arabic language quiz** worth running: it reminds students that Arabic isn't only a set of grammar rules to memorise for exams, but a long story of scholars, poets and books. Below are 40 questions in five rounds, many with a line explaining the answer, because an answer you understand is one you remember. World Arabic Language Day falls on 18 December, so teachers can pull a round or two from here for that day's assembly.

## Using the quiz in class or at assembly

For a school assembly, five or six questions from different rounds is plenty: one student asks, another answers, or the question goes out to the whole line and hands go up. In class, split students into two teams and play one round at a time. The last round suits the youngest pupils; the grammar and literature rounds work best from middle school up.

## History of the Arabic language

| # | Question | Answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | How many letters are in the Arabic alphabet? | 28, from alif to yāʾ |
| 2 | Why is Arabic called "the language of the ḍād"? | The sound of the letter ḍād (ض) was seen as distinctive to Arabic, so it became a name for the language |
| 3 | When is World Arabic Language Day? | 18 December. On that date in 1973 the UN General Assembly passed resolution 3190, making Arabic one of its official and working languages |
| 4 | What are the six official languages of the United Nations? | Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian and Spanish |
| 5 | Which direction is Arabic written in? | Right to left. Persian and Urdu are also written in Arabic script |
| 6 | Who is traditionally credited with founding Arabic grammar? | Abu al-Aswad al-Duʾali, according to the best-known accounts. He also marked the Quran with dots to show vowel endings |
| 7 | Who designed the vowel marks used today, such as fatḥa, ḍamma and kasra? | Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi. He based them on the long-vowel letters: fatḥa is a small slanted alif, ḍamma a small wāw, kasra a small yāʾ |
| 8 | What was the first Arabic dictionary? | *Kitab al-ʿAyn* by al-Khalil. It is named after the letter ʿayn, which it starts with, because he ordered letters by where they are pronounced, from the throat to the lips |
| 9 | Who compiled the dictionary *Lisan al-ʿArab*? | Ibn Manzur. It is one of the largest and best-known Arabic dictionaries |
| 10 | What is the most famous early book of Arabic grammar? | Sibawayh's work, known simply as *Al-Kitab* ("The Book") |

## Arabic grammar questions

| # | Question | Answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 11 | What are the three parts of speech in Arabic? | **Noun, verb and particle**. *Madrasa* (school) is a noun, *kataba* (he wrote) a verb, *fī* (in) a particle |
| 12 | What are the three verb forms? | Past (*darasa*), present (*yadrusu*) and imperative (*udrus*) |
| 13 | What case does the subject of a verb (fāʿil) take? | Always the nominative (marfūʿ), as in *najaḥa al-ṭālibu* |
| 14 | What case does the object (mafʿūl bihi) take? | The accusative (manṣūb), as in *qaraʾa al-ṭālibu al-kitāba* |
| 15 | What do *kāna* and its sisters do to a nominal sentence? | The subject stays nominative and the predicate becomes accusative: *kāna al-jawwu bāridan* (the weather was cold) |
| 16 | And *inna* and its sisters? | The opposite: the subject becomes accusative and the predicate stays nominative: *inna al-ʿilma nūrun* (knowledge is light) |
| 17 | How is the sound masculine plural marked in the nominative? | With wāw (*al-muʿallimūna*), and with yāʾ in the accusative and genitive (*al-muʿallimīna*) |
| 18 | How is the dual marked in the nominative? | With alif (*al-ṭālibāni*), and with yāʾ in the other cases |
| 19 | What are the "five nouns"? | *Ab, akh, ḥam, fū, dhū*. They take wāw, alif and yāʾ for the three cases: *abūka, abāka, abīka* |
| 20 | Which famous poem sets out Arabic grammar in verse? | Ibn Malik's *Alfiyya*, so called because it has about a thousand lines |

## Spelling and letters

| # | Question | Answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 21 | How many "sun letters" and "moon letters" are there? | 14 of each |
| 22 | What is the difference between them? | After a sun letter, the *l* of *al-* is written but not pronounced (*ash-shams*); before a moon letter it is pronounced (*al-qamar*) |
| 23 | Which are the weak letters (ḥurūf al-ʿilla)? | Alif, wāw and yāʾ |
| 24 | How can you tell tāʾ marbūṭa from an open tāʾ? | Stop on the word. If it sounds like *h*, it's tāʾ marbūṭa (*madrasa*); if it stays *t*, it's open (*bayt*) |
| 25 | What is the difference between hamzat al-qaṭʿ and hamzat al-waṣl? | **Hamzat al-qaṭʿ** is always pronounced and written with a hamza (*akala*). Hamzat al-waṣl is heard at the start of speech but dropped mid-sentence (*istikhrāj*, *ibn*) |
| 26 | Who added dots to tell similar letters apart, like bāʾ, tāʾ and thāʾ? | Historical sources credit Nasr ibn ʿAsim and Yahya ibn Yaʿmar, in the Umayyad period |

## Questions teachers ask about Arabic quizzes

### How many questions should I use at assembly?

Five or six is enough, since a quiz slot at assembly rarely runs longer than three minutes. Take one from each round and open with an easy one so students are keen to join in.

### Why do some answers differ between books?

Some historical details, such as who first set down Arabic grammar, reach us through old accounts that historians don't fully agree on. It's better to say "tradition credits…" than to present them as certain, and to accept the answer given in the school textbook.

### How can I make the quiz fun for young children?

Stick to opposites, singulars and plurals, and once a child answers, ask them to use the word in a sentence. That turns the quiz into real language practice rather than a memory test.
