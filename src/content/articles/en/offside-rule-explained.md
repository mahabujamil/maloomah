A disallowed goal starts arguments in cafés and stands while the replay runs again and again. Yet the **offside rule** in football is simpler than all that noise suggests: **a player is offside when they are in an offside position at the moment a teammate plays the ball, and then become involved in play**. So the rule has two stages, and once you separate them, most arguments settle themselves.

The rule lives in Law 11 of the Laws of the Game, which are published by the International Football Association Board (IFAB).

## Stage one: when is a player in an "offside position"?

Two conditions have to be met together. First, the player must be in the opponents' half; the halfway line itself doesn't count as part of it. Second, some part of their head, body or feet must be nearer to the opponents' goal line than both the ball and the second-last opponent.

Hands and arms are ignored, and that goes for every player, goalkeepers included.

The phrase "second-last opponent" confuses a lot of people. Why not just say "last defender"? Because the last opponent is normally the goalkeeper, which makes the second-last one the last defender. If the goalkeeper wanders upfield, though, the count changes. What matters is where players are standing, not what position they play.

One more detail ends plenty of playground disputes: an attacker who is level with the second-last defender is not offside.

> **Note:** being in an offside position is not an offence by itself. A player is only penalised if they become involved in play, which is what stage two is about.

## Stage two: when is an offside player penalised?

Offside is called when a player was in an offside position at the moment a teammate played or touched the ball, and then got involved in one of three ways:

1. Interfering with play: playing or touching the ball.
2. Interfering with an opponent: blocking the goalkeeper's line of vision, for instance, or challenging a defender for the ball.
3. Gaining an advantage: playing a ball that rebounds to them off the post, the crossbar, a match official or an opponent, or after a save by the goalkeeper.

The third point stops a striker loitering offside and waiting for a rebound.

### The key moment: when the ball is passed, not when it's received

Offside is judged at the instant the teammate passes the ball, not when it arrives. This is what makes those runs in behind the defence possible. The attacker stays in a legal position as the pass is played, then sprints past the defenders once the ball has left their teammate's foot, and collects it completely legally even if they are now behind everyone.

## Situations where there's never offside

Whatever a player's position, there is no offside if they:

- receive the ball directly from a goal kick
- receive it directly from a throw-in
- receive it directly from a corner kick
- are in their own half

## What is the punishment?

The opposing team gets an indirect free kick from the spot where the offence happened. "Indirect" means another player has to touch the ball before it can go into the goal.

## Offside rule examples

Four situations you see every weekend:

- Example 1: A midfielder passes, and the striker was behind the last defender at the moment of the pass. The striker receives the ball and scores. Verdict: offside.
- Example 2: The winger was level with the last defender at the moment of the pass, then outran him and scored. Verdict: not offside.
- Example 3: A player in an offside position, far from the ball, doesn't affect anyone, and a teammate scores with a long-range shot. Verdict: the goal stands.
- Example 4: A striker behind the defenders receives the ball directly from a throw-in. Verdict: not offside.

Example 3 is stage two in action. The player was in an offside position but never got involved in any of the three ways, so there's nothing to punish.

## How has the offside rule changed over time?

Some version of the rule has been around since the game's first written laws in 1863. The old form was stricter: three opponents had to be between the attacker and the goal line. In 1925 that was cut to two, and the number of goals rose sharply afterwards.

In 1990 the rule shifted again in the attackers' favour, when a player level with a defender became onside.

## Technology and offside

When the margin can be a few centimetres, the human eye needs help. The Video Assistant Referee (VAR) was first used at a World Cup in Russia in 2018. At the 2022 World Cup in Qatar, semi-automated offside technology appeared. It relies on cameras that track players' limbs and a sensor inside the ball that pinpoints the moment it is kicked, which is exactly the moment offside is judged.

## Questions fans ask about offside

### Why does the offside rule exist?

It stops attackers camping next to the opponents' goal all match waiting for long balls, so the game stays collective and tactical.

### Can a goalkeeper be offside?

In theory, yes. The rule applies to any player in an offside position. In practice it's extremely rare. Next time an argument breaks out over a disallowed goal, two questions usually settle it: where was the player when the ball was passed, and did they get involved afterwards? And remember that goal kicks, throw-ins and corners can never produce an offside.
