No rule in football sparks more arguments in cafés and stands than **offside**. A goal is ruled out, a player protests, and a replay is shown dozens of times. But the rule is actually simpler than it looks once you understand it in two stages.

Offside is covered by **Law 11** of the Laws of the Game, published by the International Football Association Board (IFAB).

## Stage one: when is a player in an "offside position"?

A player is in an offside position if both of these are true:

1. They are **in the opponents' half** (the halfway line doesn't count).
2. Any part of their **head, body or feet** is nearer to the opponents' goal line than both **the ball** and **the second-last opponent**.

Important notes:

- **Hands and arms don't count**, for all players, including goalkeepers.
- **The second-last opponent** is usually the last defender, because the goalkeeper is normally the last player. But if the goalkeeper comes forward, what matters is where players are, not their positions.
- If the attacker is **level** with the second-last defender, they are **not offside**.

> **Note:** being in an offside position is **not an offence by itself**. A player is only penalised if they become involved in play.

## Stage two: when is an offside player penalised?

Offside is called if a player was in an offside position **at the moment a teammate played or touched the ball**, and then became involved in play by:

1. **Interfering with play:** playing or touching the ball.
2. **Interfering with an opponent:** for example, blocking the goalkeeper's line of vision, or challenging a defender for the ball.
3. **Gaining an advantage:** for example, playing a ball that rebounds to them off the post, the crossbar, a match official or an opponent, or after a save by the goalkeeper.

### The key moment: when the ball is passed, not when it's received

Offside is judged **at the moment the teammate passes the ball**, not when it's received. That's why an attacker can be in a legal position at the pass, then sprint past the defenders and receive the ball completely legally.

## Situations where there's never offside

- Receiving the ball directly from a **goal kick**.
- Receiving it directly from a **throw-in**.
- Receiving it directly from a **corner kick**.
- Being in **your own half**.

## What is the punishment?

The opposing team is awarded an **indirect free kick** from where the offence happened.

## Examples

- **Example 1:** A midfielder passes, and the striker was behind the last defender at the moment of the pass. The striker receives the ball and scores. → **Offside.**
- **Example 2:** The winger was level with the last defender at the moment of the pass, then outran him and scored. → **Not offside.**
- **Example 3:** A player in an offside position far from the ball doesn't affect anyone, and a teammate scores with a long-range shot. → **Goal stands.**
- **Example 4:** A striker behind the defenders receives the ball directly from a throw-in. → **Not offside.**

## How has the offside rule changed?

- Versions of the rule have existed since the game's first written laws in 1863.
- In **1925**, the rule changed so that only **two** opponents (instead of three) needed to be between the attacker and the goal line, and the number of goals rose sharply.
- In **1990**, an attacker **level** with a defender became onside, to favour attacking play.

## Technology and offside

The **Video Assistant Referee (VAR)** was used at a World Cup for the first time in Russia in 2018. At the 2022 World Cup in Qatar, **semi-automated offside technology** appeared, using cameras that track players' limbs and a sensor inside the ball that pinpoints the moment it is kicked.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does the offside rule exist?

To stop attackers from waiting next to the opponents' goal for the whole match, keeping the game collective and tactical.

### Can a goalkeeper be offside?

In theory yes, as the rule applies to any player in an offside position, but in practice it's extremely rare.

## Key takeaway

Offside has two stages: first, **offside position** (being nearer the goal than both the ball and the second-last defender, in the opponents' half), and second, **becoming involved in play** at the moment a teammate passes. And there's no offside from a goal kick, throw-in or corner.
