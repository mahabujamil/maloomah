The app that picks your next video and the search engine that sorts your results both run on algorithms. That word is, quite literally, a person's name. Who was al-Khwarizmi? He was **a Baghdad scholar whose book gave algebra its name**, who helped spread the Hindu-Arabic decimal numerals, and whose own name, passed through Latin, turned into a basic term of computer science.

## Who was al-Khwarizmi? A short biography

His full name was Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi. Like many scholars of his time, he didn't stick to one field: he was a mathematician, an astronomer and a geographer. He was born around 780 CE and died around 850 CE. Those dates are only approximate, because early writers preserved his books far better than the details of his life.

## Where was al-Khwarizmi from?

His name gives it away. "Al-Khwarizmi" means "from **Khwarazm**", a historical region of Central Asia around the delta of the Amu Darya river, roughly where Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan are now. Historians think he or his family came from there. His career, though, belonged to Baghdad, then the capital of the Abbasid state, and he wrote his works in Arabic.

## Al-Khwarizmi at the House of Wisdom

He worked at the House of Wisdom under the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun, who ruled roughly 813–833. Picture a place where Greek, Indian and Persian books were being translated into Arabic, and where scholars met to study and write. Al-Khwarizmi had the knowledge of several older civilisations within reach at once.

Sources say he dedicated some of his works to al-Ma'mun, including the algebra book. What set him apart was not just that he gathered this knowledge but that he organised it and added a systematic method of his own.

## Al-Khwarizmi's algebra: why is he called the father of algebra?

His most famous work is *al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala*, usually translated as *The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing*, written in the first decades of the 9th century. Before it, equation problems tended to be solved one at a time, each with its own trick. Al-Khwarizmi laid out systematic rules for solving linear and quadratic equations and sorted them into six standard types, so a reader could match a problem to its type and apply the right method.

You won't find an x or a y anywhere in it. Algebraic symbols hadn't been invented yet, so he explained everything in words and backed it up with geometric proofs. Nor was the book meant only for scholars: much of it deals with everyday problems such as trade, measuring land and working out inheritance shares.

The title describes the method. Al-jabr means "completion" or "restoring", moving a subtracted term to the other side of an equation. Al-muqabala means "balancing", cancelling equal terms on both sides. Those are exactly the two moves you make in a maths lesson when you tidy up an equation, and the English word **algebra** comes straight from al-jabr.

> **Did you know?** Al-Khwarizmi's algebra was translated into Latin by Robert of Chester in 1145, and again soon after by Gerard of Cremona. It remained an important mathematics textbook in Europe for centuries.

## From al-Khwarizmi to "algorithm"

His other big book was about arithmetic with Indian numerals. It explained place value, the use of zero, and how to add, subtract, multiply and divide. The Arabic original is lost, but a Latin translation survives, and it opens with the words "Dixit Algorizmi", meaning "al-Khwarizmi said".

That Latinised name, Algoritmi or Algorismus, gave Europe the word "algorism" for calculating with decimal numerals. Over time it became **algorithm**: a precise, ordered set of steps for solving a problem. Long division as you learned it at school is an algorithm, and so is a recipe followed step by step. The same idea is the backbone of all programming.

The book also helped carry the digits we still write to Europe; the full story is in our article on [where Arabic numerals come from](/en/articles/origin-of-arabic-numerals/).

## Al-Khwarizmi's contributions to astronomy and geography

In astronomy he compiled the Zij al-Sindhind, a set of tables for calculating the positions of the sun, moon and planets, drawing on Indian and other sources. Again the Arabic original is lost. What survives is a Latin translation of a revised version, made by the English scholar Adelard of Bath in the early 12th century.

In geography he wrote *Kitab Surat al-Ard* (*The Image of the Earth*), which lists the longitude and latitude of about 2,400 places: cities, mountains, islands and rivers. He built on the Greek geographer Ptolemy and corrected some of his errors. His maps didn't survive, but modern scholars have redrawn some of them from his descriptions.

## Al-Khwarizmi's legacy

Open any maths textbook and he is there, since both "algebra" and "algorithm" exist in languages around the world because of him. His arithmetic book helped spread the decimal system we write numbers in. Universities, schools and science prizes are named after him, and so is a crater on the Moon.

He belongs to a line of great scholars of the Arab and Islamic world, alongside [Ibn Sina](/en/articles/who-is-ibn-sina/) in medicine and [Ibn al-Haytham](/en/articles/ibn-al-haytham/) in optics.

## Questions students ask about al-Khwarizmi

### What was al-Khwarizmi famous for?

Founding algebra as a subject with his book on al-jabr and al-muqabala, explaining arithmetic with Hindu-Arabic numerals and zero, and lending his name to the word algorithm.

### What books did al-Khwarizmi write?

His main works are the algebra book, a book on Indian arithmetic known through its Latin translation, the astronomical tables Zij al-Sindhind, and the geography *Kitab Surat al-Ard*.

### Why is an algorithm named after al-Khwarizmi?

Because his Latinised name, Algoritmi, became the word for calculating with decimal numerals, and over centuries "algorithm" came to mean any step-by-step procedure. Every program on your phone carries, in a sense, the name of a scholar from the House of Wisdom.
