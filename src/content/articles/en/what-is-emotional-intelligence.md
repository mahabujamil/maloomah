Most classrooms have one: the student who tops every test but storms off when their team loses at break, and can't bring themselves to apologise afterwards. Sitting a few desks away is someone with ordinary grades who calms down fast, gets on with everyone and seems to know when to speak and when to stay quiet. What sets the second student apart isn't skill at maths or memorising. Psychologists call it **emotional intelligence**. So what is it, and is it something you're born with or something you can learn?

## What is emotional intelligence?

Emotional intelligence (EI or EQ) is **the ability to understand, manage and use emotions well**, both your own and other people's, in how you think, act and get along with others.

The idea was first set out scientifically by psychologists Peter Salovey and John Mayer in a 1990 paper. It only reached a wide audience five years later, when Daniel Goleman published his bestselling book "Emotional Intelligence" in 1995.

Salovey and Mayer describe four abilities, each building on the one before. The first is perceiving emotions: picking up on feelings in yourself and in other people's faces and voices. Next comes using them. Emotions aren't always in the way; a little nervousness before an exam can push you to revise properly. Then there's understanding them, which means knowing what triggered a feeling and how it might develop, the way irritation left alone can turn into anger. Last is managing them, keeping your own reactions in check and handling other people's feelings with some wisdom.

## Goleman's five components of emotional intelligence

Goleman split emotional intelligence into five parts. Here they are with everyday examples:

| Component | What it means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-awareness | Knowing what you feel and why | "I'm tense because the exam is tomorrow" |
| Self-regulation | Controlling impulses instead of reacting rashly | Taking a deep breath instead of shouting |
| Motivation | Driving yourself towards goals despite setbacks | Keeping on learning after a failure |
| Empathy | Understanding others' feelings and seeing their side | Noticing a friend is sad and asking how they are |
| Social skills | Communicating well, resolving conflict, building relationships | Calming a heated argument between colleagues |

The first two are about you and yourself, the last two are about you and other people, and motivation sits in the middle. That order isn't an accident: it's hard to read someone else's feelings when you can't yet read your own.

## Emotional intelligence vs IQ

IQ covers abilities like logical reasoning, maths, language and memory, which is mostly what school tests. EQ is about emotions, relationships and self-control. You won't see it on an exam paper, but you'll see it in the playground, at the dinner table and, later on, at work.

Neither replaces the other. Many studies link emotional intelligence with better relationships, mental wellbeing and performance in some jobs, particularly leadership and roles built around dealing with people. Be wary of the inflated claims that float around online, though. Lines like EQ being "responsible for 80% of success" **have no solid scientific basis**.

> **Did you know?** Most online "EQ tests" are just for fun. Researchers use validated tools such as the Mayer–Salovey–Caruso Emotional Intelligence Test (MSCEIT), which measures emotional abilities with real tasks instead of only asking how you rate yourself.

## How to improve your emotional intelligence

Here's the encouraging part: emotional intelligence is **a skill you can develop**. You get better at it the same way you get better at algebra, by practising. A few places to start:

1. Name your feelings precisely. "I'm upset" is vague. Is it anger, disappointment or worry? A feeling you can name is easier to deal with.
2. Pause before you respond. Give yourself a few seconds or a deep breath when things get heated. Most of what we regret saying comes out in the first moment.
3. Keep a journal. Jot down what you felt today and why. After a few weeks you'll start to see patterns in what sets you off and what calms you down.
4. Listen to understand. Pay attention to what the other person is saying instead of rehearsing your reply while they talk.
5. Ask for feedback. People you trust can tell you how you come across in tense moments, which is hard to judge from the inside.
6. Build small habits. Change takes time and repetition, not a single good week. Read: [How long does it take to form a habit?](/en/articles/how-long-does-it-take-to-form-a-habit/)

## Emotional intelligence in parenting

Children pick up how to handle feelings from their parents first, mostly by watching. Psychologist John Gottman recommends an approach he calls "emotion coaching". The parent notices the child's emotions and treats an emotional moment as a chance to connect rather than something to shut down quickly. They help the child put a name to what they feel, and at the same time set clear limits on behaviour while working out a solution together.

Picture a child in tears because a brother grabbed their toy. "Stop crying" ends the crying. "You're angry because he took it without asking, and hitting isn't OK, so what could we do?" teaches something that lasts. Children who are allowed to express their feelings and learn to manage them cope better with life, and the same idea works with older children too: [How to deal with a stubborn teenager](/en/articles/dealing-with-stubborn-teenager/).

## Questions people ask about emotional intelligence

### What's the difference between emotional and social intelligence?

Emotional intelligence is the broader of the two, because it includes understanding and controlling your own inner feelings. Social intelligence focuses on reading social situations and dealing successfully with others. They overlap a great deal; people who understand themselves tend to understand others more easily.

### Is emotional intelligence innate or learned?

Some of it comes from temperament and personality, but upbringing and experience shape it strongly, and it can be developed at any age.

### Can emotional intelligence be measured?

Yes, with validated scientific tools such as the MSCEIT mentioned above. Quick online quizzes usually aren't accurate, so don't read too much into your score on one.
