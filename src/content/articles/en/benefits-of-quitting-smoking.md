A lot of long-time smokers reason that the damage is already done, so stopping now would change very little. The body says otherwise. **The benefits of quitting smoking begin within minutes of your last cigarette** and keep adding up for years, whatever your age and however long you have smoked. Below is what happens inside you over that time, what quitting does for your skin, mood and wallet, and how to get through the withdrawal weeks.

## Benefits of quitting smoking: the timeline

The World Health Organization and the UK's NHS both publish timelines of what happens after you stop, and they broadly agree. This table combines them, with the source for each line:

| Time since your last cigarette | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 20 minutes | Heart rate and blood pressure drop (WHO) |
| 8 hours | Carbon monoxide and oxygen levels in the blood start returning to normal (NHS) |
| 12 hours | Carbon monoxide in the blood drops to normal (WHO) |
| 48 hours | Taste and smell improve (NHS) |
| 72 hours | Breathing feels easier and energy may rise (NHS) |
| 2 to 12 weeks | Circulation and lung function improve (WHO) |
| 1 to 9 months | Coughing and shortness of breath decrease (WHO) |
| 1 year | Risk of coronary heart disease is about half a smoker's (WHO) |
| 5 to 15 years | Stroke risk falls toward that of a non-smoker (WHO) |
| 10 years | Risk of dying from lung cancer is about half a smoker's (WHO) |
| 15 years | Coronary heart disease risk is close to a non-smoker's (WHO) |

People often ask when the lungs start to heal. The table answers it: lung function improves within 2 to 12 weeks, and the cough and breathlessness ease over 1 to 9 months. Treat all of these figures as a general guide, since the details vary from person to person.

## After 1 week and after 1 month

By the end of the first week, the extra carbon monoxide has cleared from your blood. Food tends to taste better, smells are sharper, and a flight of stairs feels a little easier. The same week is usually the hardest one mentally, though, because withdrawal peaks in the first few days before it starts to ease.

A month in, most people are past the worst of withdrawal. Circulation and lung function are picking up, so moving around takes less effort. That makes it a good time to add some light activity to your day. The [benefits of walking](/en/articles/benefits-of-walking/) every day are a natural fit, and a walk can fill the gap an old smoking break used to occupy.

## Benefits of quitting smoking for your body

The heart and blood vessels are where the change is clearest. Smoking narrows blood vessels and raises the risk of clots, so quitting gradually eases the load on your [heart](/en/articles/human-heart-facts/). Your lungs gain too, with less coughing and phlegm and better lung capacity over time.

The effect on lifespan has been measured. A large US study published in 2013 in the New England Journal of Medicine found that smokers lose about 10 years of life expectancy, and that quitting before 40 removes about 90% of the extra risk of death linked to continued smoking. Put simply, the earlier you stop, the more of that lost time you win back.

Men have one more reason. The NHS notes that smoking harms blood flow and can reduce fertility, and that quitting improves circulation throughout the body.

## Benefits of quitting smoking on skin

Smoking cuts the supply of blood and oxygen to your skin, and its chemicals damage **collagen and elastin**, the proteins that keep skin firm and springy. That is why smokers tend to develop wrinkles early and a dull complexion.

After you quit, blood flow to the skin improves, and many people notice a healthier colour within weeks. The smell of smoke also leaves your hair, fingers and clothes. It helps to keep expectations realistic: wrinkles that have already formed won't all disappear. The main gain is slowing down premature ageing from here on.

## Mental health benefits

Plenty of smokers light up after an argument, before an exam or in heavy traffic because a cigarette seems to calm them. The NHS explains that this feeling is mostly short-lived relief from nicotine withdrawal itself. In other words, each cigarette soothes tension that the previous one created.

That helps explain why research suggests that, after a few weeks, people who quit feel less anxiety, stress and depression than those who keep smoking. On top of that come a sense of achievement and the freedom of no longer planning your day around the next cigarette.

> **Did you know?** The WHO says tobacco kills more than 8 million people every year, including more than a million non-smokers exposed to second-hand smoke. Quitting protects your family too.

## Withdrawal symptoms and practical tips

According to the NHS, withdrawal can start within a few hours of stopping. The **first 3 days** are usually the hardest, and symptoms last **3 to 4 weeks** on average. The common ones are strong cravings, irritability, poor concentration, disturbed sleep and a bigger appetite.

That bigger appetite is why so many people worry about weight. Some do gain a little, which activity and balanced meals help keep in check, and the health gains far outweigh a few extra kilograms.

These steps make the first weeks more manageable:

1. Set a quit date and get rid of cigarettes and lighters beforehand.
2. Know your triggers, such as coffee, finishing a meal or stress, and plan a replacement for each.
3. Ride out cravings. Each one usually passes within minutes, so drink water, take a short walk or breathe slowly until it does.
4. Track the money you save and put it toward something you want. It adds up quickly.
5. Get support. Many countries have free quitlines and stop-smoking services, and a doctor or pharmacist can talk you through the help that suits you.
6. Give the new routine time to stick; see [how long it takes to form a habit](/en/articles/how-long-does-it-take-to-form-a-habit/).

## Quitting suddenly or gradually?

Both can work. A Cochrane review found similar success rates for stopping abruptly and for cutting down gradually toward a set quit date. What matters is picking the approach you can stick with. If you choose to cut down, set a firm date to stop completely, because smoking less is not the same as stopping.

Some people fear that quitting cold turkey is dangerous. For most people it is safe, and the first days are simply uncomfortable. If you have a long-term condition, check with your doctor first. Whichever route you take, the opening weeks are the hard part, and with a clear plan and support around you, quitting is possible at any age.
