**Black holes** are among the strangest objects in the universe: regions of space where gravity is so strong that **not even light can escape**. So what is a black hole, how does one form, and can we ever see it? Here's a clear, fact-checked explainer.

## What is a black hole?

Despite the name, a black hole isn't an empty "hole". It's an **enormous amount of mass squeezed into a tiny space**. The more tightly mass is packed, the stronger its gravity becomes close to it.

Picture it this way: to escape Earth's gravity, a rocket needs a speed of about **11 kilometres per second**. For a black hole, the escape speed is greater than the **speed of light**, the fastest speed in the universe. So nothing gets out, not even light, and it looks black.

Einstein's **general relativity** allowed for such objects, and in 1916 the German physicist **Karl Schwarzschild** found a solution to its equations that describes one. The name "black hole" caught on after American physicist **John Wheeler** used it in 1967.

## How are black holes formed?

The best-known way a black hole forms is the **death of a very massive star**:

1. For millions of years the star burns nuclear fuel, and the heat it produces balances its gravity.
2. When the fuel runs out, gravity wins and the core collapses.
3. The outer layers are blasted away in a huge explosion called a **supernova**.
4. If the remaining core is massive enough, the collapse continues until a **black hole** forms.

Our Sun will never become a black hole; it isn't nearly massive enough. Billions of years from now it will end its life as a **white dwarf**.

## Inside a black hole: the event horizon and the singularity

A black hole has two main parts:

- **The event horizon:** the outer boundary and the "point of no return". Anything that crosses it, even light, can't come back.
- **The singularity:** the centre, where current theory predicts the mass is crushed to infinite density. Most physicists take this as a sign that our present theories break down there.

No one knows for certain what lies inside the event horizon, because no information from inside can reach us. But calculations show that an object falling towards a small black hole would be **stretched out like a noodle** by the huge difference in gravity between its head and feet. Scientists call this, half-jokingly, **spaghettification**.

## Types of black holes

| Type | Approximate mass | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stellar black holes | A few to dozens of solar masses | Cygnus X-1 in our galaxy |
| Supermassive black holes | Millions to billions of solar masses | Sagittarius A* at the centre of the Milky Way |
| Intermediate-mass black holes | In between; rarely detected | Astronomers are still looking for confirmed cases |

Scientists believe most large galaxies have a **supermassive black hole** at their centre. Ours is **Sagittarius A***, with about **4 million times the mass of the Sun**, roughly 26,000 light-years away, far too distant to pose any danger.

## The first real image of a black hole

If a black hole gives off no light, how did we photograph one? The gas swirling around it moves so fast that it heats up and glows. On **10 April 2019**, the **Event Horizon Telescope** (EHT) team, a network of radio telescopes around the world working like one Earth-sized telescope, released the **first real image of a black hole**: the one at the centre of galaxy **M87**, about 55 million light-years away.

The image shows a glowing orange ring around a dark shadow. In **2022** the same team published the first image of our own galaxy's black hole, Sagittarius A*.

> **Did you know?** In 2015 the LIGO observatory in the United States detected **gravitational waves** for the first time, produced by two black holes merging more than a billion light-years away. It was powerful new evidence that black holes exist, and the scientists who led the project won the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics.

## Frequently asked questions

### Could a black hole swallow the Earth?

No known black hole is close enough to threaten Earth. A black hole doesn't "suck up" the universe; it only pulls in things that come very close, like any other object with gravity.

### What is the biggest known black hole?

One of the most massive known is **TON 618**, estimated at tens of billions of solar masses, although estimates vary between studies.

### Do black holes die?

In 1974 **Stephen Hawking** predicted that black holes slowly lose energy through **Hawking radiation** and eventually evaporate. But this would take far longer than the current age of the universe, and the radiation has not yet been observed.

### Who won a Nobel Prize for black holes?

In 2020 **Roger Penrose** won the Nobel Prize in Physics for showing that black holes are a robust prediction of general relativity. He shared it with **Reinhard Genzel** and **Andrea Ghez** for discovering a supermassive compact object at the centre of our galaxy.

## Key takeaway

A black hole is a huge mass packed into a tiny space, with gravity so strong that not even light escapes. Most form when a massive star collapses, and their boundary is the event horizon. We can't see one directly, but in 2019 scientists photographed the glowing gas around one for the first time, and since 2015 they have detected the gravitational waves from black holes colliding.
