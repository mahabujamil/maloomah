Throw a ball straight up as hard as you can and it always comes back down. Earth's gravity pulls it home. Now picture a place where gravity is so overwhelming that even light, the fastest thing there is, can't get away. That's a black hole: **a region of space where gravity is so strong that not even light can escape**. It sounds like science fiction, but astronomers have weighed them, heard them collide and even photographed one. Here's how they work.

## What is a black hole?

The name is a little misleading. A black hole isn't an empty pit in space. It's an enormous amount of mass squeezed into a very small volume, and the tighter you pack mass, the stronger its gravity becomes close to it.

Rockets make this easier to grasp. To leave Earth for good, a rocket has to reach about 11 kilometres per second. Below that, it falls back. Scientists call this threshold the **escape velocity**. Around a black hole, the escape velocity is higher than the speed of light. Since nothing travels faster than light, nothing can climb out, and the object looks completely black.

Black holes were predicted on paper long before anyone found one. Einstein's general relativity allowed for them, and in 1916 the German physicist Karl Schwarzschild worked out a solution to its equations that describes exactly this situation. The catchy name came later: it spread after the American physicist John Wheeler used it in 1967.

## How are black holes formed?

The most familiar route to a black hole begins with the death of a very massive star:

1. For millions of years the star burns nuclear fuel. The heat pushes outward and holds the star up against its own gravity.
2. When the fuel runs out, that support disappears. Gravity wins and the core collapses.
3. The outer layers are blown off in a colossal explosion called a supernova.
4. If the leftover core is heavy enough, nothing can stop the collapse, and it keeps going until a black hole forms.

Students often ask whether the Sun will end up this way. It won't. It simply doesn't have enough mass. In billions of years it will settle down as a much quieter object, a white dwarf.

## Inside a black hole: the event horizon and the singularity

Physicists describe two key parts. The outer boundary is the **event horizon**, often called the point of no return. Think of the edge of a waterfall where the current is too strong to swim back against: once anything crosses it, light included, it can't come out again.

At the centre is the **singularity**, where our current theory says the mass is crushed to infinite density. Infinite density isn't something nature is likely to produce, so most physicists read this as a sign that today's theories stop working there, rather than a literal description of what's inside.

What actually lies behind the event horizon? Nobody knows for sure, because no information from inside can reach us. The approach, though, is easier to calculate, and the result is odd. Fall feet-first towards a small black hole and the pull on your feet would be far stronger than the pull on your head. You'd be stretched out like a noodle, which scientists, only half-jokingly, call spaghettification.

## Types of black holes

Black holes come in very different sizes, and astronomers sort them by mass:

| Type | Approximate mass | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stellar black holes | A few to dozens of solar masses | Cygnus X-1 in our galaxy |
| Supermassive black holes | Millions to billions of solar masses | Sagittarius A* at the centre of the Milky Way |
| Intermediate-mass black holes | In between; rarely detected | Astronomers are still looking for confirmed cases |

Most large galaxies seem to have a supermassive black hole at their heart, and the Milky Way is no exception. Ours, Sagittarius A*, holds about 4 million times the mass of the Sun. That sounds alarming until you remember it's roughly 26,000 light-years away, far too distant to pose any danger to us.

## The first real image of a black hole

If a black hole gives off no light, how can anyone take its picture? The trick is to photograph its surroundings. Gas spiralling around a black hole moves so fast that it heats up and glows, outlining the dark region in the middle.

On 10 April 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) team released the first real image of a black hole. The EHT isn't a single instrument but a network of radio telescopes around the world, linked so they behave like one telescope the size of Earth. Their target sat at the centre of the galaxy M87, about 55 million light-years away, and the picture showed a glowing orange ring around a dark shadow. In 2022 the same team published the first image of Sagittarius A*, the black hole in our own galaxy.

> **Did you know?** In 2015 the LIGO observatory in the United States detected **gravitational waves** for the first time, produced by two black holes merging more than a billion light-years away. It was powerful new evidence that black holes exist, and the scientists who led the project won the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics.

## Questions students ask about black holes

### Could a black hole swallow the Earth?

No known black hole is anywhere near close enough to threaten Earth. And despite what films suggest, a black hole doesn't vacuum up everything around it. It only pulls in things that come very close, just like any other object with gravity. If the Sun were somehow replaced by a black hole of the same mass, Earth would keep orbiting as before, though it would be dark and freezing.

### What is the biggest known black hole?

One of the most massive known is TON 618, estimated at tens of billions of solar masses, although estimates vary between studies.

### Do black holes die?

In 1974 Stephen Hawking predicted that black holes slowly lose energy through what's now called Hawking radiation, and eventually evaporate. It would take far longer than the current age of the universe, though, and the radiation has not yet been observed.

### Who won a Nobel Prize for black holes?

In 2020 Roger Penrose won the Nobel Prize in Physics for showing that black holes are a robust prediction of general relativity. He shared it with Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez, who discovered a supermassive compact object at the centre of our galaxy. Not bad for an idea that started as a solution to some equations in 1916.
