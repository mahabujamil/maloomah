Watch a classroom window on a wet morning. Drops land on the glass, slide, bump into each other, merge into bigger drops and finally run down. Something very similar is happening inside the clouds overhead. So how does rain form? In short, **water evaporates and rises with the air, cools and condenses into tiny droplets that make clouds, and those droplets grow until the air can no longer hold them up**. Every rainy season people start searching for "rain" and "weather tomorrow", so it's worth following one drop from the sea to the sky and back again.

## The water cycle: where does rainwater come from?

The rain that soaks you on the walk home wasn't made in the sky. It went up there first. Rain is part of the water cycle, a loop that never stops and has four stages:

1. Evaporation: the Sun warms seas, oceans and lakes, turning some of the water into invisible water vapour that rises with the air. Plants release vapour from their leaves too, a process called transpiration.
2. Condensation: air cools as it rises, and the vapour turns into tiny droplets that make up clouds.
3. Precipitation: the droplets grow until they're heavy enough to fall as rain, snow or hail.
4. Collection: water flows back to rivers and seas or soaks into the ground to refill groundwater, and the cycle starts again.

## How do clouds form?

Warm air can hold more water vapour than cold air. When moist air rises, it expands and cools, and at a certain temperature, called the **dew point**, it can't keep all of its vapour any more. Condensation begins. You've seen the same thing on a glass of cold water taken out of the fridge: the air touching the glass cools down and leaves its moisture behind as beads on the outside.

Up in the sky there's no glass, though, and vapour still needs something to condense on. That's where **condensation nuclei** come in. They're tiny particles of dust, sea salt and smoke floating in the air. Vapour gathers around them to form cloud droplets, each one far smaller than a pinhead.

## How does rain form in clouds?

Cloud droplets are so light that rising air keeps them aloft, which is why plenty of clouds drift past without a drop of rain. To become a raindrop heavy enough to fall, a droplet has to grow roughly a million times in volume. There are two main ways that happens, and which one wins depends on how cold the cloud is.

### 1. Collision and coalescence in warm clouds

Droplets move around inside the cloud, bump into each other and merge, much like the drops on that classroom window. A bigger drop falls faster than its neighbours, so it sweeps up more droplets on the way down. Eventually it's too heavy for the rising air to support, and it falls as rain. This is the usual route in warm tropical regions.

### 2. The ice-crystal process in cold clouds

High in many clouds the temperature is below freezing. Ice crystals sit there alongside "supercooled" water droplets that haven't frozen yet, and the crystals grow quickly at the droplets' expense before they start to fall. If they pass through warmer air on the way down, they melt and arrive as rain. If the air stays cold all the way to the ground, they arrive as snow. Scientists call this the **Bergeron process**, and it produces most of the rain in mid-latitude regions.

> **Did you know?** Raindrops aren't teardrop-shaped, despite how we draw them. Small drops are nearly round, while larger ones get flattened underneath by air resistance as they fall, looking more like a bean or a hamburger bun.

## Types of rainfall

Rain only forms when moist air rises and cools. What pushes the air up in the first place varies from place to place, and geographers use that to sort rainfall into three types:

| Type | What lifts the air | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Convectional | The Sun heats the ground and warm air shoots upwards | Summer showers and tropical thunderstorms |
| Orographic (relief) | Moist air hits mountains and is forced up | Rain on the windward slopes of coastal mountain ranges |
| Frontal (cyclonic) | Warm air meets cold air and rides up over it | Most winter rain in the Mediterranean and Western Europe |

When the air rises very violently, you get [thunderstorms](/en/articles/what-is-a-thunderstorm/), with lightning, thunder and sometimes hail.

## Rain vs snow vs hail

All three fall from clouds. The difference is the state the water is in when it reaches you. Rain is liquid drops. Snow is six-sided ice crystals that didn't melt on the way down. Hail is something else again: hard balls of ice that form inside thunderclouds, where strong updrafts carry drops up again and again. Each trip adds another layer of ice, a bit like the layers of an onion, until the ball is heavy enough to drop out.

Forecasters use satellites and radar to work out when and where rain will fall, as we explain in [how weather forecasts are made](/en/articles/how-are-weather-forecasts-made/).

## Questions students ask about rain

### How does rain form, for kids?

The Sun warms water so it evaporates and rises into the sky. Up there it cools and turns into tiny drops that make clouds. The drops join together and grow until they're heavy, and then they fall to the ground as rain. The water runs back to the sea, and the whole thing starts over.

### Why don't all clouds produce rain?

In many clouds the droplets stay too small to fall, or they evaporate in dry air before they reach the ground.

### What is acid rain?

Rain that is more acidic than normal because of polluting gases such as sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning fuel. It can damage trees, lakes and buildings.

### What is artificial rain (cloud seeding)?

An attempt to increase rainfall by spreading substances such as silver iodide or salts into clouds so they act as condensation nuclei. It can't make rain from a clear sky; suitable clouds have to be there already.
