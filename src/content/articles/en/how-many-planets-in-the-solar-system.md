Ask your parents how many planets are in the solar system and there's a good chance they'll say nine. That's what schools taught until 2006. Today's textbooks say **eight**. No planet disappeared, of course. What changed was the definition of a planet, and Pluto no longer fit it. Let's start with the list in order, then come back to Pluto.

## How many planets are in the solar system, in order?

These are the eight planets, from closest to the Sun to farthest:

1. Mercury
2. Venus
3. Earth
4. Mars
5. Jupiter
6. Saturn
7. Uranus
8. Neptune

This is the count used by the International Astronomical Union (IAU), the body that names and classifies objects in space.

An easy way to remember the order is to split it in half. First come four small, rocky planets near the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars. Then come four distant giants: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. Earth is third, and its neighbour Mars is fourth. If you like rhymes, the old English line "My Very Educated Mother Just Served Us Nachos" gives you the first letters in order.

## Why isn't Pluto a planet anymore?

Pluto was discovered in 1930 and spent decades as "the ninth planet" in school books. Then astronomers started spotting similar bodies in the outer solar system, some very close to Pluto in size, such as Eris. That raised an awkward question: if Pluto is a planet, why aren't these?

To settle it, the IAU agreed on a definition on 24 August 2006. An object counts as a planet if it orbits the Sun, has enough mass to pull itself into a nearly round shape, and has **cleared its orbit** of other similar objects.

Pluto orbits the Sun and is roughly round, but it shares its part of space with many other objects in the Kuiper Belt, so it fails the third test. That's why it is now a **dwarf planet**. The IAU recognises four other dwarf planets alongside it: Ceres, Haumea, Makemake and Eris.

So if a source gives you a number other than 8, it's probably old or unreliable.

## Inner and outer planets

The first four planets are called the inner or rocky planets. They are fairly small and have solid surfaces, and spacecraft have actually landed on both Mars and Venus.

Past Mars lies the asteroid belt, and beyond it the outer giants. Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants, Uranus and Neptune are ice giants, and none of them has a solid surface you could stand on.

> **Did you know?** A day on Venus is longer than its year. Venus takes about 243 Earth days to spin once on its axis, but only about 225 days to go around the Sun. On top of that, it spins the opposite way to most planets.

## Is there a Planet Nine?

Maybe. Some scientists have noticed that the orbits of several distant objects at the edge of the solar system are bunched together in an odd way, and they think a large, undiscovered planet could be pulling on them. They call it "Planet Nine". Nobody has seen it yet, though, so for now it's an idea waiting for evidence, not a ninth planet.

## Questions students ask about the planets

### Are there 7, 8 or 9 planets?

Eight. The count was 9 until 2006, when Pluto was reclassified as a dwarf planet, and no scientific definition gives seven.

### What is the largest planet in the solar system?

Jupiter. It's so big that more than 1,300 Earths could fit inside it.

### Which planet is closest to Earth?

The answer surprises a lot of people. Venus is the planet that comes closest to us at certain times. Averaged over time, though, Mercury is our nearest neighbour, because it always stays close to the Sun, while Venus swings far away when it's on the other side of the Sun.
