Your child suddenly screams, drops to the floor and cries nonstop because you said no to sweets. If that sounds familiar, you're not alone. **Tantrums** are a normal part of growing up for most children, especially in the early years. The good news is that how you respond makes a big difference.

## Are tantrums normal?

In most cases, yes. Tantrums are very common **between ages 1 and 4** and often peak **around ages 2 and 3**, the stage many parents call the "terrible twos". They fade as children's language and ability to express feelings develop, and they usually become much less frequent by age 5.

At this age a child wants independence and control, but doesn't yet have enough words or the ability to manage strong emotions. The frustration comes out as screaming and crying.

## What causes tantrums?

- **Hunger, tiredness or sleepiness:** among the most common triggers, and easy for parents to overlook.
- **Frustration:** when a child can't do something they want, or can't say how they feel.
- **A push for independence:** they want to choose for themselves and resist being told what to do.
- **Attention:** if they've learned that screaming gets results or makes everyone focus on them.
- **Sudden change:** play ending abruptly, a new place, or a new baby in the family.

## 10 tips for handling tantrums

1. **Stay calm.** Shouting fuels the tantrum, and your child learns self-control from you.
2. **Keep them safe.** Move them away from anything dangerous, and move dangerous things away from them.
3. **Name the feeling:** "I know you're angry because we're leaving the park." This gives them the words they're missing.
4. **Don't give in to the demand that started it.** If screaming gets them what they want, they learn that screaming works.
5. **Skip the lecture mid-tantrum.** An upset child can't take in explanations. Wait until they're calm.
6. **Try distraction** with younger children: a toy, a window, a different activity.
7. **Offer limited choices:** "Do you want the blue shirt or the red one?" instead of "Get dressed now."
8. **Praise good behaviour** when they use words to express anger or calm down on their own.
9. **Keep a steady routine** for sleep and meals. A rested, fed child is less likely to melt down.
10. **Give a warning before changes:** "In five minutes we're leaving," rather than leaving suddenly.

> **Did you know?** The **American Academy of Pediatrics** advises against spanking and harsh verbal punishment. It says these methods don't teach better behaviour in the long run and may increase aggression.

## After the tantrum

Once your child is calm, hug them and reassure them that you love them even when they're angry. Then talk briefly and simply about what happened, and teach a better way to express it, such as saying "I'm angry." There's no need to keep blaming them. The goal is learning, not punishment.

## When to talk to a doctor

Most tantrums are normal, but it's worth **speaking to your paediatrician** if:

- Frequent, intense tantrums continue past about age 5
- Your child hurts themselves or others during tantrums
- Tantrums last a very long time or happen several times a day
- They come with speech delays or a loss of skills your child already had

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do 2-year-olds have so many tantrums?

At this age children want independence but lack the words and the self-control to manage big feelings, so frustration comes out as screaming and crying.

### Should I ignore my child during a tantrum?

You can ignore the behaviour if it's aimed at getting attention, while staying close and keeping them safe. Don't ignore a child who is frightened or hurt.

### Are frequent tantrums inherited?

Some children are born with more intense temperaments, but environment, parenting style and daily routine strongly shape behaviour.

### When do tantrums stop?

They usually ease noticeably as speech improves, often by age 4 or 5.

## Key takeaway

**Tantrums** in the early years are usually normal. They come from a child's drive for independence and a lack of words. Stay calm, name the feeling, don't reward screaming, and keep sleep and meals regular. Talk to a doctor if tantrums are severe or continue past age 5.
