You're in the checkout queue, your child asks for sweets, and you say no. A few seconds later they're on the floor, screaming, and half the shop is watching. Nearly every parent has been there. **Toddler tantrums** are a normal part of growing up for most children, and how you deal with them has a lot to do with whether they fade or keep coming back.

## Are toddler tantrums normal?

In most cases, yes. Tantrums are very common between ages 1 and 4 and often peak **around ages 2 and 3**, the stage many parents call the "terrible twos". They ease off as children's language and ability to express feelings develop, and they usually become much less frequent by age 5.

It makes more sense from the child's side. A two-year-old wants independence and control, but doesn't yet have the words to say what they want or the ability to manage strong emotions. When frustration builds up with nowhere to go, it comes out as screaming and crying.

## Why do toddlers have tantrums?

The most common triggers are the simplest ones: hunger, tiredness and sleepiness. Parents overlook them all the time. A meltdown that looks like it's about a toy may really be about a missed nap.

Frustration is the next big one, when a child can't do something they're trying to do, like fitting two pieces of a toy together, or can't explain how they feel. Then there's the push for independence. Toddlers want to choose for themselves and will resist being told what to do, sometimes just because they were told.

Some tantrums are about attention. If a child has learned that screaming gets results, or gets everyone focused on them, they'll use it. And sudden change throws young children off: play ending without warning, a new place, or a new baby at home.

## How to deal with toddler tantrums: 10 tips

Some of these are for the tantrum itself and some are for preventing the next one:

1. **Stay calm.** Shouting fuels the tantrum, and your child learns self-control by watching you.
2. Keep them safe. Move them away from anything dangerous, and move dangerous things away from them.
3. Name the feeling: "I know you're angry because we're leaving the park." That gives them the words they're missing.
4. **Don't give in to the demand that started it.** If screaming gets them what they want, they learn that screaming works.
5. Skip the lecture mid-tantrum. An upset child can't take in explanations, so wait until they're calm.
6. Try distraction with younger children: a toy, a window, a different activity.
7. Offer limited choices: "Do you want the blue shirt or the red one?" instead of "Get dressed now."
8. Praise good behaviour when they use words to express anger or calm down on their own.
9. Keep a steady routine for sleep and meals. A rested, fed child is less likely to melt down.
10. Give a warning before changes: "In five minutes we're leaving," rather than leaving suddenly.

Tips 1 and 4 are the hardest to stick to, especially in public, and they matter most. Children read your reaction. If you lose your temper or give in, they learn that a tantrum is a tool that works.

> **Did you know?** The American Academy of Pediatrics advises against spanking and harsh verbal punishment. It says these methods don't teach better behaviour in the long run and may increase aggression.

## What to do once the tantrum is over

The calm afterwards is where the real teaching happens. Hug your child and let them know you love them even when they're angry. Then talk briefly and simply about what happened, and show them a better way to express it, such as saying "I'm angry." There's no need to go back over the blame. The point is for them to learn, not to be punished twice.

## When to talk to a doctor

Most tantrums are normal, but it's worth speaking to your paediatrician if:

- Frequent, intense tantrums continue past about age 5
- Your child hurts themselves or others during tantrums
- Tantrums last a very long time or happen several times a day
- They come with speech delays or a loss of skills your child already had

## Questions parents ask about tantrums

### Why do 2-year-olds have so many tantrums?

They want independence but lack the words and the self-control to handle big feelings, so frustration comes out as screaming and crying.

### Should I ignore my child during a tantrum?

You can ignore the behaviour if it's aimed at getting attention, as long as you stay close and keep them safe. Don't ignore a child who is frightened or hurt.

### Are frequent tantrums inherited?

Some children are born with more intense temperaments, but environment, parenting style and daily routine strongly shape behaviour.

### When do tantrums stop?

They usually ease noticeably as speech improves, often by age 4 or 5. If they're still severe after that, it's a good time to check in with a doctor.
