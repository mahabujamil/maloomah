The Sun is a **star**, and a fairly ordinary one. Our galaxy holds billions of stars like it; the Sun only looks so big and bright because it is close. Everything else in the night sky is a star too, just so far away that it shrinks to a point of light. Below are the facts about the Sun that students are most often asked about: its size, its temperature, what it's made of, how it produces light, and why life on Earth depends on it.

## A star, not a planet

Younger students sometimes group the Sun with the planets, since we see all of them in the same sky. The difference matters, though. A star makes its own light and heat. A planet makes none; it only reflects light from the star it orbits. The Moon shines at night for the same reason a mirror shines in a lit room: it is bouncing sunlight back at us.

The Sun sits at the centre of the solar system. Eight planets travel around it, along with dwarf planets, asteroids and comets, and its gravity is what keeps all of them on their paths. If you need the planets in order, see [How Many Planets Are in the Solar System?](/en/articles/how-many-planets-in-the-solar-system/)

## How big is the Sun?

The Sun is about 1.39 million kilometres across, roughly **109 times the width of Earth**. Picture 109 Earths lined up side by side and you have the Sun's diameter. By volume the gap is even bigger: more than a million Earths would fit inside it.

Its mass is about 333,000 times Earth's. On its own, the Sun holds around 99.8% of all the mass in the solar system, which means every planet, Jupiter included, is left sharing a tiny remainder.

Even so, astronomers call the Sun a medium-sized star. Some stars are hundreds of times wider; many are much smaller. It's a useful reminder that "big" and "small" only mean something once you say what you're comparing with.

## What is the Sun made of?

The Sun is not rock, and it isn't burning like a campfire. It's an enormous ball of extremely hot gas, made mostly of two elements. Hydrogen accounts for more than 70% of its mass and helium for about a quarter. Oxygen, carbon, iron and everything else together add up to only a small fraction.

At those temperatures the gas doesn't behave like the air around you. Electrons are stripped away from their atoms, turning the gas into **plasma**, often called the fourth state of matter. That's also why the Sun has no solid surface to stand on. It is gas and plasma all the way down.

## How hot is the Sun?

There is no single answer, because each layer has a very different temperature:

| Layer | Approximate temperature |
| --- | --- |
| Core | about 15 million °C |
| Visible surface (photosphere) | about 5,500 °C |
| Corona (outer atmosphere) | more than 1 million °C |

Look at the last row again. The corona is far hotter than the surface underneath it, which is the opposite of what you'd expect when moving away from a heat source. Scientists are still working out why, and there isn't a settled answer yet. Normally the corona is drowned out by the glare of the surface, but during a total solar eclipse it appears as a pale glowing halo around the darkened Sun.

## How does the Sun make light and heat?

Deep in the core, pressure and temperature are so extreme that hydrogen nuclei collide hard enough to fuse into helium. This is **nuclear fusion**. Each fusion reaction turns a sliver of mass into energy, and because millions of tonnes of matter are converted every second, the Sun pours out a staggering amount of light and heat.

That energy takes a very long time to work its way out from the core. Once it leaves the surface, though, it travels at the speed of light and reaches Earth in about 8 minutes and 20 seconds. The reason for that number is explained in [How Long Does Sunlight Take to Reach Earth?](/en/articles/how-long-does-sunlight-take-to-reach-earth/)

> **Did you know?** The Sun spins, but not as one solid piece. Near its equator a full turn takes about 25 days, while regions near the poles take about 35 days. A rocky planet couldn't do that; a ball of gas can.

## Why life depends on the Sun

Think about your breakfast. Bread comes from wheat, and wheat built its food from sunlight through photosynthesis. Milk came from a cow that ate grass, which also grew using sunlight. Nearly every food chain on land starts with a plant powered by the Sun.

The Sun also drives the water cycle. Its heat evaporates water from seas and rivers, and that vapour later condenses into clouds and rain. Uneven heating of the air creates wind. Day and night come from Earth turning towards and away from the Sun, and the seasons come from the tilt of Earth's axis as it orbits.

Sunlight can also do harm. Never look straight at the Sun, not even with ordinary sunglasses or through binoculars, because it can permanently damage your eyes. Astronomers use special solar filters made for exactly this purpose.

## What will happen to the Sun?

The Sun is about 4.6 billion years old, roughly halfway through its life. Scientists expect it to carry on much as it is now for about another 5 billion years, until the hydrogen in its core runs low.

After that, it will swell enormously and its outer layers will cool and redden, turning it into a **red giant**. Later it will shed those outer layers, leaving behind a small, dense core called a white dwarf that cools very slowly over an immensely long time. None of this is anything to worry about; it lies billions of years ahead.

## Questions students ask about the Sun

### Is the Sun really yellow?

Sunlight is a mix of all colours, and seen from space the Sun looks closer to white. Earth's atmosphere scatters blue light more than other colours, so from the ground the Sun looks yellowish and the sky looks blue.

### How far away is the Sun?

About 150 million kilometres on average. Astronomers call this distance one astronomical unit and use it to measure distances within the solar system.

### Could the Sun suddenly go out?

No. The Sun isn't burning the way a fire burns, which needs oxygen. It runs on nuclear fusion and has enough hydrogen for billions of years. Any change will be extremely slow, not sudden.
