Ask a class who invented electricity and you'll get a handful of confident answers: Edison, Franklin, maybe Tesla. None of them is quite right. The honest answer is that **nobody invented electricity**. It was here long before any scientist, crackling in lightning and carrying signals along the nerves in our bodies. What people did, over more than two thousand years, was notice it, figure out how it works and then put it to use. Here's how that happened, one name at a time.

## The beginning: a piece of amber in ancient Greece

Around 600 BCE, the Greek philosopher Thales of Miletus noticed something odd. When he rubbed a piece of amber, which is fossilised tree resin, with wool, it started pulling feathers and bits of straw towards it. He had no idea why, but it's the first recorded observation of what we now call **static electricity**. You've seen the same thing if you've ever rubbed a plastic ruler on your hair and used it to pick up scraps of paper.

> **Did you know?** The word "electricity" comes from the Greek "elektron", meaning amber. Interestingly, the Arabic word for electricity, **"kahraba"**, also comes from the Persian name for amber, "kahruba", meaning "straw-attractor"!

## William Gilbert: the man who named it

For centuries, Thales's amber trick stayed a curiosity. Then in 1600 the English physician William Gilbert published a book on magnetism. He tested many materials that behaved like amber and described them with the Latin word "electricus", which is where the word for electricity in European languages comes from.

## Benjamin Franklin and the kite

In the 18th century, plenty of people assumed lightning was something quite different from the tiny sparks you could get from a charged object. In 1752 the American Benjamin Franklin set out to show otherwise, flying a kite in a thunderstorm to demonstrate that lightning is electricity. That work led him to the **lightning rod**, which still protects buildings today.

There's a twist that often gets left out. Franklin had first proposed a different test using a tall metal rod, and scientists in France carried it out in May 1752, before news of his kite even reached them. They managed to draw electric sparks from storm clouds. The kite experiment itself probably took place in June 1752, though nobody knows the exact date.

A serious word of warning: flying a kite in a thunderstorm is extremely dangerous and can kill. Never try to repeat this experiment, and never fly a kite near power lines.

## Alessandro Volta: the first battery

Everything up to this point was static electricity, a quick spark and then nothing. In 1800 the Italian scientist Alessandro Volta built the "voltaic pile", the first battery to produce a steady electric current. That changed things, because a steady current can actually do work. The unit of voltage, the volt, is named after him.

## Michael Faraday: the secret of generators

Batteries run down, and the world needed a way to make electricity in bulk. The breakthrough came in 1831, when the British scientist Michael Faraday discovered **electromagnetic induction**: move a magnet inside a coil of wire and a current flows through the wire. It sounds almost too simple, yet it's the principle behind the generators in power stations all over the world, and behind electric motors too, from ceiling fans to washing machines.

## Thomas Edison: electricity comes home

In 1879 the American inventor Thomas Edison developed a practical, long-lasting incandescent light bulb, while Joseph Swan in Britain was working on a similar design at the same time. A bulb is no use without power, though, so in 1882 Edison opened the Pearl Street Station in New York, one of the first commercial power stations to supply homes and shops.

## Nikola Tesla: alternating current

Edison's system ran on direct current (DC). The engineer Nikola Tesla developed **alternating current (AC)** systems and motors instead, working with the businessman George Westinghouse. The two sides fought a famous rivalry known as the "War of the Currents", and AC won because it's much easier to send over long distances. It's still what comes out of the sockets in your home.

Here are the milestones in one place, which makes it handy for revision before a test:

| --- | --- | --- |

| 1752 | Shows that lightning is electricity; invents the lightning rod | Benjamin Franklin |

## Other scientists worth remembering

A few more names turn up in every physics textbook, and two of them became units of measurement:

| Scientist | Contribution |
| --- | --- |
| André-Marie Ampère | Studied the link between electricity and magnetism; the "ampere" is named after him |
| Georg Ohm | Formulated Ohm's law, relating voltage, current and resistance |
| James Clerk Maxwell | Unified electricity, magnetism and light in a single set of equations |

## Questions students ask about the inventor of electricity

### Who is the real inventor of electricity?

There isn't one, because electricity is a natural phenomenon. If a test asks for the key names, though: Thales first observed it, Volta built the first battery, Faraday opened the way to generating it, and Edison and Tesla brought it into everyday life.

### What about the "Baghdad Battery"?

It's a set of ancient clay jars found near Baghdad, which some people think might have produced a weak electric current. There's no conclusive evidence for that, and most researchers believe the jars had other uses.

### Did Benjamin Franklin invent electricity?

No. He showed that lightning is a form of electricity, invented the lightning rod and introduced terms we still use, such as positive and negative charge. But electricity itself was there all along.

### Why are electrical units named after scientists?

To honour them. The volt (voltage) is named after Volta, the ampere (current) after Ampère, and the ohm (resistance) after Ohm.

### When did electricity reach people's homes?

It began spreading in big cities in the late 19th century, then took many decades to reach most parts of the world.
