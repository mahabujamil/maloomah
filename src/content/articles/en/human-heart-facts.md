Press two fingers against the side of your neck and wait a moment. That steady tapping is the **human heart**, a muscle that started beating before you were born and will keep going, without rest, for the rest of your life. You never have to remind it. So where exactly is it, why do we feel it on the left, and how does it get blood to every cell in the body?

## Where is the human heart located?

Ask a class to point to their heart and most hands go to the left side of the chest. That's close, but not quite right. The heart sits **roughly in the middle of the chest, tilted to the left**. It lies between the lungs and behind the breastbone, in a space called the mediastinum. Its pointed lower tip, the apex, angles towards the left, and that's where the beat is easiest to feel.

So is the heart on the left side? Mostly in the centre, leaning left. A small number of people are born with the heart pointing to the right instead, a rare condition called dextrocardia.

## How big is the heart?

Make a tight fist. An adult heart is about that size. It weighs around 250 to 350 grams, depending on body size. It sits inside a double-layered sac called the pericardium, which protects it and cuts down friction as it beats thousands of times an hour.

## The four chambers of the heart

Inside, the heart is divided into **four chambers**. The two upper ones, the atria, collect blood. The two lower ones, the ventricles, push it out.

| Chamber | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Right atrium | Receives blood returning from the body, low in oxygen |
| Right ventricle | Pumps that blood to the lungs to pick up oxygen |
| Left atrium | Receives oxygen-rich blood from the lungs |
| Left ventricle | Pumps oxygen-rich blood to the whole body; the strongest chamber, with the thickest wall |

The left ventricle has the thickest wall for a good reason. It has to drive blood all the way to your toes and the top of your head, while the right ventricle only sends blood next door to the lungs.

Four valves sit between the chambers and work like one-way doors, so blood can't slosh backwards. The "lub-dub" a doctor hears through a stethoscope isn't the muscle squeezing at all. It's the sound of those valves snapping shut.

## How does the heart work?

Think of the heart as **two pumps joined together**. The right side sends tired, low-oxygen blood on a short loop to the lungs, called pulmonary circulation. There the blood drops off carbon dioxide and picks up fresh oxygen. It then flows into the left side, which pumps it out to the brain, muscles and every other organ. That longer loop is systemic circulation. Once the body's cells have taken the oxygen they need, the blood returns to the right side and the whole cycle repeats.

What tells the heart when to beat? Not your brain, surprisingly. The heart has its own electrical system, which starts in a small cluster of cells called the sinoatrial (SA) node. It acts as a built-in pacemaker, which is why your heart keeps time while you sleep or concentrate on homework.

> **Did you know?** An adult heart beats **about 100,000 times a day**, more than 2 billion times over an average lifetime. At rest it pumps about 5 litres of blood every minute, which adds up to roughly 7,000 litres a day.

## How many times does the heart beat per minute?

For an adult at rest, a normal heart rate is usually between 60 and 100 beats per minute. You can test it: count your pulse for one minute while sitting, then run up a flight of stairs and count again.

Exercise, excitement and heat all push the rate up, and sleep brings it down. Well-trained athletes often have a slower resting pulse than other people, because their hearts have grown strong enough to move more blood with each beat. Children's hearts beat faster than adults', so their normal range is different.

## Habits that keep your heart healthy

These are general healthy-living tips, not a substitute for advice from a doctor:

- Move regularly, for example brisk walking on most days of the week.
- Eat a balanced diet rich in vegetables, fruit and whole grains, with less salt, sugar and saturated fat.
- Don't smoke.
- Get enough sleep; see [how much sleep do you need](/en/articles/how-much-sleep-do-you-need/).
- Have regular check-ups for blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol, as your doctor advises.

## Questions students ask about the heart

### Why doesn't the heart get tired?

Heart muscle is a special kind of muscle, packed with mitochondria, the parts of a cell that produce energy. It has its own generous blood supply through the coronary arteries, and it gets a tiny rest, a fraction of a second, between every beat. Those short breaks add up over a day.

### When does a baby's heart start beating?

Very early, in the first weeks of pregnancy, before many other organs have finished forming. It is one of the first organs to start working, and it keeps going for a lifetime.
