Who invented the light bulb? Most people say Thomas Edison, 1879, and stop there. That answer isn't wrong, but it leaves out half the story. **Thomas Edison and Joseph Swan** both produced practical bulbs around 1879, one in the United States and one in Britain, and other inventors had been making light from electricity for decades before them. Plenty more came afterwards, which is how we got from a glowing bamboo thread to the LED in your ceiling.

## Who really invented the light bulb?

Getting light out of electricity was never the hard part. Scientists could do that long before Edison. The hard part was a bulb that lasted for hours, cost little, and was safe to leave on in a family's living room. Two people cracked it at almost the same moment.

**Joseph Swan** (1828–1914) was a British chemist and physicist who demonstrated a carbon-filament bulb in Newcastle around 1879, and homes and buildings in Britain were soon lit by his lamps. Thomas Edison (1847–1931), across the Atlantic, developed a carbon-filament bulb at his Menlo Park laboratory that passed its key test in October 1879. His patent was granted in January 1880.

So credit usually goes to both. Rather than fight it out for years, their British companies merged in 1883 under a name that stitched them together: "Ediswan".

## When was the light bulb invented? The early attempts

The first electric light came well over half a century before Swan and Edison. Britain's Humphry Davy demonstrated the arc lamp, in which a spark leaps between two carbon rods and gives off a dazzling glare. It was fine for streets and theatres, but nobody wanted to do homework under something that looked like a lightning flash. Gentler lamps were tried too; they were either far too expensive or burned out within minutes.

## What exactly did Edison add?

If Swan got there at the same time, why does Edison's name stick? Because Edison thought about the **whole system** a bulb needs, not just the bulb.

He started with the filament, the thin thread that glows inside the glass. His lab tested thousands of materials, landing first on carbon fibres and then on carbonised bamboo that lasted hundreds of hours. He also pushed for a better vacuum, since any oxygen left in the bulb quickly burns a hot filament away. But a perfect bulb is useless in a house with no power, so Edison designed generators, wiring, meters and switches, and in 1882 opened the Pearl Street Station in New York to supply homes with electricity.

That's what moved the bulb out of the lab and into ordinary houses. The story of electricity itself is a lesson of its own: [who invented electricity](/en/articles/who-invented-electricity/).

> **Did you know?** Lewis Latimer, an American engineer who later worked with Edison, patented an improved way of making carbon filaments in 1882, helping bulbs last longer and cost less.

## From tungsten to LED: the bulb after Edison

Bamboo didn't last long as the winner. In the early 1900s **tungsten** replaced carbon because it copes with much higher temperatures and so glows brighter, and in 1910 William Coolidge worked out how to draw it into fine wire. Engineers then stopped leaving bulbs empty and filled them with an inert gas that doesn't react with the filament, which made it last longer still.

Fluorescent lamps, the long tubes you often see in school ceilings, went on sale from the late 1930s and use less energy than incandescent bulbs. The era we live in now began in 1962, when Nick Holonyak made the first visible-light LED. White LED light stayed out of reach until the blue LED arrived, and three Japanese-born scientists won the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics for it, because that's what made white LED lighting possible.

Many countries have since phased out traditional incandescent bulbs in favour of LEDs. An incandescent bulb turns most of its energy, roughly 90%, into heat rather than light; hold your hand near an old one that's been on for a while and you'll feel it. Swapping it out is one of the simplest ways to [save electricity at home](/en/articles/how-to-save-electricity-at-home/).

## Questions students ask about the light bulb

### Did Edison invent the light bulb?
Not alone, and not first. Davy's arc lamp came decades earlier. What Edison did, alongside Swan, was make the first practical, long-lasting bulb, and then build the electrical system that made it worth owning. "Edison invented the light bulb" is a shorthand for a long team effort across the 19th century.

### Who invented the neon light?
French engineer Georges Claude, who demonstrated neon lamps in 1910.

### Who invented the LED light?
Nick Holonyak made the first visible-light LED in 1962. Many scientists developed it further, most famously the winners of the 2014 Nobel Prize for the blue LED.
