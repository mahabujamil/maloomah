Bahrain is an island, and for most of its history the only ways to reach it were by boat or by plane. That changed in 1986, when the **King Fahd Causeway** opened across the shallow water separating it from Saudi Arabia. Today it is the only land route connecting the island kingdom to the mainland, and one of the longest sea crossings in the region. It also pops up in Saudi search trends every so often, usually around holidays, when thousands of people want to know how bad the queues are.

## What is the King Fahd Causeway?

It's a road that runs over the Arabian Gulf from Al Khobar, in Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province, to Al Jasra in Bahrain. Some people simply call it the Bahrain Causeway. Its importance isn't really about size. Before it existed, a trip to Bahrain meant a ferry or a flight; afterwards you could get in a car and drive there, and travel, trade and tourism between the two countries changed as a result.

## How long is the King Fahd Causeway?

The whole crossing is **about 25 km (16 miles)**. If you picture it as one long bridge, though, you've got it slightly wrong. It is a chain of bridges joined by embankments, which are stretches of road built on land reclaimed from the sea. In shallow water that makes sense: filling in some sections is easier than putting the entire route up on pillars.

## History: when did the King Fahd Causeway open?

People talked about linking the two countries by land for decades before anything happened. The project agreement was finally signed in 1981, and the foundation stone went down in November 1982. Construction took about four years. On **26 November 1986**, King Fahd bin Abdulaziz and Bahrain's then ruler, Sheikh Isa bin Salman Al Khalifa, officially opened the causeway, which was named after King Fahd. Sources put the cost at roughly 3 billion Saudi riyals, paid for by Saudi Arabia.

Four years may sound slow for a road, but this one was built in open sea. Engineers had to reclaim land from the water and set large numbers of precast concrete supports in place, which made it a serious engineering challenge for its time.

> **Did you know?** Halfway along the King Fahd Causeway sits an artificial island, often called "Passport Island", where both countries carry out passport and customs checks. It also has a mosque, restaurants and tall towers you can see from far away.

## Passport Island: where are the border checks?

You might expect to leave Saudi Arabia at one end of the causeway and enter Bahrain at the other. In fact, travellers stop at the artificial island near the middle, where the Saudi and Bahraini passport and customs posts stand side by side. Having both countries' checks in one place saves a second stop.

The island also has insurance offices, service stations and rest areas. As traffic has grown, the procedures have been upgraded over the years to ease congestion, with extra lanes and more services available online.

## Tips before you drive across

Driving the causeway itself takes roughly 20 to 30 minutes. The total trip depends on the queues at the border posts, and during holidays that can add a lot of time. That's why so many people search for live causeway traffic before they leave. The King Fahd Causeway Authority's official channels post updates, and queues usually peak at weekends and on public holidays, so a quieter day is worth choosing if you can.

Before setting off, sort out the following:

- Insurance: your car needs valid insurance covering the other country. It can usually be bought online or at the insurance offices on the causeway, but check the current rules before you travel.
- Documents: a passport, or a GCC national ID for Gulf citizens, a visa if you need one, your driving licence and the vehicle registration. If the car isn't registered to you, you'll need an authorisation letter.
- Toll: vehicles pay a crossing fee. The amount and payment methods can change, so check the official website.
- Rules: keep to the speed limit and only stop in designated areas.

## The King Hamad Causeway: a second link

One road can only carry so much traffic, and the King Fahd Causeway is under growing pressure. Saudi Arabia and Bahrain have therefore announced a second, parallel crossing, the King Hamad Causeway. What makes it different is the plan to include a railway for passengers and freight alongside the road. When it will be finished depends on the construction phases the authorities announce.

If giant building projects interest you, the [Great Wall of China](/en/articles/great-wall-of-china/) is one of history's most famous, built for the opposite reason: the causeway was made to join two countries, the wall to keep people out.
