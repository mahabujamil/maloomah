When the first cold evenings of autumn arrive, searches for a **sahlab recipe** climb across the Middle East. Sahlab (also spelled salep) is a thick, silky milk drink scented with rose water and dusted with cinnamon, and you can make it at home in about ten minutes with ingredients you probably already have.

## What is sahlab?

**Sahlab** is a hot drink made from milk and a thickener, usually topped with cinnamon, coconut and nuts. It's a winter favourite in the Levant, Egypt and Turkey, sold in cafés and from street carts.

The name comes from **salep flour**, a powder ground from the tubers of certain wild orchids, mainly in the genus *Orchis*. Those tubers contain a starchy substance called glucomannan, which gives traditional sahlab its stretchy, velvety texture.

## Ingredients (makes 4 cups)

Real salep flour is rare and expensive, so most home recipes and nearly all commercial mixes today use **cornstarch** instead. The result is delicious and very close to the original.

| Ingredient | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Whole milk | 4 cups |
| Cornstarch | 3 tablespoons |
| Sugar | 4 tablespoons (or to taste) |
| Rose water or orange blossom water | 1 teaspoon |
| Ground mastic | a pinch (optional) |
| To garnish | cinnamon, coconut, pistachios or walnuts |

## How to make sahlab, step by step

1. **Dissolve the cornstarch in cold milk.** Whisk it into one cup of cold milk until smooth. Never add cornstarch straight to hot milk, or it will clump instantly.
2. **Warm the rest of the milk.** Heat the remaining three cups with the sugar over medium heat until hot but not boiling.
3. **Add the cornstarch mixture.** Pour it in gradually while whisking.
4. **Cook until thick.** Keep whisking for 5 to 8 minutes. Once it starts to bubble, it will thicken and turn glossy.
5. **Flavour it.** Add the mastic (crushed with a pinch of sugar), then the rose or orange blossom water, and remove from the heat.
6. **Serve hot.** Pour into cups and finish with cinnamon, coconut and nuts.

> **Did you know?** In the 18th and 19th centuries salep reached England, where it was sold as "saloop", a cheap hot drink that competed with tea and coffee until those became affordable for everyone.

## Tips for perfectly smooth sahlab

- **Whisk constantly.** Starch sticks to the bottom of the pan very quickly.
- **Adjust the thickness.** For a heavier drink add another half tablespoon of cornstarch; if it gets too thick, loosen it with a splash of hot milk.
- **Use whole milk** for a richer taste.
- **Add rose water at the end,** since long heating dulls its aroma.
- For an extra-indulgent version, replace some of the milk with **sweetened condensed milk** and cut back on the sugar.

## Why real salep is so rare

Each orchid plant dies when its tuber is harvested, and it takes many tubers to make a small amount of flour. As demand grew, researchers reported falling wild orchid populations, and even local extinctions, in parts of Turkey, Greece and Iran. Some countries now restrict harvesting or exporting it, which is why manufacturers turned to starches and flavourings.

Historical sources suggest salep was already being drunk in Ottoman palaces by around the 15th century. In the 17th century the Ottoman traveller Evliya Çelebi described salep sellers in Istanbul preparing it in copper kettles.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I make sahlab without cornstarch?

Yes. **Rice flour** works as a thickener, or you can buy a ready-made sahlab mix. Genuine salep flour exists but is scarce and pricey.

### Are store-bought sahlab mixes made from orchids?

Usually not. Most commercial mixes are based on starch, milk powder and flavourings. Check the ingredient list on the pack to know exactly what's inside.

### Can I use plant-based milk?

Yes. Oat or almond milk both work, but the texture is a little lighter, so you may need slightly more cornstarch.

### How long does sahlab keep?

It's best fresh. You can refrigerate it for a day, then reheat it gently with a splash of milk, whisking as it warms.

## Key takeaway

A good **sahlab recipe** needs only milk, cornstarch, sugar and rose water, plus about ten minutes of steady whisking. It's the perfect drink for cold evenings, and pairs nicely with a simple dessert like [basbousa](/en/articles/basbousa-recipe/), or follows a warming bowl of [lentil soup](/en/articles/lentil-soup-recipe/).
