Once the evenings turn cold, people start looking for a **sahlab recipe**. Sahlab (also spelled salep) is a thick, silky milk drink scented with rose water and dusted with cinnamon. It takes about ten minutes, the ingredients are probably already in your kitchen, and the only real trick is one small step at the start.

## What is sahlab?

It's a hot drink made from milk and a thickener, usually topped with cinnamon, coconut and nuts. In the Levant, Egypt and Turkey it's a winter staple, sold in cafés and from street carts.

The name comes from **salep flour**, a powder ground from the tubers of certain wild orchids, mostly in the genus *Orchis*. Those tubers contain a starchy substance called glucomannan, and that's what gives traditional sahlab its stretchy, velvety body.

## Ingredients (makes 4 cups)

Real salep flour is scarce and expensive, so most home cooks, and nearly every commercial mix, use **cornstarch** instead. It tastes very close to the original.

| Ingredient | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Whole milk | 4 cups |
| Cornstarch | 3 tablespoons |
| Sugar | 4 tablespoons (or to taste) |
| Rose water or orange blossom water | 1 teaspoon |
| Ground mastic | a pinch (optional) |
| To garnish | cinnamon, coconut, pistachios or walnuts |

## How to make sahlab, step by step

1. Dissolve the cornstarch in cold milk. Whisk it into one cup of cold milk until smooth. This is the step that matters: cornstarch tipped straight into hot milk clumps instantly.
2. Warm the rest of the milk. Heat the other three cups with the sugar over medium heat until hot but not boiling.
3. Add the cornstarch mixture. Pour it in slowly while whisking.
4. Cook until thick. Keep whisking for 5 to 8 minutes. Once it starts to bubble, it thickens and turns glossy.
5. Flavour it. Add the mastic (crushed with a pinch of sugar), then the rose or orange blossom water, and take the pan off the heat.
6. Serve hot, finished with cinnamon, coconut and nuts.

> **Did you know?** In the 18th and 19th centuries salep reached England, where it was sold as "saloop", a cheap hot drink that competed with tea and coffee until those became affordable for everyone.

## Getting a smooth, thick sahlab

Keep whisking the whole time. Starch catches on the bottom of the pan very quickly, and once it does you get lumps and a scorched taste. You control the thickness, too: add another half tablespoon of cornstarch for a heavier drink, or loosen it with a splash of hot milk if it's gone too far.

Whole milk gives a fuller flavour than low-fat. Leave the rose water until the end, because long heating dulls its scent. And if you want something richer, swap some of the milk for sweetened condensed milk and use less sugar.

## Why real salep is so rare

Harvesting the tuber kills the orchid, and a small amount of flour takes a lot of tubers. As demand grew, researchers reported falling wild orchid populations, and even local extinctions, in parts of Turkey, Greece and Iran. Some countries now restrict collecting or exporting it, which pushed manufacturers towards starches and flavourings.

The drink itself is old. Historical sources suggest it was already served in Ottoman palaces by around the 15th century, and in the 17th century the Ottoman traveller Evliya Çelebi described salep sellers in Istanbul making it in copper kettles.

## Questions about making sahlab at home

### Can I make sahlab without cornstarch?

Yes. Rice flour works as a thickener, or you can buy a ready-made sahlab mix. Genuine salep flour exists but is scarce and pricey.

### Are store-bought sahlab mixes made from orchids?

Usually not. Most are based on starch, milk powder and flavourings; the ingredient list on the pack will tell you exactly.

### Can I use plant-based milk?

Oat or almond milk both work, though the texture comes out a little lighter, so you may want slightly more cornstarch.

### How long does sahlab keep?

It's best fresh. It will keep in the fridge for a day; reheat it gently with a splash of milk, whisking as it warms.

On a cold night it goes well with a simple dessert like [basbousa](/en/articles/basbousa-recipe/), or after a warming bowl of [lentil soup](/en/articles/lentil-soup-recipe/).
