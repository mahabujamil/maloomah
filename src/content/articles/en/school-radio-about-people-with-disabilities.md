A **school assembly on people with disabilities** is one of the most meaningful scripts a school can present, because it teaches students to respect every person and value their abilities whatever their circumstances. Every year on **3 December** the world marks the **International Day of Persons with Disabilities**, which makes it a natural date for this morning programme (what Arab schools call "school radio"). Below is a complete, ready-to-read script: an introduction, Quran recitation, a hadith, a morning talk, a "Did you know?" segment, a short story, a quiz and a closing, plus a segment on famous people who overcame disability.

## Running order for a school assembly on people with disabilities

This order fits a morning assembly of about 10 to 12 minutes. Drop or shorten any segment to fit your time.

| Order | Segment | Approx. time | Presented by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Introduction | 1 minute | Host |
| 2 | Quran recitation | 1 minute | A student who recites well |
| 3 | Hadith | 1 minute | A student |
| 4 | Morning talk | 2 minutes | A student |
| 5 | "Did you know?" | 1.5 minutes | Two students taking turns |
| 6 | Famous people who overcame disability | 1.5 minutes | Three students |
| 7 | Short story | 2 minutes | A confident reader |
| 8 | Quiz | 2 minutes | Host |
| 9 | Closing | 1 minute | Host |

## Introduction to the assembly

> In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.
> Good morning to determination and hope, and to hearts that see before eyes do.
> Dear principal, dear teachers and fellow students, good morning.
> Today we want to talk about a valued part of our community: **people with disabilities**, sometimes also called people with special needs or "people of determination". They are our classmates, neighbours and family members. They have dreams and talents just like us, and what they need is a community that opens doors for them instead of closing them.
> We begin with verses from the Holy Quran, recited by ……

## Quran recitation

The student recites the verses below. The Arabic text is copied word for word from quran.com, and the English meaning is from the Saheeh International translation. It is best to recite them from the Mushaf with proper tajweed.

> ﴿عَبَسَ وَتَوَلَّىٰ ۝ أَن جَاءَهُ الْأَعْمَىٰ ۝ وَمَا يُدْرِيكَ لَعَلَّهُ يَزَّكَّىٰ ۝ أَوْ يَذَّكَّرُ فَتَنفَعَهُ الذِّكْرَىٰ﴾
> "He frowned and turned away because there came to him the blind man. But what would make you perceive that perhaps he might be purified, or be reminded and the remembrance would benefit him?" [Surah Abasa, 80:1–4]

> ﴿يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا يَسْخَرْ قَوْمٌ مِّن قَوْمٍ عَسَىٰ أَن يَكُونُوا خَيْرًا مِّنْهُمْ...﴾
> "O you who have believed, let not a people ridicule [another] people; perhaps they may be better than them…" [Surah Al-Hujurat, from 49:11]

**Host after the recitation:** The first verses of Surah Abasa were revealed about the blind companion **Abdullah ibn Umm Maktum**, and they remind us that a person's worth is not measured by their body. Next, a hadith presented by ……

## Hadith

> Abu Hurairah reported that the Messenger of God (peace be upon him) said: "God does not look at your appearance or your wealth, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds." [Sahih Muslim, 2564]

**Meaning in brief:** A person's true measure is their heart and their actions, not their looks, wealth or physical strength. No one has the right to look down on another because of their appearance or disability.

## Morning talk about people with disabilities

> Dear friends,
> The **World Health Organization** estimates that about **1.3 billion** people around the world live with a significant disability, roughly **one in every six people**. Disability is not rare or far away from us; it is part of every community.
> There are different kinds of disability: **physical**, affecting walking or movement; **visual**, affecting sight; **hearing**, affecting hearing; **intellectual**, affecting how quickly someone learns and understands; and conditions such as **autism**, which affects how a person communicates with others.
> Very often, though, the real barrier is the one society builds: stairs without a ramp, a book without Braille, or a hurtful, mocking look. When we remove those barriers, we discover amazing abilities.
> There is a lot we students can do. We can welcome a classmate with a disability and include them in games and group work. We can ask "Would you like some help?" before helping. We can refuse to mock or use hurtful nicknames, and we can keep reserved parking spaces and ramps clear.
> Let our motto today be: "Different abilities, equal dignity."

## "Did you know?" facts about people with disabilities

Two students can take turns reading these:

- **Did you know** that the UN General Assembly declared **3 December** the International Day of Persons with Disabilities in **1992**?
- **Did you know** that the **UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities** was adopted in **2006** and came into force in **2008**?
- **Did you know** that Frenchman **Louis Braille** lost his sight at the age of three, then as a teenager invented the raised-dot writing system for blind readers that carries his name, publishing it in **1829**?
- **Did you know** that each Braille character is built from a cell of **six raised dots**, arranged in different patterns to form letters and numbers?
- **Did you know** that **World Braille Day** is marked on **4 January**, Louis Braille's birthday?
- **Did you know** that the **Paralympic Games** for athletes with disabilities were first held in Rome in **1960**?

> **Did you know?** Sign language is not one single language worldwide: most countries have their own. That is why the UN marks the **International Day of Sign Languages** on **23 September**. For more surprising facts about the senses, visit the [human body "Did you know?" page](/en/did-you-know/body/).

## Famous people who overcame disability

Each student reads one paragraph:

1. **Taha Hussein:** an Egyptian writer and thinker who lost his sight as a child. He still earned a doctorate, became Egypt's Minister of Education and was called the "Dean of Arabic Literature".
2. **Helen Keller:** an American who lost both her hearing and her sight as a small child. She learned to read in Braille, and in **1904** became the first deafblind person to earn a university degree.
3. **Stephen Hawking:** a British physicist whose illness weakened his muscles until he used a wheelchair and spoke through a computer. He still became one of the best-known scientists of space and physics, writing books read by millions.

**Host:** None of them let disability stop their dreams, a reminder that [science and scholars](/en/articles/school-radio-about-science/) know no limits.

## A short story for the assembly

> **Story: Yusuf and the competition**
> Yusuf moved to a new school. He used a wheelchair, and in his first days he sat alone at break, watching the others play.
> Sami noticed, came over and asked: "What do you like to play?" Yusuf smiled and said, "I love chess, and I also like refereeing matches."
> The next day Sami brought a chessboard, and Yusuf beat him three times in a row! Soon Yusuf became the break-time referee, because he knew the rules better than anyone.
> At the end of the year the class chose Yusuf to represent them in the school chess competition, and he came home with first place.
> **The lesson:** one kind question can open the door to a friendship, and everyone has a talent waiting to be discovered.

## Closing

> To close our assembly, let us remember that disability is not the end of the road, and that determination can achieve wonders.
> Let us support our classmates, offering friendship rather than pity, and respecting them as we would like to be respected.
> Thank you for listening, and peace be upon you.

## Frequently asked questions

### When is the International Day of Persons with Disabilities?

It is on **3 December** every year. The UN General Assembly proclaimed it in 1992, which is why schools often hold assemblies on people with disabilities in late November and early December.

### What is the difference between "people with disabilities", "special needs" and "people of determination"?

They refer to roughly the same group. "Persons with disabilities" is the wording used in the UN Convention, "special needs" is common in schools, and "people of determination" is an encouraging term used in some Arab countries. What matters is choosing words that respect the person.

### How can I adapt this assembly for primary school?

Shorten the morning talk and focus on the story and the quiz. If you can, show the students a page written in Braille, or ask a student to teach everyone the sign for "thank you" in your local sign language.

### Can a student with a disability help present the assembly?

Yes, and it is one of the best things a school can do on this day, as long as the student wants to. They can present a favourite segment, read the closing or run the quiz.

## Key takeaway

A **school assembly on people with disabilities** teaches students that dignity is equal even when abilities differ. Use the ready-made segments above as they are or adapt them for your school, and present them in the week of **3 December** with the motto "Different abilities, equal dignity."
