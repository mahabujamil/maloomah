Coffee is the first thing many Arab families offer a guest, and hundreds of millions of people around the world start their day with a cup. So **where does coffee come from**? In brief: **the plant is native to the highlands of Ethiopia, and the drink was first reliably recorded in Yemen in the 15th century.** How it got from there to nearly every country on Earth is a story of ports, coffeehouses and a word that changed shape as it travelled.

## The Ethiopian highlands: coffee's original home

The best-known coffee species is called Arabica (Coffea arabica), which is a little misleading, because it didn't start in Arabia. It is native to the highlands of Ethiopia in East Africa, where coffee shrubs grew wild in the forests long before anyone elsewhere knew of them.

You may have heard the tale of Kaldi, an Ethiopian goat herder who noticed his goats became oddly lively after nibbling the berries of a certain shrub, and tried them himself. It's a good story, but treat it as a legend. It only appears in writing centuries later, and there's no historical evidence behind it.

## Yemen, where coffee became a drink

On this point historians agree: the earliest reliable evidence of people drinking coffee comes from Yemen in the 15th century. It was used to stay awake through long nights, so from the start it was linked with alertness.

Yemen also sent coffee out into the world, through the Red Sea port of Mocha (al-Makha). The port was so well known that its name still appears on café menus. Order a mocha today and you are, in a sense, naming an old Yemeni harbour.

## How coffee spread around the world

Coffee didn't spread all at once. It moved city by city over several centuries:

| Stage | Place | Approximate period |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wild plant | Ethiopian highlands | Before the 15th century |
| First documented drink | Yemen | 15th century |
| Spread to the Hejaz and Egypt | Mecca and Cairo | Early 16th century |
| Famous early coffeehouses | Istanbul | Mid-16th century |
| Arrival in Europe | Venice, London and others | 17th century |
| Grown outside Africa and Arabia | Java, then Latin America | Late 17th and 18th centuries |

Istanbul was a turning point. Its coffeehouses became places to talk, read poetry and play games. The name of the drink followed the same road as the beans: the English word "coffee" comes from the Dutch *koffie*, which came from the Turkish *kahve*, which came from the Arabic qahwa.

> **Did you know?** Brazil is now the world's largest coffee producer, yet coffee only reached it in the 18th century. The country that grows the most coffee isn't where coffee began.

Vietnam, Colombia and Ethiopia follow Brazil among the big producers, so coffee's first home is still one of its main sources.

## Arabic coffee and Turkish coffee

Arabic coffee is usually made from lightly roasted beans, brewed with cardamom and sometimes saffron or cloves. It's poured into small cups, usually without sugar, and is closely tied to customs of welcoming guests. UNESCO added it to its list of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2015.

Turkish coffee works differently. The beans are ground very fine and boiled with water, and sometimes sugar, in a small long-handled pot called a cezve. Nothing is filtered out, so the foam and the grounds end up in the cup. Turkish coffee culture and tradition went onto the UNESCO list in 2013.

Is Turkish coffee really Turkish, then? The brewing style and the coffeehouse culture flourished in Ottoman Istanbul, but the coffee itself arrived there from Yemen.

## Arabica vs Robusta

Most of the world's coffee comes from two species. Arabica is the most widely produced; it grows at higher altitudes and has a smoother, more varied flavour. Robusta copes better with heat and pests, so it can be farmed where Arabica struggles, but it tastes stronger and more bitter and contains more caffeine.
