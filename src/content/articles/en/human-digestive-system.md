A sandwich at break time takes you five minutes to eat. Dealing with it takes your body far longer: hours at the very least, and often a day or more. That slow, carefully ordered work is the job of the **human digestive system**. It breaks food into particles small enough for the blood to absorb and deliver to every cell, and then it gets rid of whatever the body can't use. Below we go through the organs one by one and follow a single mouthful from start to finish.

## What is the digestive system?

The digestive system is a team of organs with three jobs, done in order. Digestion breaks food down into tiny molecules. Absorption moves those useful molecules into the blood. Elimination clears out the leftovers. Each job depends on the one before it: food that hasn't been broken down can't be absorbed, and anything that isn't absorbed has to leave somehow.

It helps to split the system into two parts. The first is the digestive tract, also called the alimentary canal: one long tube that food actually travels through, from the mouth to the anus. The second is the group of accessory organs. Food never passes through them, but they release juices that make digestion possible. These are the salivary glands, the liver, the gallbladder and the pancreas. Think of the tract as a production line and the accessory organs as workshops beside it, sending in the tools the line needs.

## Organs of the digestive tract and their functions

Here are the organs food passes through, in order:

| Organ | What it does | Key fact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mouth | Teeth cut and grind food; saliva moistens it | Adults usually have 32 teeth; children have 20 milk teeth |
| Pharynx | Shared passage for food and air | The epiglottis covers the windpipe when you swallow |
| Oesophagus | Muscular tube that carries food to the stomach | About 25 cm long in adults |
| Stomach | Stores food and mixes it with gastric juice | Releases hydrochloric acid and the enzyme pepsin |
| Small intestine | Finishes digestion and absorbs nutrients | The longest part of the tract, about 6 to 7 metres |
| Large intestine | Absorbs water and salts; forms waste | About 1.5 metres long, and wider than the small intestine |
| Rectum and anus | Store waste, then expel it | The end of the digestive tract |

So the longest organ in the system is the small intestine. In an adult it runs to about 6 to 7 metres, and it only fits inside the abdomen because it is coiled up tightly.

The small intestine has three sections: the **duodenum** first, where pancreatic juice and bile flow in, then the jejunum, then the ileum. Its inner wall is packed with tiny finger-like bumps called **villi**. Why bother with bumps? Because nutrients are absorbed through the surface of the wall, and every fold and bump adds surface. A fluffy towel soaks up water faster than a smooth cloth of the same size for exactly the same reason.

## The accessory organs

Help arrives as soon as food enters your mouth. The salivary glands produce saliva, which wets the food so it slides down easily. Saliva also carries the enzyme amylase, so starch starts being digested before the food is even swallowed.

The **liver** is the largest internal organ in the human body, and its largest gland as well. For digestion, its main contribution is bile, which helps the body deal with fat. That is only one of its many jobs, though: it also stores some vitamins and clears certain harmful substances from the blood. Tucked underneath it is the gallbladder, a small sac that stores bile and squeezes it into the duodenum when it's needed, rather like a reserve tank that opens when a fatty meal arrives.

The pancreas does two quite different things. It releases a juice full of enzymes that digest starch, protein and fat, and it also makes the hormone insulin, which controls the amount of sugar in the blood. That's why it turns up twice in a biology course, once with digestion and once with hormones.

## Following food through the system, step by step

Take one bite of an apple and track where it goes:

1. In the mouth, the front teeth bite off a piece and the back teeth grind it. The tongue turns it over and mixes it with saliva until it becomes a soft lump called a bolus.
2. To swallow, the tongue pushes the bolus back into the pharynx. Air uses this passage too, so the **epiglottis** closes over the windpipe to stop food getting into the lungs. When something "goes down the wrong way" and you cough hard, that flap didn't close in time.
3. Food doesn't simply fall down the oesophagus. Its muscles squeeze in waves, a movement called peristalsis, which pushes the food along to the stomach.
4. In the stomach, strong muscles churn the food with acidic gastric juice for several hours until it turns into a thick liquid called chyme.
5. In the small intestine, pancreatic juice, bile and intestinal juice finish the digestion, and the villi absorb the nutrients into the blood.
6. In the large intestine, most of the remaining water and salts are taken back, and waste is formed.
7. Finally, waste is stored in the rectum until it leaves the body.

In short, digestion starts in the mouth with chewing and the enzyme in saliva, and it is completed in the small intestine, where nutrients are absorbed. The large intestine then recovers water and forms waste. The whole trip varies a lot between people and between meals, taking anywhere from about a day to a few days.

A sharp student might spot a puzzle here. The stomach makes acid and an enzyme that digests protein, and its own wall is made of protein. So why doesn't it digest itself? Its wall is covered by a layer of mucus that shields it from the acid and enzymes, and the cells of its lining are constantly being replaced, so any damage is quickly repaired.

> **Did you know?** The small intestine is called "small" because it's narrower than the large intestine, not because it's shorter. In fact it is several times longer.

## Mechanical and chemical digestion

Two kinds of digestion go on side by side. Mechanical digestion breaks food into smaller pieces without changing what it is made of: chewing, and the churning of the stomach. A chewed bit of apple is still apple, just in smaller pieces. Chemical digestion is different, because it turns food into simpler substances using **enzymes** and digestive juices, so starch, for example, becomes sugars.

The two support each other. Chewing makes the pieces smaller, which gives the enzymes more surface to work on. These are the main enzymes and juices involved:

| Enzyme or juice | Made in | What it digests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salivary amylase | Salivary glands | Starts digesting starch |
| Pepsin | Stomach | Starts digesting protein |
| Bile | Liver (stored in the gallbladder) | Breaks fat into small droplets |
| Lipase | Pancreas | Digests fat |
| Pancreatic amylase | Pancreas | Finishes digesting starch |
| Trypsin | Pancreas | Continues digesting protein |

Notice what bile actually does. It doesn't digest fat itself; it breaks fat into small droplets so that lipase can digest it more easily, a bit like washing-up liquid breaking a greasy patch on a plate into droplets that rinse away. ## How to keep your digestive system healthy

Most of what your gut needs comes down to everyday habits:

- Chew your food well and don't rush. Chewing is the first step of mechanical digestion, and whatever you skip there leaves more work for your stomach.
- Eat plenty of vegetables, fruit and whole grains, which are rich in fibre.
- Drink enough water through the day. Read: [How much water should you drink a day?](/en/articles/how-much-water-should-you-drink/)
- Wash your hands before eating, and wash fruit and vegetables well.
- Stay active and exercise regularly.
