Why has the dollar cost the same in Riyadh for decades, while in Cairo its price can shift from one month to the next? That question gets to the heart of **how exchange rates are determined**: **either a central bank fixes the rate by pegging its currency to another, or the market sets it through supply and demand**. Millions of people search "dollar rate today" every day, but far fewer know what sits behind the number on the bank screen or the board outside an exchange office.

> This article is general financial education only. It contains no currency forecasts or investment advice.

## What is an exchange rate?

An exchange rate is simply the price of one currency paid in another. If one US dollar equals 3.75 Saudi riyals, then buying a single dollar costs you 3.75 riyals.

Most currencies are quoted against the dollar because it is the world's main currency for international trade and for central bank reserves. Key commodities such as oil and gold are priced in it too, so the dollar ends up as the ruler everything else is measured with.

## Supply and demand: how exchange rates are determined in the market

A currency follows the same rule as tomatoes at the market. When more people want it, its price rises; when there is more of it around than people want, its price falls. The real question is what makes people, companies and investors want a country's currency, or want to get rid of it. These are the main forces:

| Factor | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Interest rates | Higher rates attract investors, increasing demand for the currency |
| Inflation | High inflation erodes a currency's purchasing power over time |
| Trade balance | A country that imports more than it exports needs more foreign currency to pay for those imports |
| Tourism and remittances | Money from visitors and workers abroad brings in foreign currency and supports the local one |
| Central bank reserves | Large foreign reserves give a central bank firepower to defend its currency |
| Confidence and stability | Investors prefer the currencies of stable economies |

These factors pull on each other. A country that buys more wheat, fuel and phones from abroad than it sells keeps needing dollars, and that steady demand weighs on its own currency. Tourists arriving and families receiving money from relatives working overseas bring dollars back in and ease the pressure. That is why currency values are so closely tied to [inflation](/en/articles/what-is-inflation/) and to the interest rates central banks set.

## Fixed vs floating exchange rates

Not every country runs its currency the same way. There are two main systems, plus a range of hybrids that borrow from both.

### Fixed (pegged) exchange rates

With a **peg**, the central bank picks a fixed price for its currency against another one, usually the dollar. To keep it there, the bank trades from its reserves, selling dollars when demand for them climbs and buying them when they flood in. The best-known Arab examples are the Saudi riyal, pegged at 3.75 per dollar since 1986 according to the Saudi Central Bank; the Qatari riyal, pegged at 3.64 per dollar; and the Jordanian dinar, pegged since the mid-1990s at about 0.709 per dollar.

The appeal is predictability. A shop owner in Amman knows roughly what next season's imported stock will cost, and a family can budget for a trip without surprises. The catch is that holding the line takes large foreign-currency reserves.

### Floating exchange rates

Under a floating system the market sets the price, so it moves daily and sometimes by the second. The euro, yen and British pound all float against the dollar. In the Arab world, Egypt's central bank moved to a flexible, market-determined exchange rate in March 2024.

A float absorbs economic shocks on its own. The downside is that its swings feed quickly into the prices of imported goods, which people notice at the checkout.

> **Did you know?** Global currency trading reached about $9.6 trillion per day in April 2025, according to preliminary figures from the Bank for International Settlements' survey, and the US dollar was on one side of roughly 89% of all trades.

## Why are buying and selling prices different?

Walk into any bank or exchange office and you'll see two numbers, not one. The buy rate is what the bank pays you for your dollars. The sell rate is what it charges when you buy dollars from it, and it is always higher. The gap is called the **spread**, and it's the dealer's margin for providing the service.

So the amount you actually receive can differ from the "official" or mid-market rate shown in the news or on search engines. Before you exchange money, compare a few licensed providers; a small difference per dollar adds up quickly on a larger sum.

## How exchange rates show up in everyday life

It's easy to think exchange rates only matter to banks and traders, but they reach ordinary households in several ways. When the local currency weakens, imported goods such as phones, cars and some foods get more expensive, because shopkeepers now pay more local money for the same dollars. Travel and study abroad follow the same pattern: a strong currency makes trips and tuition cheaper, and a weak one makes them pricier.

There's a flip side. Families receiving remittances from abroad get more local currency when their own currency weakens. And if you [earn money online](/en/articles/ways-to-earn-money-online/) in dollars, what that income is worth at home changes with the rate on the day you convert it.

## Questions people ask about the dollar rate

### Who sets the dollar rate in a country?

It depends on the system. Under a peg, the central bank sets and defends it. Under a floating system, market supply and demand set it, though the central bank may still step in at times.

### Is a rising dollar always bad?

Not necessarily. A weaker local currency makes imports dearer, but it can also help exports and tourism, since the country's goods and hotels become cheaper for foreigners.

### What's the difference between the official rate and the parallel market?

The official rate is what licensed banks quote. A parallel, or black, market usually appears when foreign currency is scarce. It's illegal in many countries and carries a real risk of fraud.

### Can anyone predict tomorrow's exchange rate?

No one can forecast currency moves reliably, because they react to many factors, some of which arrive without warning, like a government decision or a global crisis. Be wary of anyone promising guaranteed profits from currency trading. Understanding interest rates, inflation and the trade balance is enough to read economic news with a clearer eye and make calmer money decisions.
