Ask a class what they know about the Great Wall of China and someone will almost certainly say it's the only man-made thing you can see from space. It isn't. The truth is stranger: the **Great Wall of China** isn't really one wall at all. It is the result of more than 2,000 years of building and rebuilding, the longest defensive structure people have ever made, and one of the New Seven Wonders of the World. So what exactly is it?

## What is the Great Wall of China?

Picture a string of kingdoms along China's northern frontier, each putting up a wall to protect itself. Then picture later states joining some of those walls together, extending them and patching the broken parts, century after century. That is roughly the story. The Great Wall is a network of walls and fortifications built by successive Chinese states and dynasties, and it includes watchtowers, forts, fortified mountain passes and trenches as well as the walls themselves.

It runs across northern China, from near the coast in the east to the deserts of Gansu province in the west. UNESCO added it to the World Heritage List in 1987, and in 2007 it was voted one of the New Seven Wonders of the World.

## Who built the Great Wall of China?

No single person, and no single generation. Still, one name is tied to it more than any other: Qin Shi Huang, who became the first emperor of a unified China in 221 BC.

Before unification, China's rival kingdoms each guarded their borders against one another. Once the country was a single state, many of those internal walls stopped mattering, while the threat from the north remained. So around 220 BC Qin Shi Huang ordered several northern walls to be joined into one defensive system. Chinese historical records say the general Meng Tian oversaw much of the work.

The people who actually carried the earth and stone were mostly soldiers, conscripted peasants and convicts. It was punishing work in steep mountains and bitter winters, and many died doing it. Nobody has reliable figures for how many, so be wary of any source that quotes an exact number.

## When was the Great Wall of China built?

The earliest walls go back to around the 7th century BC, and building and repairs carried on, with long gaps, until the 17th century AD.

| Phase | Approximate date | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rival kingdoms | From about the 7th century BC | Separate walls built to guard borders |
| Qin dynasty | About 221–206 BC | Northern walls linked into one system |
| Han dynasty | 206 BC – AD 220 | Wall extended west to protect trade routes |
| Ming dynasty | 1368–1644 | Large-scale rebuilding in stone and brick |
| World Heritage listing | 1987 | Recognised by UNESCO |

Here's why the dates matter. The postcard image of the wall, solid stone climbing green hills near Beijing at Badaling and Mutianyu, is almost all Ming dynasty work. The Ming built in brick and stone, whereas many earlier walls were made of rammed earth, soil packed down layer by layer until it holds. That difference explains why Ming sections still stand while so much of the older wall has worn away.

## Why was the Great Wall of China built?

Mainly to protect the farmland of northern China from raids by nomadic peoples of the northern steppe. A farmer can't pick up his fields and run; a mounted raider can strike fast and vanish. A wall slowed attackers down and bought time.

It did more than block the way, though. Watchtowers along its length passed warnings from one to the next with smoke by day and fire by night, so news of a raid could reach the garrison before the raiders did. Gates controlled the flow of people and caravans, especially on Silk Road routes, which made the wall a way to watch trade and collect tolls. And in rough mountain country the walkway on top doubled as a road for moving soldiers and supplies.

> **Did you know?** Chinese researchers have found that Ming builders mixed **sticky rice soup** into their lime mortar, making it stronger and more water-resistant. It is one reason some sections have survived for centuries.

## How long is the Great Wall of China?

That depends on what you count. If you mean the Ming wall alone, China announced in 2009 that it measures about 8,851.8 km, from Hushan in Liaoning province in the east to the Jiayuguan Pass in Gansu in the west. The figure includes walls, trenches and stretches of natural barrier such as hills and rivers.

If you mean every wall from every dynasty, the number is far bigger. In 2012, after a field survey that began in 2007, China's State Administration of Cultural Heritage put the full **length of the Great Wall of China** at about **21,196 km**.

| Measurement | Approximate length |
| --- | --- |
| Ming-dynasty wall (2009 survey) | About 8,850 km |
| Whole wall, all dynasties (2012 survey) | About 21,196 km |
| Whole wall in miles | About 13,170 miles |

Height and width vary a great deal. Restored stone sections near Beijing are usually a few metres high and can reach about 7–8 metres, with a walkway wide enough for several people side by side. Out west, many old earthen stretches are so eroded and low that you could walk past one without realising it was part of the wall.

## Can you see the Great Wall of China from space?

Back to that classroom claim. **The Great Wall of China is not visible to the naked eye from space** under normal conditions, however long it is. What makes something visible from far away is its width and contrast, not its length. The wall is very narrow for its length, and its colour is close to the soil and rock around it, so it blends in. A wide motorway is easier to spot.

Yang Liwei, China's first astronaut, said after his 2003 flight that he did not see it, and NASA has said the wall is generally not visible to the unaided eye from low Earth orbit. Some astronauts have photographed it with zoom lenses, and a few say they glimpsed parts of it in ideal light when they knew exactly where to look. That is a long way from the popular myth. From the Moon, it is completely impossible.

## Questions students ask about the Great Wall

### How long did it take to build?

It was never built in one go. Work came in phases over more than 2,000 years, from around the 7th century BC until the end of the Ming dynasty in 1644.

### Where is the Great Wall of China?

In northern China, crossing many provinces from east to west. The most visited sections are near Beijing, such as Badaling and Mutianyu.

### What should I write if a test asks who built it?

Say that it was built over many dynasties, that Qin Shi Huang linked the northern walls around 220 BC, and that the Ming dynasty built most of the sections that survive today. Those three points make a complete and accurate answer.
