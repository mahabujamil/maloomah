You're on the school bus when the driver brakes hard, and your body lurches forward even though nobody pushed you. When the bus pulls away again, you feel pressed back into the seat. Those two moments are a good way into **Newton's three laws of motion**, three short rules that explain why things start moving, keep moving and stop, whether it's a football on the playground or a rocket leaving the launch pad.

## Who was Newton?

Isaac Newton was an English mathematician, physicist and astronomer, born in 1642 in a small village in Lincolnshire and died in 1727. In 1687 he published the book usually called the *Principia*. In it he set out the three laws of motion and used them to build his law of universal gravitation, which explains both why an apple falls and why the Moon keeps circling Earth.

That wasn't all he did. He showed that white light is a mixture of the colours of the spectrum, built a telescope that used a mirror instead of lenses, and was one of the founders of calculus. The unit of force is named after him: the **newton**.

The apple story comes from Newton himself, who told it late in his life: he saw an apple fall and wondered why it always drops straight towards the ground. The popular version where it hits him on the head has no evidence behind it.

## The first law: inertia

An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion keeps moving at the same speed in the same direction, unless an unbalanced force acts on it.

This tendency to keep doing whatever it's already doing is called **inertia**. It's exactly what happened on the bus. Your body was moving along with the bus; when the bus stopped, nothing had stopped you yet, so you carried on forward. A seatbelt exists to supply that missing force.

A sharp student usually objects here. "If I roll a ball across the playground, it stops by itself." It doesn't, though. Friction with the ground and air resistance are acting on it the whole time. Roll the same ball across smooth ice and it goes much further, because there's far less friction to slow it.

You can spot inertia all over the place:

- Beat a rug and the fabric jumps while the dust tends to stay where it was, so the two separate.
- Yank a sheet of paper quickly from under a cup and the cup barely moves.
- Loose bags on a car's back seat slide forward when the car stops suddenly.

## The second law: force equals mass times acceleration

The first law says a force is what changes motion. The second law tells you by how much:

**F = m × a**, force equals mass multiplied by acceleration.

Acceleration means how quickly velocity changes each second. Two everyday experiments make the idea clear. Push an empty shopping trolley, then push a full one just as hard; the empty one speeds up much more, because it has less mass. Or kick the same ball gently and then hard; the hard kick sends it off faster, because the force is bigger.

So the law says two things at once. More force gives more acceleration, and more mass gives less acceleration for the same force.

### A worked example

A student pushes a 20 kg cart and it accelerates at 2 m/s². How big was the force?

F = m × a = 20 × 2 = 40 newtons.

Now the same student pushes a 40 kg cart with the same 40 N. The acceleration is a = F ÷ m = 40 ÷ 40 = 1 m/s². Doubling the mass halved the acceleration.

## The third law: action and reaction

For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. Forces always come in pairs: if one object pushes on another, the second pushes back on the first just as hard, in the opposite direction.

Try it on a skateboard or a wheeled office chair. Push against a wall and you roll away from it. You pushed the wall, and the wall pushed you back by the same amount.

Swimmers rely on this. They push water backwards with their arms, and the water pushes them forwards. A rocket blasts hot gas downwards with enormous force, and the gas pushes the rocket upwards. That is why a rocket works in space, where there's no air for it to push against.

One common mistake is worth flagging. The action and reaction forces don't cancel each other out, because they act on different objects. When you push the wall, your force acts on the wall and the wall's force acts on you.

> **Did you know?** One newton is roughly the weight of a small apple. An apple of about 100 grams is pulled towards Earth with a force of close to one newton, so the apple and Newton are still linked, even in the units of physics.

## The three laws at a glance

| Law | What it says | Everyday example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First (inertia) | Objects keep their state of rest or motion unless a force acts | Passengers lurch forward when the bus brakes |
| Second (F = m × a) | More force means more acceleration; more mass means less | An empty trolley is easier to speed up than a full one |
| Third (action and reaction) | Every force has an equal, opposite partner | A rocket rises by pushing gas downwards |

## Questions students ask about Newton's laws

### Are Newton's laws still true today?

For everyday life, yes. Engineers use them to design cars and bridges and to plan satellite orbits. In the 20th century, scientists found they need replacing in two situations: very tiny objects such as electrons, and objects moving close to the speed of light. That's where relativity and quantum mechanics take over.

### What's the difference between mass and weight?

Mass is the amount of matter in an object. It's measured in kilograms and doesn't change wherever you go. Weight is the force of gravity pulling on that mass, measured in newtons. An astronaut on the Moon has exactly the same mass as on Earth but weighs only about a sixth as much, because the Moon's gravity is weaker.

### What's an easy way to remember the three laws?

Tie each law to one picture: the first to the braking bus, the second to the empty and full trolleys, and the third to the rocket. Remember the picture and the law comes with it.
