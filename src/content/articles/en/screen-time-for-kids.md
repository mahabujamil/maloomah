A toddler who won't eat until a video is playing, a tablet in the car, a TV on from breakfast to bedtime. Sooner or later the same question comes up: how much **screen time for kids** is actually OK? The World Health Organization (WHO) and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) both give clear guidance, and it comes first below, followed by where the harm comes from and steps that work at home.

## Screen time for kids by age

In 2019 the WHO published guidelines for children under 5 covering physical activity, sitting and sleep, and they include clear limits on screen time spent sitting still. The AAP has advised for years that children avoid screens before about 18 months, with one exception: video chatting with family. Here is how the two sets of advice line up:

| Child's age | WHO (2019) | American Academy of Pediatrics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under 1 | No screen time recommended | Avoid screens except video chatting |
| 1 to 2 years | Sedentary screen time not recommended | Before 18 months: video chat only. After: high-quality content, watched with a parent |
| 2 years | No more than 1 hour a day; less is better | About 1 hour of high-quality content, watched together |
| 3–4 years | No more than 1 hour a day; less is better | About 1 hour of high-quality content, watched together |
| 5 and up | Not covered by these guidelines | A family media plan rather than a fixed number |

Put simply: before age 2, almost no screens apart from video calls; from 2 to 4, an hour a day at most, and less is better. Older children don't get a magic number. They get a plan the family agrees on.

A new AAP policy statement on children and the digital world, published in January 2026, pushes further in that direction. It cares less about counting hours and more about **content quality, app design** and what screens crowd out, such as sleep, play and family time. That matters most for older children and teens. The AAP sums up its approach as the "5 Cs": think about your child, the content, how they calm down, what media is crowding out, and keep communicating.

## Screen time for children under 2

Why so much caution in the earliest years? Because this is when the brain and language grow fastest, and babies learn mostly from live interaction. A baby babbles and a parent answers; a baby looks up and gets a smile back. A screen can't do that back-and-forth, and that gap is the root of the main concerns.

The first is language. Many studies have linked heavy early screen use with delays in some language skills. The likely reason isn't the screen itself so much as what it replaces: every quiet half hour in front of a video is a half hour the child wasn't hearing and answering a parent or grandparent.

The second is attention and self-soothing. If a screen becomes the go-to fix whenever a child cries or gets bored, they get less practice calming themselves. Heavy use is associated with attention difficulties in some children, although an association like that doesn't prove screens alone are the cause.

The third is movement. WHO recommends that 3–4-year-olds get at least 180 minutes of varied physical activity a day. Hours on a screen usually come straight out of that time.

### Are tablets worse than TV?

Not in themselves. Everything above applies to an iPad too, but a tablet differs in three ways: it's held close to the face, it goes everywhere from the bedroom to a restaurant, and its apps are often designed to keep children watching as long as possible. That makes it harder to keep in check than a TV fixed to the living room wall.

## Effects of screens on kids' eyes

When children focus on a screen up close, they blink less than usual, and long stretches of this can cause **digital eye strain**: dry, tired eyes and headaches. Researchers also link lots of near work and too little time outdoors with **myopia (nearsightedness)**, which is becoming more common in children. Studies suggest outdoor play and daylight may help protect children's eyes.

To ease strain, eye doctors, including the American Academy of Ophthalmology, recommend the 20-20-20 rule. Every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet (6 meters) away for 20 seconds. If your child holds things very close or often complains of headaches, have their eyes checked.

> **Did you know?** The AAP makes an exception for video chatting with grandparents and relatives, even for babies. It's a live, two-way interaction where someone responds to the child, not passive watching.

## Screens and sleep

Screen use near bedtime is linked to falling asleep later and sleeping less. Two things seem to be going on: exciting content keeps the brain alert, and screen light is thought to affect the sleep hormone melatonin. That's why many health bodies, including official UK guidance from 2019, advise avoiding screens for about an hour before bed and keeping devices out of the bedroom overnight. ## Cutting back without a battle

Rules agreed together tend to stick better than a sudden ban. Sit down as a family and make a **family media plan** that covers when screens are used, where, and what kind of content is allowed; the AAP offers a free template for this. Then pick a couple of screen-free zones. Bedrooms and the dinner table are the easiest places to start.

When your child does watch, join in if you can. Ask what's happening, and connect it to real life: "That dog looks like the one next door, doesn't it?" Watching becomes something to talk about instead of a replacement for talking. Be choosy about what goes on, too. Slower-paced, educational shows with no ads and no autoplay are a much better bet than an app that rolls from one clip to the next. The parental controls built into most devices let you set time limits and approve apps.

Screens also shouldn't be the only way your child learns to settle. Help them put a name to feelings ("You're cross because the tower fell down") and find other ways to calm down, like a hug, slow breathing or a quieter game. For more on this, see: [How to handle toddler tantrums](/en/articles/how-to-deal-with-child-tantrums/)

Finally, children copy what they see. If a parent's phone never leaves the table at dinner, it's hard to argue a child out of their tablet, so cutting back on your own phone use around them does a lot of the work. Most of the harm from screens comes down to what they replace: sleep, movement and time with you. Protect those three and encourage outdoor play.

## Questions parents ask about screen time

### How much screen time should a 2-year-old have?

WHO says no more than 1 hour a day, and less is better. The AAP similarly suggests sticking to high-quality content, ideally watched with a parent.

### Is any screen time OK for babies under 2?

The AAP recommends avoiding screens before about 18 months except video chatting, and WHO recommends no sedentary screen time before age 2.
