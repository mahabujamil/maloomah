A phone at the dinner table, a tablet in the car, the TV on in the background all day. It raises a fair question: how much **screen time for kids** is actually OK? This guide sums up what the World Health Organization (WHO) and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommend and offers practical steps that work at home.

## Screen time for kids by age

In 2019 the **WHO** published guidelines for children under 5 on physical activity, sitting and sleep, including clear limits on sedentary screen time. The **AAP** has long advised avoiding screens before about 18 months, apart from video chatting with family.

| Child's age | WHO (2019) | American Academy of Pediatrics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under 1 | No screen time recommended | Avoid screens except video chatting |
| 1 to 2 years | Sedentary screen time not recommended | Before 18 months: video chat only. After: high-quality content, watched with a parent |
| 2 years | No more than 1 hour a day; less is better | About 1 hour of high-quality content, watched together |
| 3–4 years | No more than 1 hour a day; less is better | About 1 hour of high-quality content, watched together |
| 5 and up | Not covered by these guidelines | A family media plan rather than a fixed number |

In January 2026 the AAP published a new policy statement on children and the digital world. It puts more weight on **content quality, app design** and what screens crowd out (sleep, play, family time) than on counting hours alone, especially for older children and teens. The AAP's "5 Cs" framework sums this up: think about your **child**, the **content**, how they **calm** down, what media is **crowding out**, and keep **communicating**.

## Screen time for children under 2

The first years are a period of rapid brain and language growth, and babies learn mainly through live interaction: talking, eye contact, back-and-forth play. That's why experts are cautious about screens at this age:

- **Language:** Many studies have linked heavy early screen use with delays in some language skills. A likely reason is that screen time replaces conversation with parents.
- **Attention and self-soothing:** When a screen becomes the main way to calm a child, they may get less practice calming themselves. Heavy use is associated with attention difficulties in some children, though that doesn't prove screens alone are the cause.
- **Less movement:** WHO recommends that 3–4-year-olds get **at least 180 minutes of varied physical activity a day**. Hours on a screen usually come out of active play.

The same applies to tablets. An iPad isn't inherently worse than a TV, but it's held close to the face, goes everywhere, and its apps are often designed to keep children watching.

## Effects of screens on kids' eyes

Long stretches of close-up screen use can cause **digital eye strain**: dry, tired eyes and headaches, partly because we blink less when we focus. Researchers also link lots of near work and too little time outdoors with **myopia (nearsightedness)**, which is becoming more common in children. Studies suggest outdoor play and daylight may help protect children's eyes.

To ease strain, eye doctors, including the American Academy of Ophthalmology, recommend the **20-20-20 rule**: every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet (6 meters) away for 20 seconds. If your child holds things very close or often complains of headaches, have their eyes checked.

> **Did you know?** The AAP makes an exception for **video chatting** with grandparents and relatives, even for babies. It's a live, two-way interaction where someone responds to the child, not passive watching.

## Screens and sleep

Screen use near bedtime is linked to falling asleep later and sleeping less. Exciting content keeps the brain alert, and screen light is thought to affect the sleep hormone melatonin. That's why many health bodies, including official UK guidance from 2019, advise **avoiding screens for about an hour before bed** and keeping devices out of the bedroom. ## Practical tips for parents

1. **Make a family media plan:** agree together on when, where and what. The AAP offers a free template.
2. **Screen-free zones:** bedrooms and the dinner table are good places to start.
3. **Watch together:** ask questions and connect what they see to real life.
4. **Choose content carefully:** slower-paced, educational shows with no ads and no autoplay.
5. **Use parental controls** built into devices to set time limits and approve apps.
6. **Don't make screens the only calming tool:** help your child name feelings and settle down. See: [How to handle toddler tantrums](/en/articles/how-to-deal-with-child-tantrums/)
7. **Model it:** children copy what they see, so cut back on your own phone use around them.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much screen time should a 2-year-old have?

WHO says no more than 1 hour a day for 2-year-olds, and less is better. The AAP similarly suggests limiting it to high-quality content, ideally watched with a parent.

### Is any screen time OK for babies under 2?

The AAP recommends avoiding screens before about 18 months except video chatting, and WHO recommends no sedentary screen time before age 2.

### Is screen time bad for kids' eyes?

Long close-up use can cause eye strain, and heavy near work combined with little time outdoors is associated with a higher risk of nearsightedness.

### How long before bed should kids stop screens?

Many experts suggest switching screens off about an hour before bedtime and keeping devices out of the bedroom overnight.

## Key takeaway

The main concern with **screen time for kids** is what it replaces: sleep, movement and time with you. Avoid screens before age 2 except video chats, keep it to about an hour of quality content a day for ages 2–4, and use a family media plan for older children. Keep devices out of bedrooms and meals, and encourage outdoor play.
