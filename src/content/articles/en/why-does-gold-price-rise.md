Why does the price of gold rise so sharply some weeks and slip back the next? Ask that at a family dinner and you'll hear plenty of theories, but the real answer comes down to a handful of forces you can learn in one sitting. "Gold price today" is one of the most searched phrases across the Middle East every single day, and the number on the jeweller's board makes far more sense once you know who sets it and what pushes it around. What follows is an explanation, not a forecast or investment advice.

## How is the gold price determined worldwide?

Nobody sits in an office and decides what gold costs. The price comes from **supply and demand** in global markets that trade almost around the clock, and three of them matter most.

London is the world's largest market for physical gold. Twice a day, at 10:30 and 15:00 London time, it publishes the LBMA Gold Price, a benchmark set through an electronic auction between accredited banks. ICE Benchmark Administration has run that auction since 2015. In New York, the COMEX exchange trades gold futures, which are contracts to buy or sell gold at a later date for a price fixed now. And the Shanghai Gold Exchange carries real weight because China is one of the world's biggest gold consumers.

Prices are quoted in US dollars per ounce. The ounce in question is a troy ounce, about 31.1 grams.

> **Did you know?** The ounce in gold news isn't the ounce in your kitchen. A regular ounce is about 28.35 grams, while a troy ounce of gold is about 31.1 grams.

## Why does the price of gold rise? Six main reasons

**A weaker US dollar** is the first thing traders check. Since gold is priced in dollars, a softer dollar makes it cheaper for anyone paying in euros, pounds or Egyptian pounds. More buyers step in and the price climbs. The reverse usually holds as well.

Interest rates come next. Gold just sits there; it pays no interest and no dividends. If a savings account or a bond pays well, holding gold means giving that income up. When rates fall, that sacrifice shrinks and investors are happier to own gold. When real interest rates (rates after inflation) climb, gold tends to lose some of its shine.

Inflation pushes in the same direction. When everyday prices rise and money buys less, many people turn to gold to protect what they have. Our guide to [what inflation is](/en/articles/what-is-inflation/) explains the mechanics.

Central banks are big buyers too, because they keep gold in their reserves. According to the World Gold Council, they bought more than 1,000 tonnes in 2022, a record, and kept buying at high levels in the years that followed. Demand on that scale props up the price.

Then there is fear. During economic crises and periods of global tension, gold is treated as a **"safe haven"**, and demand rises.

Finally, supply barely budges. Mine output changes little from year to year and can't be ramped up quickly, however high the price goes. So most of the movement you see comes from shifts in demand, not supply.

## Why does the price of gold fall?

Run the same forces backwards: a stronger dollar, higher interest rates, calmer markets that pull investors back into stocks, or traders selling to lock in profits after a big run-up. Because these factors shift constantly, gold moves up and down every day, and nobody can know its next direction for certain.

That's also the honest answer to "if gold is rising, will it keep rising?" Not necessarily. Past performance doesn't guarantee the future, and gold has been through long periods of decline. This is education, not a recommendation to buy or sell.

## How is the price of a gram calculated locally?

The price per gram in a local shop is built on the global price, roughly in four steps:

| Step | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Price per ounce in USD ÷ 31.1 = price of one gram of pure (24-karat) gold in USD |
| 2 | Multiply by the local exchange rate against the dollar |
| 3 | Multiply by the karat's purity: 21K = 21/24, 18K = 18/24 |
| 4 | Add the **making charge**, the dealer's margin and any local taxes or fees |

This explains why gold can cost different amounts in two countries on the same day, and why it sometimes looks more expensive locally than the world price suggests. Exchange rates, local demand, making charges and taxes all feed in. If the local currency weakens, the price per gram rises even when the global price stays flat. For more on karats, read [18K vs 21K vs 24K gold](/en/articles/gold-karats-difference/); for the currency side, see [how exchange rates are determined](/en/articles/how-are-exchange-rates-determined/).

## Reading the headlines: "gold is going up"

When a headline says gold is climbing, the cause is usually a mix of the factors above: expectations of interest-rate cuts, a weaker dollar, central bank buying and nervous markets. Their weight changes over time. One month the dollar may be doing most of the work; another month it's central banks.

So who sets the price of gold? No single person or country. Global supply and demand do, with the key benchmark published in London twice a day. Your local exchange rate, the karat and the making charge then turn that figure into the price on the shop window.
