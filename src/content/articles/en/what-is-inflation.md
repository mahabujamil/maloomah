A loaf of bread, a carton of milk, a bus ticket: ask an older relative what any of these cost ten years ago and you'll probably hear a much smaller number than today's. That gap has a name. It's called **inflation**, and once you understand how it works, you'll see why the same pocket money buys less each year and how to make better choices with your own savings.

## What is inflation?

**Inflation is a general, sustained rise in the prices of goods and services over time.** Flip it around and it means the same thing as a fall in the purchasing power of money: a banknote buys a little less each year than it did the year before.

Two words in that definition do most of the work. "General" means it isn't about one product. If tomatoes get expensive for a few weeks because of a bad harvest, that's a price spike, not inflation. "Sustained" means it keeps going. Inflation is when food, rent, transport and clothes all creep up together, month after month.

## How is inflation measured?

Nobody can track the price of every single thing sold in a country, so statisticians use a shortcut called the **consumer price index (CPI)**. They build a "basket" of what a typical household buys, including food, transport, housing, clothing and education, and price that basket again at regular intervals.

The maths is simple. If the basket cost 100 last year and 104 this year, annual inflation is 4%. When the news reports that "inflation hit 4%", that's the figure they mean.

## Why do prices rise? The main causes of inflation

Economists usually point to four causes, and in real life several often happen at once.

The first is rising demand. Picture the school canteen on a hot day when everyone wants the same cold drink and there are only a few crates left. If the seller could set any price, it would go up. Across a whole economy, when people want to buy more than is available, prices rise.

The second is higher production costs. When energy, raw materials or shipping get more expensive, factories rarely absorb the extra cost themselves. It ends up in the price you pay.

The third is more money in circulation. If the money supply grows faster than the goods and services it can buy, there's more money chasing the same stuff, and each unit of money is worth a bit less.

The fourth is expectations, and it's partly psychological. If people and businesses expect prices to rise, workers push for higher wages and companies raise prices in advance. Everyone acts on the forecast, and the forecast comes true.

## How does inflation affect your money?

The clearest way to see inflation is to watch what happens to cash that just sits there. Here is the value of 1,000 kept in a drawer for 10 years:

| Annual inflation | Purchasing power after 10 years |
| --- | --- |
| 3% | about 744 |
| 5% | about 614 |
| 10% | about 386 |

The notes still say 1,000. What they can buy has shrunk, and at high inflation it falls to well under half.

> **Did you know?** There's a quick shortcut called the rule of 70: divide 70 by the inflation rate to estimate how many years it takes for prices to double. At 7% inflation, prices double in about 10 years.

### Who loses and who gains?

The people hit hardest are those who keep their savings in cash for long periods, and those on fixed incomes that don't rise with prices, such as a pension that stays the same every year. On the other side, someone with a fixed-rate loan may come out ahead, because they repay it with money that has lost some of its value since they borrowed.

## Types of inflation

Not all inflation is a problem. Moderate inflation, a slow and steady rise, is what most central banks consider normal in a growing economy. High inflation is when the rate speeds up and people feel it in almost every purchase.

At the extreme is hyperinflation. Germany went through it in 1923 and Zimbabwe in 2008, when prices changed within a single day and the currency almost stopped meaning anything.

Then there's deflation, the opposite: a general fall in prices. That sounds like good news, but when people expect things to get cheaper they put off buying, sales drop and the whole economy can slow down.

## What do central banks do about inflation?

Major central banks, such as the US Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank, aim for inflation close to 2% a year. Notice that the target isn't zero. Their main tool is the **interest rate**.

Raising rates makes borrowing more expensive. Families postpone buying a car or a home, businesses hold off on expanding, spending slows and price growth cools. When the economy is weak, they do the reverse and cut rates to encourage borrowing and spending.

In Jordan, the dinar has been pegged to the US dollar since 1995, so the Central Bank of Jordan closely follows US interest-rate decisions.

## Practical ways to protect your money from inflation

You can't stop inflation on your own, but you can soften its effect on your household:

1. Make a monthly budget and track where your money goes. You can't control what you don't measure.
2. Keep an emergency fund covering a few months of expenses, but don't leave large sums sitting idle in cash for years.
3. Learn the basics of long-term investing and diversification, and talk to a trustworthy professional before any decision.
4. Negotiate your salary so it keeps up with the cost of living.
5. Shop smart: compare prices and stock up on non-perishables during sales.
6. Be careful with variable-rate debt, since payments can rise when interest rates go up.

## Two questions students often ask about inflation

### Is inflation always bad?

No. Moderate, stable inflation is a normal part of a healthy economy, which is exactly why central banks aim for around 2% rather than zero. The real trouble is high, unexpected inflation, because it makes planning hard and quietly eats into savings.

### Why don't countries just print more money?

Because money on its own doesn't produce bread or medicine. Print a lot of it without producing more goods, and you get more money chasing the same things. Prices jump, and in the worst cases the result is hyperinflation that wipes out a currency's value, as in the two examples above.
