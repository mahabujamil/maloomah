Every year, as 18 December comes round, Arabic teachers write a familiar title on the board: "Write an **essay on the Arabic language**." That date is World Arabic Language Day, and schools mark it with assemblies, competitions and writing tasks. The topic looks easy because students use Arabic every day, yet that is exactly what makes it hard. Writing well about something familiar means seeing it with fresh eyes. Below is an outline, a full model essay, a short version for younger pupils, and some advice on making the essay your own.

## Outline for an essay on the Arabic language

Plan before you write. An outline keeps you from repeating yourself and shows the marker a clear structure:

| Part | Main idea |
| --- | --- |
| Introduction | Language carries thought; Arabic is the "language of the dad" |
| Point one | Its status: the language of the Quran and of a long civilisation |
| Point two | Why Arabic matters to individuals and to the Arab world |
| Point three | The beauty and features of Arabic |
| Point four | What threatens Arabic today |
| Point five | How to protect the Arabic language |
| Conclusion | Arabic is a trust in our hands |

## Introduction

A language is more than words passed back and forth. It is the container for our thoughts, our feelings and our history, and it tells us who we are and where we belong. Arabic, often called the language of the *dad* after one of its distinctive letters, is among the oldest and richest languages in the world. It carried the Quran, it was the language of medicine, astronomy and mathematics for centuries, and it is still spoken by hundreds of millions of people today. What gives it this standing, and how can we keep it strong?

## Body: the status and importance of Arabic

### Its status

Arabic is the language of the Quran. God says: "Indeed, We have sent it down as an Arabic Qur'an that you might understand" (Surah Yusuf, 12:2, Saheeh International translation). Because of this, Arabic is tied to the daily lives of Muslims everywhere. They recite the Quran and pray in Arabic even when they speak another language at home.

It was never only a language of faith. During the golden age of Arab and Islamic civilisation, scholars wrote in Arabic on medicine, astronomy, mathematics and philosophy, and many of their books were later translated into other languages. Al-Khwarizmi, whose work gave algebra its early shape, wrote in Arabic, and so did Ibn Sina, author of *The Canon of Medicine*.

The modern world recognised this too. On 18 December 1973, the United Nations General Assembly added Arabic to its official languages, making it one of six. That is why the date was chosen for World Arabic Language Day.

### Why it matters

Arabic is the thread that connects Arabs across a huge area. A student in Morocco can read a book written in Iraq, and a student in Egypt can follow a poem by a Palestinian poet, because Modern Standard Arabic is shared by all of them however different their local dialects are.

It is also the door to a vast heritage. Someone who reads Arabic well can open the poetry of al-Mutanabbi or Ibn Khaldun's *Muqaddimah* without a translator. Someone who neglects it slowly loses touch with that inheritance.

There is a practical side as well. Understanding starts with language, so a student who is confident in Arabic tends to follow lessons in other subjects more easily, and someone who expresses ideas clearly earns the listener's trust.

### Its beauty

Arabic has an enormous vocabulary, often with several words for the same thing. It also works through roots. From the three letters *k-t-b* come *kataba* (he wrote), *katib* (writer), *maktub* (written), *kitab* (book), *maktaba* (library) and *maktab* (desk or office). Once you know the root, new words carry their meaning inside them.

The alphabet has 28 letters and is written from right to left. Its shapes gave rise to a whole art form, Arabic calligraphy, with styles such as Naskh, Thuluth, Diwani and Ruq'ah.

### What threatens it

For all its standing, Arabic faces real pressure. Many young people text in Latin letters and numbers instead of Arabic script, and drop foreign words into speech when an Arabic word would do. Some shops put up signs only in foreign languages. Some students let grammar and spelling slide until mistakes become habits.

Learning foreign languages is valuable and should be encouraged. The problem starts when it comes at the cost of one's own language.

### How to protect it

Protecting Arabic starts with small daily habits:

1. Read something in Arabic every day, even a few pages of a story.
2. Write messages in Arabic script and check them before sending.
3. Use Modern Standard Arabic in class, in assemblies and in competitions.
4. Memorise some Quran and some Arabic poetry; both strengthen the tongue.
5. Choose the Arabic word whenever one exists.

Schools, families and the media share the job of making Arabic a language people enjoy rather than a chore, through stories, competitions and good programmes.

## Conclusion

Arabic is not just a school subject whose rules we memorise for an exam. It is our identity, our history and the language of the Quran. Earlier generations carried it to us across many centuries and preserved it in their books and poems; now it is our turn to pass it on. So let us read it, write it and be proud of it, because whoever keeps their language keeps a part of themselves.

> **Did you know?** *Kitab al-Ayn*, compiled by al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi, is one of the earliest Arabic dictionaries. He ordered the words by where each letter is pronounced in the mouth, from the throat to the lips, rather than alphabetically, which is why the book starts with the letter *ayn*.

## A short essay for younger pupils

Arabic is my favourite language. I speak it with my family and friends, and I read the Quran in it, because it is the language of the Quran. It has 28 letters and is written from right to left. It has the letter *dad*, so people call it the language of the *dad*. I will look after my language by reading Arabic stories, writing neatly and speaking Standard Arabic at school. I love Arabic and I am proud of it.

## Tips for writing a strong essay

Don't copy the model word for word. Take the plan and the ideas, then add your own. You might open with something you actually noticed, such as a shop sign in your street written in Latin letters, or a classmate mixing foreign words into every sentence. A real observation makes your essay stand out from the rest of the class.

Quote a verse or a line of poetry if you like, but check the exact wording and say where it comes from. A misquoted line hurts an essay more than it helps. A well-known line by the poet Hafez Ibrahim, written in the voice of Arabic itself, says: "I am the sea, with pearls hidden in its depths; did they ask the diver about my shells?"
