Reach round and run your fingers down the middle of your back, from your neck downwards. Those small bumps under the skin are the bones of your **backbone**, or spine. Scientists use that single feature to split the animal kingdom into two big groups: **vertebrates**, which have a backbone like us, and **invertebrates**, which have none at all. The split is far from even. Invertebrates vastly outnumber vertebrates, and most of the animals you meet on an ordinary day, from the ant on the pavement to the snail in the garden, have no backbone whatsoever.

## What is a backbone, and why does it matter?

A backbone is a column of small bones called vertebrae, stacked one on top of another along the back. It does two main jobs. It holds the body up and gives it shape, much like the main pillar of a building. It also protects the spinal cord, the bundle of nerves that carries messages between the brain and the rest of the body.

The column isn't one rigid piece. The vertebrae are linked by small joints, which is why you can bend down to tie your shoes and then stand straight again. The backbone is also part of an internal skeleton that grows along with the animal, a detail that becomes important when we get to invertebrates.

## The five groups of vertebrates

Vertebrates are animals with a backbone and an internal skeleton. At school they are usually divided into five groups:

| Group | How it breathes | Body covering | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fish | Gills | Scales | Sardine, shark, salmon |
| Amphibians | Gills when young, then mostly lungs and skin | Moist, smooth skin | Frog, salamander |
| Reptiles | Lungs | Dry scales | Lizard, snake, crocodile, tortoise |
| Birds | Lungs | Feathers | Pigeon, eagle, ostrich |
| Mammals | Lungs | Hair or fur | Human, cat, camel, whale |

Each group has a trademark. Amphibians start life in water and many move onto land later, the way a tadpole turns into a frog. Birds are the only animals covered in feathers. Mammals feed their young on milk, and some of them live in the sea, such as whales and dolphins. That is why a whale is not a fish, even though it swims.

Older students also meet a second way of grouping them. Birds and mammals keep their body temperature roughly constant whatever the weather, so they are called warm-blooded (endotherms). Fish, amphibians and reptiles take on the temperature of their surroundings, so they are cold-blooded (ectotherms). It's the reason a lizard basks in the morning sun before it gets moving.

## Invertebrates: most of the animal kingdom

Invertebrates are animals without a backbone. Despite the name sounding like a leftover category, they make up the overwhelming majority of known animal species: estimates put them at around 95% or more. The best-known groups are:

- Sponges: some of the simplest animals, fixed in place on the sea floor, with bodies full of holes that water flows through.
- Cnidarians: jellyfish, corals and sea anemones, with tentacles armed with stinging cells.
- Worms: the familiar earthworm is one, burrowing through soil and letting air in, which helps farmers.
- Molluscs: soft-bodied animals, many protected by a shell, like snails and mussels, while others, like the octopus and squid, have no outer shell.
- Arthropods: the largest animal group by far, including insects, spiders, crustaceans such as crabs and shrimp, and centipedes. Their legs are divided into jointed sections, which is where the name comes from.
- Echinoderms: sea creatures such as starfish and sea urchins, with rough or spiny skin.

Many invertebrates have no internal skeleton, so they protect themselves in other ways. Arthropods wear a hard outer shell called an exoskeleton, like a suit of armour. The catch is that armour doesn't grow, so the animal has to shed it and form a new, larger one. This is called moulting. If you have ever found the empty, see-through skin of a cicada or a grasshopper clinging to a twig, you have seen what was left behind.

> **Did you know?** An octopus has three hearts and blue blood. Two hearts pump blood through the gills and the third sends it round the rest of the body. The blue colour comes from a copper-based protein that carries oxygen, instead of the iron-based one that makes our blood red.

## Vertebrates and invertebrates: the difference in one table

These are the differences that come up most often in tests:

| Feature | Vertebrates | Invertebrates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Backbone | Yes | No |
| Skeleton | Internal, made of bone or cartilage | None, an exoskeleton, or a shell |
| Number of species | Relatively few: tens of thousands | The vast majority: well over a million known |
| Size | Includes the largest animals on Earth | Mostly small |
| Examples | Humans, birds, fish, frogs | Insects, earthworms, snails, jellyfish |

Most invertebrates are small, but there are striking exceptions, such as the giant squid and the colossal squid, deep-ocean animals that grow several metres long. Even so, the largest animal that has ever lived is a vertebrate; you can read about it in [the largest animal in the world](/en/articles/largest-animal-in-the-world/).

## How to classify any animal yourself

When a teacher asks you to classify an animal, work through these questions in order:

1. Does it have a backbone? If yes, it's a vertebrate. If not, it's an invertebrate.
2. If it's a vertebrate, what covers its body? Feathers mean a bird, hair means a mammal, scales plus gills mean a fish, dry scales mean a reptile, and moist smooth skin usually means an amphibian.
3. If it's an invertebrate, does it have jointed legs? Then it's an arthropod. Is it soft-bodied, often with a shell? Then it's a mollusc.

Try it on a bat. It has a backbone, it's covered in fur and it feeds its young on milk, so it's a mammal, even though it flies. A penguin has feathers, so it's a bird, even though it can't fly. The lesson is that classification depends on body features, not on how an animal moves or where it lives. ## Questions students ask about vertebrates and invertebrates

### Is a snake a vertebrate?

Yes. Snakes are reptiles with a very long backbone made of a large number of vertebrae, which is what makes them so flexible.

### Is a jellyfish a fish?

No, despite the name. A jellyfish is an invertebrate, a cnidarian, with no backbone, no gills and no bones at all.

### Are humans vertebrates?

Yes. Humans are mammals, and mammals are vertebrates. A baby is born with 33 vertebrae; several of them fuse at the base of the spine during growth, which is why adults are usually said to have 26.
