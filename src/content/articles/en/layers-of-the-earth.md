The deepest hole people have ever drilled goes down about 12 kilometres. The centre of the planet is more than 6,000 kilometres away. So everything we know about the **layers of the Earth** comes from clever detective work rather than direct observation. In order, from the outside in, those layers are the crust, the mantle, the outer core and the inner core. We live on the thin crust; below it lies a hot, heavy world nobody has visited. Here is what each layer is like, and how scientists figured it out.

## The layers of the Earth in order: one table

The Earth's radius is about 6,371 kilometres. The table below sums up the four main layers. Treat the numbers as approximate, since they are estimated indirectly:

| Layer | Approx. depth below the surface | State | Main ingredients |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Crust | Surface to 5–70 km | Solid | Rocks rich in silicon, oxygen and aluminium |
| Mantle | Down to about 2,900 km | Solid, but flows very slowly | Rocks rich in iron and magnesium |
| Outer core | 2,900 to about 5,150 km | Liquid | Molten iron and nickel |
| Inner core | 5,150 km to the centre (6,371 km) | Solid | Iron and nickel |

If you need a memory trick for a test, think of a hard-boiled egg. The thin shell is the crust, the white is the mantle and the yolk is the core.

## The crust: where we live

The crust is the solid outer skin of the planet, with its mountains, plains and ocean floors. It is the thinnest layer by far, less than 1% of the Earth's volume. Compared with the whole planet, it is thinner than the skin on an apple.

It comes in two kinds. **Oceanic crust** sits under the seas. It is relatively thin, about 5 to 10 km, and made mostly of dark, heavy basalt. **Continental crust** carries the land. It is much thicker, about 30 to 70 km, reaching its greatest thickness under big mountain ranges, and it is made mostly of lighter rock such as granite. Because that rock is lighter, the continents ride higher than the ocean floor.

The crust is also where groundwater lives. When rain falls, some of it soaks down between the grains of porous rock and collects in layers called aquifers, which is what a well taps into. To see where that rain comes from in the first place, read [how rain forms](/en/articles/how-does-rain-form/).

## The mantle: the biggest layer

Just beneath the crust, the mantle reaches down to about 2,900 km. That makes it the largest layer, taking up roughly 84% of the Earth's volume. The boundary between crust and mantle is called the Mohorovičić discontinuity, or simply the "Moho", after the Croatian scientist Andrija Mohorovičić, who found it in 1909 by studying earthquake waves.

A common mistake is to picture the mantle as an ocean of lava. In fact it is mostly solid rock. It is so hot, though, that the rock creeps very slowly, a bit like thick putty, perhaps only a few centimetres a year. That is roughly how fast your fingernails grow, and over millions of years it is enough to carry whole continents around.

### Lithosphere and asthenosphere

In middle and high school you will meet a second way of slicing up the Earth. Instead of asking what the rock is made of, it asks how stiff the rock is.

The top slice is the **lithosphere**: the crust plus the rigid uppermost part of the mantle, about 100 km thick on average. It isn't one solid shell. It is cracked into giant pieces called tectonic plates. Underneath lies the **asthenosphere**, where the rock is soft enough to flow slowly, so the plates can slide across it. Most earthquakes and volcanoes happen along plate edges, and where plates crash together they push up huge ranges, including the [highest mountain in the world](/en/articles/highest-mountain-in-the-world/).

## The outer core: the only liquid layer

At about 2,900 km down, rock gives way to metal. The outer core is about 2,200 km thick and is the only liquid layer of the Earth, made mainly of molten iron and nickel.

This layer matters to you more than you might think. As the liquid iron swirls, it generates the Earth's magnetic field. That field is why a compass needle points north, and it also works as an invisible shield, deflecting much of the stream of charged particles that pours out of the Sun.

## The inner core: the centre of the Earth

At the very middle sits a solid ball with a radius of about 1,220 km, roughly 70% of the Moon's radius. Its temperature is estimated at about 5,000 to 6,000 °C, close to the temperature of the Sun's surface.

So why doesn't it melt? Because of **enormous pressure**. The weight of every layer above squeezes the iron atoms so tightly that they can't break loose into a liquid. The Danish seismologist Inge Lehmann discovered the inner core in 1936.

## How do scientists know the layers of the Earth?

The clues come from earthquakes. When one strikes, seismic waves travel right through the planet and are picked up by seismographs at stations all over the world. The waves change speed and bend whenever they pass from one material into another, so each recording carries information about what the wave went through.

Two kinds of wave are especially useful. Primary (P) waves are fast and can pass through both solids and liquids. Secondary (S) waves are slower and cannot travel through liquid at all. Scientists noticed that S waves disappear on the side of the Earth opposite an earthquake, which told them something liquid inside was blocking them: the outer core. Later, Inge Lehmann spotted P waves bouncing off a boundary deep inside the core, and concluded that the very centre must be a solid inner core.

## Questions students ask about the Earth's layers

### Which layer is the thickest, and which is the thinnest?

The mantle is the thickest, reaching down about 2,900 km. The crust is the thinnest, no more than about 70 km even at its thickest points.

### Which layer of the Earth contains water?

The water we know is in the crust: on its surface in seas and rivers, and below the surface as groundwater held in the pores and cracks of rocks.

### How many layers does the Earth have?

Scientists usually describe four main layers based on what they are made of. Look closer and you can count more, such as the upper and lower mantle, or the lithosphere and asthenosphere.

### Why is the outer core liquid while the inner core is solid?

Both are extremely hot, but the pressure at the centre is far greater. That pressure keeps the inner core's iron solid, while the lower pressure in the outer core lets the metal stay liquid, and its movement keeps our magnetic field running.
