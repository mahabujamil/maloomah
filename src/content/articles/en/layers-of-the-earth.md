The **layers of the Earth** in order, from the outside in, are the **crust**, the **mantle**, the **outer core** and the **inner core**. We live on the thin crust, while beneath us lies a hot, enormous world no person has ever reached. This simple lesson walks through each layer — its thickness, what it is made of and how hot it is — and explains how scientists worked out what lies deep inside the Earth without ever digging that far.

## The layers of the Earth in order: one table

The Earth's radius is about **6,371 kilometres**. This table sums up its four main layers. The numbers are approximate, because scientists estimate them indirectly:

| Layer | Approx. depth below the surface | State | Main ingredients |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Crust | Surface to 5–70 km | Solid | Rocks rich in silicon, oxygen and aluminium |
| Mantle | Down to about 2,900 km | Solid, but flows very slowly | Rocks rich in iron and magnesium |
| Outer core | 2,900 to about 5,150 km | Liquid | Molten iron and nickel |
| Inner core | 5,150 km to the centre (6,371 km) | Solid | Iron and nickel |

An easy way to remember it: picture a **hard-boiled egg**. The thin shell is the crust, the white is the mantle and the yolk is the core.

## Layer 1: the crust

The crust is the solid outer layer we live on, with its mountains, plains and ocean floors. It is the **thinnest layer**, making up less than 1% of the planet's volume. There are two kinds:

- **Oceanic crust** lies under the oceans. It is relatively thin (about 5 to 10 km) and made mostly of dark, heavy **basalt**.
- **Continental crust** forms the continents. It is much thicker (about 30 to 70 km, thickest under great mountain ranges) and made mostly of lighter rocks such as **granite**.

The crust also holds the groundwater we pump from wells. Rainwater seeps between the grains of porous rock and collects in layers called **aquifers**. To see where that rain comes from, read [how rain forms](/en/articles/how-does-rain-form/).

## Layer 2: the mantle

The mantle lies just beneath the crust and reaches down to about **2,900 km**, making it the **largest layer**, at roughly **84%** of the Earth's volume. The boundary between the crust and the mantle is called the **Mohorovičić discontinuity** ("Moho" for short), after Croatian scientist **Andrija Mohorovičić**, who discovered it in **1909** by studying earthquake waves.

The mantle is mostly solid rock, but it is extremely hot, so its rock creeps very slowly, like thick putty, perhaps only a few centimetres a year. That slow movement is what carries the continents along on top of it.

### Lithosphere and asthenosphere

When students in middle and high school study the layers in more detail, they meet a second way of dividing them, based on how rigid the rock is:

- **Lithosphere:** the crust plus the rigid top part of the mantle, about 100 km thick on average. It is broken into giant pieces called **tectonic plates**.
- **Asthenosphere:** the layer below the lithosphere, where the rock is soft enough to flow slowly so the plates can slide over it. Most earthquakes and volcanoes happen at plate edges, and colliding plates push up great mountains such as the [highest mountain in the world](/en/articles/highest-mountain-in-the-world/).

## Layer 3: the outer core

The outer core begins at about 2,900 km down and is about 2,200 km thick. It is the **only liquid layer** of the Earth, made mainly of **molten iron and nickel**.

Its most important job: the swirling liquid iron generates the **Earth's magnetic field**. That field makes a compass needle point north, and it acts as an invisible shield that deflects much of the stream of charged particles coming from the Sun.

## Layer 4: the inner core

The inner core is the **centre of the Earth**: a solid ball with a radius of about **1,220 km**, roughly 70% of the Moon's radius. Its temperature is estimated at about **5,000 to 6,000 °C**, close to the temperature of the Sun's surface.

How can it be solid at that heat? The answer is **enormous pressure** from the weight of all the layers above, which squeezes the iron atoms so hard they cannot melt. Danish seismologist **Inge Lehmann** discovered the inner core in **1936**.

> **Did you know?** The deepest hole ever drilled is the **Kola Superdeep Borehole** in Russia. It reached about **12,262 metres** in 1989, only about 12 km, which is not even enough to get through the continental crust there! ## How do scientists know the layers of the Earth?

Nobody has seen the Earth's interior, so how do we know its layers? The answer is **seismic waves**. When an earthquake strikes, waves spread through the planet and are recorded by **seismographs** at stations all over the world. The waves change speed and direction when they pass from one material into another.

1. **Primary (P) waves** are fast and travel through both solids and liquids.
2. **Secondary (S) waves** are slower and cannot travel through liquids at all.
3. Scientists noticed that S waves vanish on the far side of the Earth from an earthquake, so they concluded there must be a **liquid layer** inside that blocks them: the outer core.
4. Inge Lehmann then noticed that some P waves bounced off a boundary deep inside the core, and concluded there is a **solid inner core**.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the layers of the Earth in order from outside to inside?

The crust, then the mantle, then the liquid outer core, and finally the solid inner core at the centre.

### Which is the thickest layer of the Earth, and which is the thinnest?

The mantle is the thickest, reaching down about 2,900 km. The crust is the thinnest, no more than about 70 km even at its thickest points.

### Which layer of the Earth contains water?

The water we know is found in the **crust**: on its surface in seas and rivers, and below the surface as groundwater held in the pores and cracks of rocks.

### How many layers does the Earth have?

Scientists usually describe **four main layers** based on composition. In more detail you can count more, such as the upper and lower mantle, or the lithosphere and asthenosphere.

### Why is the outer core liquid while the inner core is solid?

Both are extremely hot, but the pressure at the centre is far greater. It stops the iron in the inner core from melting, while the lower pressure in the outer core lets the metal stay liquid.

## Key takeaway

The **layers of the Earth** are four: a thin crust we live on, a huge mantle whose rock flows slowly, a liquid outer core that creates the magnetic field, and a super-hot solid inner core at the centre. Scientists learned all this by studying earthquake waves, not by digging. For a quick review, remember the boiled egg: shell, white, yolk.
