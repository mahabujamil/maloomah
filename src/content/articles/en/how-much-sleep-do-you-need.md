How much sleep do you need? It depends on how old you are. A newborn sleeps most of the day, a primary school child needs 9 hours or more, and most adults need **7 to 9 hours** a night. Those hours aren't wasted, either. Sleep is when the body repairs itself, the brain files away memories and the immune system recharges.

## How much sleep you need at each age

The figures below come from recommendations by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the US National Sleep Foundation. For young children they include naps:

| Age group | Hours of sleep per day |
| --- | --- |
| Newborns (0–3 months) | 14–17 hours |
| Infants (4–12 months) | 12–16 hours |
| Toddlers (1–2 years) | 11–14 hours |
| Preschoolers (3–5 years) | 10–13 hours |
| School-age children (6–12 years) | 9–12 hours |
| Teenagers (13–18 years) | 8–10 hours |
| Adults (18–64 years) | 7–9 hours |
| Older adults (65+ years) | 7–8 hours |

The need drops as we grow up, but it levels off. For adults, the number to remember is **at least 7 hours** on most nights. Teenagers are the group most often short: a 13-year-old who stays up until midnight and gets up at six for school is getting six hours, two or more below what the table recommends.

## What sleep actually does for you

Some of the body's work can only happen while you're asleep. The clearest example is memory. Overnight, the brain moves what you learned during the day into **long-term memory**, which is why a good night's sleep before an exam usually beats revising until 3 a.m.

Sleep also supports the immune system, so you fight off infections better when you're rested. It keeps hormones in balance too, including the ones that control appetite. That explains why a short night often leaves you hungrier the next day and craving sugary snacks. Over years, chronic short sleep is linked to a higher risk of high blood pressure, diabetes, obesity and depression.

> **Did you know?** Each night you pass through four to six sleep cycles of about 90 minutes each, moving between light sleep, deep sleep and dreaming (REM) sleep.

## Signs you are not sleeping enough

Sleep debt builds up slowly, so it's easy to miss. Watch for these:

- You only wake up with an alarm and find it very hard to get up.
- You sleep much longer on days off to catch up.
- You feel drowsy in meetings, in class or while driving.
- You're irritable, forgetful or find it hard to concentrate.
- You rely on coffee or energy drinks to get through the day.

If several of these sound like you, you're probably short on sleep, even if it feels normal by now.

## Simple habits for deeper sleep

The biggest one is **a fixed bedtime and wake-up time**, weekends included, because your internal clock runs on routine. Morning daylight helps set that clock, so open the curtains when you wake or walk to school if it's close enough.

In the evening, cut back on caffeine after midday, since it can stay in your system for hours, and put screens away 30 to 60 minutes before bed. A dark, quiet, fairly cool bedroom makes a real difference, and so does skipping heavy meals right before sleep. Regular exercise improves sleep as well, as long as it isn't just before bedtime.

If you've been lying awake for about 20 minutes, don't keep tossing and turning. Get up, do something calm in dim light, like reading a paper book, and go back to bed when you feel sleepy. Naps are fine if they're short (20 to 30 minutes) and early in the afternoon, so they don't eat into the night.

## When to see a doctor about sleep

See a doctor if you have trouble sleeping most nights for several weeks, if others notice you snore loudly and stop breathing during sleep, or if you feel very sleepy during the day despite enough hours in bed. These can be signs of treatable sleep disorders such as sleep apnoea.

## Common questions about sleep hours

### Is 6 hours of sleep enough?

For most adults, no. You may feel used to it, but studies show mental performance and health suffer over time. Only a rare few people naturally need less.

### Can you catch up on sleep at the weekend?

Partly. Extra sleep eases tiredness, but it doesn't fully undo a week of short nights. A regular schedule works better than catching up.

### Is too much sleep bad for you?

Regularly sleeping more than 9 hours as an adult can be a sign of a health issue or poor-quality sleep, and it's worth checking if it continues. How well you sleep matters as much as how long.
