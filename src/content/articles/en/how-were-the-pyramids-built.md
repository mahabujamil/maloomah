More than two million stone blocks, most of them heavier than a family car, stacked almost as high as a modern skyscraper, and not a single crane on site. That is the Great Pyramid of Giza, finished about 4,500 years ago. So how were the pyramids built? In short, by **organised human labour**: skilled crews, stone floated down the Nile and dragged on sledges, and ramps whose exact layout archaeologists still argue about. Not every detail is settled, but discoveries over the last few decades have answered a lot of the old questions.

## The Great Pyramid in numbers

The Great Pyramid was built for the pharaoh **Khufu** of the Fourth Dynasty and was most likely completed around 2560 BCE. A few figures show why it still astonishes people:

- Original height: about 146.6 metres. Today it stands at about 138.5 metres, having lost its outer casing and capstone.
- Length of each side of the base: about 230 metres.
- Number of blocks: around 2.3 million.
- Average block weight: about 2.5 tonnes, though some granite blocks in the "King's Chamber" weigh tens of tonnes.

For roughly 3,800 years, nothing built by humans was taller. It is also the only one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World that is still standing.

## Who built the pyramids? Not slaves

Films love the picture of thousands of slaves hauling stone under the whip. The evidence doesn't back it up. In the 1990s, archaeologists uncovered a **workers' village** near the pyramids, along with the workers' own tombs.

What they found there describes organised work crews who were fed bread, beer and meat. They also got medical care. Some skeletons have fractures that healed properly, which only happens if a broken bone is set and the injured person is allowed to rest. And some workers were buried in tombs close to the pyramids, an honour slaves would not normally have received.

As for numbers, many researchers estimate that the workforce at any one time ran into the thousands, possibly up to about 20,000 people. That included permanent craftsmen who knew their trade well and seasonal labourers who joined at certain times of year.

## Where did the stones come from?

Different stone did different jobs, and each type had its own source:

- Limestone for the core: mostly quarried right on the Giza plateau, so it barely had to travel.
- Fine white limestone for the outer casing: brought from the Tura quarries on the other side of the Nile.
- Granite: shipped from Aswan in the south, more than 800 kilometres away.

Moving a granite block 800 kilometres sounds impossible until you remember the river running the length of the country.

## The Diary of Merer: a 4,500-year-old work log

In 2013, an archaeological team at Wadi al-Jarf on the Red Sea coast found the oldest inscribed papyri known so far. Among them is the logbook of an official named **Merer**. It records how his crew carried limestone blocks from Tura to Giza by boat, along the Nile and through canals, late in Khufu's reign.

What makes it so valuable is who wrote it. This isn't a modern scholar's guess; it is a work record kept by someone on the project. It shows directly that the Nile served as the "highway" for building materials, and that the work was run with logs and schedules, much like a large construction project today.

> **Did you know?** A wall painting in the tomb of the Egyptian official Djehutihotep shows a worker pouring water in front of a sledge carrying a colossal statue. Physics experiments in 2014 showed that wetting sand by the right amount greatly reduces friction, making heavy loads far easier to pull.

You can feel the same effect at the beach. Dry sand gives way under your feet, while damp sand near the water is firm, so a sledge slides over it instead of digging in.

## How were the pyramids built? Theories about lifting the stones

Getting stone to the plateau was one problem. Lifting it dozens of metres into the air was another, and this is the part that is still debated. The main ideas are:

1. A straight ramp that grew with the pyramid, although near the top it would have had to be enormously long.
2. Zigzag ramps running up the pyramid's faces.
3. A spiral ramp wrapping around the outside.
4. An internal ramp climbing inside the body of the pyramid, a hypothesis put forward by French architect Jean-Pierre Houdin.

Each has weak points, which is why none has won outright. A find in 2018 added a useful clue, though. At the ancient Hatnub quarry, archaeologists found a steep ramp flanked by staircases and post holes for wooden posts, dating roughly to Khufu's time. It suggests the Egyptians had clever hauling systems for pulling blocks up steep slopes, not only up long, gentle ones.

The most likely answer is that the builders used more than one method, depending on the stage of construction. What works for the wide lower layers doesn't necessarily work near the narrow top.

## Astonishing precision

Take a compass to the Great Pyramid and you'll find its sides line up with the four cardinal directions (north, south, east and west) remarkably closely, off by only a small fraction of a degree. The Egyptians had no magnetic compass, so researchers believe they relied on observing the stars or the Sun. Building a pyramid, in other words, took astronomy and careful planning before the first block was laid.

## The three pyramids of Giza

The Great Pyramid shares the Giza plateau with two other large pyramids, built by kings of the same Fourth Dynasty. Heights are approximate:

| Pyramid | King | Approx. original height | What stands out |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Great Pyramid | Khufu | about 146 m | The largest and oldest of the three |
| Second pyramid | Khafre (Khufu's son) | about 143 m | Looks taller than Khufu's because it stands on higher ground; some of its casing survives near the top |
| Third pyramid | Menkaure | about 65 m | The smallest of the three; parts of its lower casing were granite |

Near Khafre's pyramid sits the **Great Sphinx**, which many archaeologists believe was carved during Khafre's reign. For the wider story of the civilization behind these monuments, read our lesson on [ancient Egyptian civilization and its achievements](/en/articles/ancient-egyptian-civilization/).

## What about alien theories?

You'll find claims online that aliens built the pyramids. There is no scientific evidence for them. The Egyptians, on the other hand, left plenty of evidence of human work: quarries, tools, workers' villages and administrative records like the Diary of Merer. Giving the credit to anyone else takes it away from the ancient Egyptians, and from the thousands of people who cut, moved and raised those stones by hand.

## Questions students ask about the pyramids

### Why did the Egyptians build pyramids?

They were monumental royal tombs, tied to beliefs about the king's passage into the afterlife and his union with the Sun.

### How long did it take to build the Great Pyramid?

Ancient accounts say about 20 years, and many researchers think that is reasonable given the size of the job and the workforce.

### How many pyramids are there in Egypt?

Archaeologists have found more than 100 pyramids in Egypt, varying widely in size and condition. The three at Giza are the largest and best known.
