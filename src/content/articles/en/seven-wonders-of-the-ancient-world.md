Only one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World can still be visited: the Great Pyramid of Giza. The other six were knocked down by earthquakes, burned, or quarried for their stone, and a couple of them have left almost nothing behind. Yet more than two thousand years later their names still turn up in textbooks and quiz nights. So who picked them, and why did the pyramid outlast the rest?

## Who drew up the list?

No king or academy ever made the list official. It started out more like a travel guide. After Alexander the Great's conquests, Greek travellers could move fairly freely between Egypt, the Levant, Mesopotamia and Asia Minor, and some of them wrote about the most impressive things they saw. The best-known lists are credited to writers such as Philo of Byzantium and the poet Antipater of Sidon. Their choices didn't match exactly, but over time the seven names we know today became the standard version.

Seven was a number the Greeks associated with completeness, so the idea was never to catalogue every marvel on Earth. It was to name the very best work human hands had produced. Notice, too, that every wonder sits around the Mediterranean or in the Near East. That was simply the world those writers knew.

## The Seven Wonders of the Ancient World in order

The usual order runs roughly from oldest to newest:

| Wonder | Location today | Built (approx.) | What happened to it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Great Pyramid of Giza | Giza, Egypt | c. 2560 BC | Still standing |
| Hanging Gardens of Babylon | near Hillah, Iraq | 6th century BC (according to tradition) | No confirmed remains |
| Temple of Artemis | Ephesus, Turkey | c. 550 BC | Burned in 356 BC, rebuilt, later destroyed |
| Statue of Zeus | Olympia, Greece | c. 435 BC | Lost in late antiquity |
| Mausoleum at Halicarnassus | Bodrum, Turkey | c. 350 BC | Wrecked by earthquakes, stones reused |
| Colossus of Rhodes | Rhodes, Greece | finished c. 280 BC | Toppled by an earthquake c. 226 BC |
| Lighthouse of Alexandria | Alexandria, Egypt | c. 280 BC | Collapsed after medieval earthquakes |

Two of the seven were in Egypt and one in Iraq, so three of the ancient wonders stood in what is now the Arab world. That one comes up a lot in school quizzes.

## The Great Pyramid, the survivor

The Great Pyramid was built as the tomb of the pharaoh Khufu of the Fourth Dynasty. It originally stood about 146 metres tall. Today it's around eight metres shorter, because its smooth outer casing of white limestone was stripped away over the centuries and reused in other buildings. Historians are still working out exactly how the blocks were moved and lifted; our lesson on [how the pyramids were built](/en/articles/how-were-the-pyramids-built/) goes through the main theories.

Why did it survive when the others fell? The answer is its shape. A pyramid is a solid mountain of stone, wide at the bottom and narrow at the top. It has no thin walls to crack, no wooden roof to burn and no metal legs to snap. Against earthquakes and time, few shapes are more stable.

> **Did you know?** The Great Pyramid is so old that Cleopatra, the last Ptolemaic ruler of Egypt, lived closer in time to the 1969 Moon landing than to the building of the pyramid. She died in 30 BC, around 2,500 years after it was built and about 2,000 years before astronauts walked on the Moon.

## The mystery of the Hanging Gardens

Ancient writers describe terraced gardens in Babylon, watered by machinery that lifted water from the Euphrates. The story usually credits King Nebuchadnezzar II, who supposedly built them for a wife who missed the green hills of her homeland. The trouble is that Babylonian records, which are plentiful, never mention them, and archaeologists have found no clear trace at the site. Some historians think the gardens were exaggerated by writers who never saw them; others suggest they were in a different Mesopotamian city. The honest answer is that nobody knows for sure.

## Four wonders of the Greek world

The **Temple of Artemis** at Ephesus, on Turkey's west coast, was one of the largest temples of its age, ringed by forests of marble columns. It burned in 356 BC, was rebuilt, and was destroyed again in later centuries. A single reassembled column marks the spot today.

At Olympia, home of the ancient Olympic Games, the sculptor Phidias made the **Statue of Zeus**, a seated figure about 12 metres high. Nothing of it survives, so our picture of it comes from written descriptions and images on old coins.

The tomb of King Mausolus at Halicarnassus, modern Bodrum, was so grand and so richly carved that his name became a word. Any large, stately tomb is now called a mausoleum.

The **Colossus of Rhodes** was a bronze statue of the Greek sun god, roughly 33 metres tall, about the height of a ten-storey building. The people of Rhodes built it to celebrate surviving a long siege, and it took around twelve years to finish. It also had the shortest life of any wonder: an earthquake brought it down only about 54 years after completion. The famous image of it straddling the harbour with ships sailing between its legs is almost certainly a later invention; most historians doubt it could have been built that way.

## The Lighthouse of Alexandria

On the island of Pharos, just off Alexandria's harbour, the Ptolemaic kings raised a great lighthouse to guide ships past the rocks. Estimates put its height at over 100 metres, which made it one of the tallest buildings in the world for centuries. A fire burned at the top at night. It stood for more than a thousand years before a series of medieval earthquakes brought it down, and some of its stone was later used in the Citadel of Qaitbay, built on roughly the same spot.

The island lent its name to languages as well. The French word for lighthouse is *phare*, and in Italian and Spanish it's *faro*, both from Pharos.

## How the new seven wonders are different

Students often mix up the two lists. The ancient one was written by Greek authors more than two thousand years ago. The **New Seven Wonders of the World** were announced in 2007 after a global vote run by a private foundation, New7Wonders, in which millions of people voted online and by phone. The winners were the Great Wall of China, Petra in Jordan, Christ the Redeemer in Brazil, Machu Picchu in Peru, Chichen Itza in Mexico, the Colosseum in Rome and the Taj Mahal in India. The Great Pyramid was given honorary status as the last survivor of the ancient list.

## Questions students ask about the seven wonders

### Which ancient wonder is still standing?

The Great Pyramid of Giza in Egypt. It's also the oldest of the seven.

### How many of the ancient wonders were in the Arab world?

Three: the Great Pyramid and the Lighthouse of Alexandria in Egypt, and the Hanging Gardens of Babylon in Iraq, keeping in mind that historians still debate whether the gardens existed.

### Which wonder lasted the shortest time?

The Colossus of Rhodes, which fell in an earthquake roughly half a century after it was finished.

### Is the list of new seven wonders official?

No. It came from a popular vote organised by a private foundation, and UNESCO doesn't treat it as an official list. UNESCO's own World Heritage List is a separate thing, with more than a thousand sites around the world.
