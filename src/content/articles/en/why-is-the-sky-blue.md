Look up on a clear day and you will see blue stretching from horizon to horizon. It seems obvious until you ask: why blue, exactly? And why does the sky turn orange and red at sunset? The answer lies in the journey sunlight takes through our atmosphere.

## Sunlight is not really white

The sunlight we see as white is actually a mix of every colour of the spectrum: red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet. You can see them separated in a rainbow, or when light passes through a glass prism.

Each colour has a different wavelength. Red has a long wavelength (around 700 nanometres), while blue and violet have short ones (roughly 400 to 450 nanometres).

## What happens in the atmosphere?

When sunlight enters the atmosphere, it runs into gas molecules, mostly nitrogen and oxygen. These molecules are far smaller than the wavelength of light, so they **scatter** the light in every direction.

The key point is that this scattering is not equal for all colours. Short wavelengths are scattered much more strongly than long ones. This is called **Rayleigh scattering**, after the British physicist Lord Rayleigh, who described it mathematically in the 1870s.

According to Rayleigh's law, blue light is scattered roughly five to six times more than red light. So whenever you look at any part of the sky away from the Sun, scattered blue light reaches your eyes from every direction, and the whole sky looks blue.

> **Did you know?** Without an atmosphere, the sky would look black even at midday, just as astronauts see it from the surface of the Moon.

## If violet scatters even more, why isn't the sky purple?

Good question! Violet has a shorter wavelength than blue, so it does scatter more. But three things make the sky look blue instead:

1. **The Sun emits less violet light** than blue light to begin with.
2. **Some violet light is absorbed** high in the atmosphere.
3. **Our eyes are more sensitive to blue** than to violet, and the way the colour cells in the retina combine signals makes the final mix look sky blue.

## Why is the sky red at sunset?

At sunrise and sunset the Sun sits low on the horizon, so its light travels through much more atmosphere before it reaches us. Along that long path, most of the blue light is scattered away, and only the longer wavelengths get through: yellow, orange and red.

The more dust, smoke or water vapour in the air, the more intense and red the sunset becomes.

## And why are clouds white?

Water droplets in clouds are much larger than air molecules, so they do not favour any colour. They scatter all wavelengths more or less equally, and when all the colours mix together again, we see white. Dark grey clouds are simply very thick, letting little light through.

## The sky on Mars is different

On Mars, the daytime sky has a yellowish-brown, butterscotch tint because of fine dust in its thin atmosphere. Amazingly, Martian sunsets look bluish, the opposite of what we see on Earth!

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the sky the same shade of blue everywhere?

No. At high, dry places the sky looks deep blue, while in humid or polluted areas it looks paler and closer to white, because larger particles scatter all colours together.

### Is the sea blue because it reflects the sky?

Only partly. Water itself absorbs red light more than blue, which is why deep water looks blue even on a cloudy day.

## Key takeaway

The sky is blue because tiny air molecules scatter short-wavelength blue light far more than other colours, sending it to us from every direction. At sunset, light travels a longer path, the blue is scattered away, and the reds and oranges remain. An everyday sight with beautiful physics behind it.
