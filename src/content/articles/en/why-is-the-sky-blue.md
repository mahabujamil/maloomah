**The sky is blue because air scatters blue sunlight far more than the other colours**, so blue light reaches your eyes from every part of the sky at once. That is the whole answer to "why is the sky blue?" in one sentence, but it hides a lot of interesting physics, and once you understand it, red sunsets, white clouds and the black sky of space all make sense too.

## Sunlight is not really white

Sunlight looks white, but it is a blend of every colour in the spectrum: red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet. A rainbow pulls those colours apart, and so does a glass prism in a school lab.

What separates one colour from another is its wavelength. Red light has long waves; blue and violet have short ones. Keep that difference in mind, because the rest of the explanation depends on it. The values below are approximate (a nanometre is a billionth of a metre):

| Colour | Approx. wavelength | How much air scatters it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Red | about 620–750 nm | Little |
| Orange and yellow | about 570–620 nm | Moderate |
| Green | about 495–570 nm | Moderate |
| Blue | about 450–495 nm | A lot |
| Violet | about 380–450 nm | The most |

## Why is the sky blue? What happens in the atmosphere

Air is mostly nitrogen and oxygen molecules, and they are far smaller than a wavelength of light. When sunlight runs into them, some of it is knocked off course and sent out in all directions. Physicists call this **scattering**.

The molecules don't treat every colour the same way. Short wavelengths get scattered much more strongly than long ones, so blue light is bounced around the sky while red light mostly carries straight on. The effect is called **Rayleigh scattering**, after the British physicist Lord Rayleigh, who worked out the maths in the 1870s.

His law says blue light is scattered roughly five to six times more than red. So when you look at any patch of sky away from the Sun, you are seeing blue light that has been redirected towards you from all around, and the whole dome looks blue.

> **Did you know?** Without an atmosphere, the sky would look black even at midday, just as astronauts see it from the surface of the Moon.

## If violet scatters even more, why isn't the sky purple?

Students who look carefully at the table usually ask this, and it's a fair point. Violet has an even shorter wavelength than blue, so it really is scattered more. Two things tip the balance towards blue. First, the Sun gives off less violet light than blue to begin with, so there's simply less of it to scatter. Second, our eyes are more sensitive to blue than to violet, and the way the colour cells in the retina combine their signals makes the final mix look sky blue.

## Why is the sky red at sunset?

At midday the Sun is high overhead and its light takes a fairly short route through the air. At sunrise and sunset it sits near the horizon, so the light has to cross a much thicker slice of atmosphere to reach you. Over that long path most of the blue gets scattered away, and what survives is the longer wavelengths: yellow, orange and red.

That's also why no two sunsets look quite alike. The more dust, smoke or water vapour in the air, the stronger and redder the colours become.

## And why are clouds white?

Cloud droplets are much bigger than air molecules, and size changes everything here. Large droplets don't pick out any particular colour; they scatter all wavelengths more or less equally, and all the colours mixed back together look white. The dark grey clouds before a storm are simply so thick that little light makes it through.

## A simple home experiment on why the sky is blue

You can make a small sky, and a small sunset, in a glass of water. It suits a science lesson or a science slot in a school assembly, and everything you need is in the kitchen:

1. Fill a clear glass with water.
2. Add a few drops of milk and stir, so the water is only slightly cloudy.
3. Turn off the room light and shine a torch (or your phone's flashlight) through the side of the glass.
4. Look at the glass from the side: the water looks slightly bluish.
5. Now look from the opposite side, with the light shining towards you: it looks yellowish or orange.

The milk particles are playing the part of air molecules. They scatter blue light sideways more than the other colours, which is why the glass looks blue from the side. The light that makes it all the way through has lost some of its blue along the way, so it comes out orange, like a setting Sun.

## The sky on Mars is different

The same physics works on other planets, with different results. On Mars the daytime sky has a yellowish-brown, butterscotch tint because of fine dust in its thin atmosphere. Strangely, Martian sunsets look bluish, the reverse of what we see on Earth.

## Questions students ask about the colour of the sky

### Is the sky the same shade of blue everywhere?

No. High, dry places tend to have a deep blue sky, while humid or polluted areas look paler and closer to white, because larger particles there scatter all colours together, much as cloud droplets do.

### Who discovered why the sky is blue?

Lord Rayleigh explained the effect mathematically in the 1870s, which is why it carries his name. Other scientists had experimented with light scattering before him, including the Irish physicist John Tyndall.

### Why does the sky look black in space?

Space has almost no air, so there are no molecules to scatter sunlight towards your eyes. Astronauts see a blazing Sun against a black sky, even in what would be daytime.

### Is the sea blue because it reflects the sky?

Only partly. Water itself absorbs red light more than blue, which is why deep water looks blue even on a cloudy day.
