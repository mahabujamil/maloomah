The bill arrives, somebody reads the total out loud, and the whole household starts asking who left the air conditioner running all night. If that sounds familiar, here is where to begin: **how to save electricity at home comes down mostly to the appliances that heat or cool things**, not to switching off the odd light. You don't need gadgets, and you don't need to sit in the dark. You need to know where the power goes and change a handful of habits. Below are twelve of them, roughly in order of how much they tend to save.

## Where does your home's electricity go?

You can't cut what you can't see. The split differs from one home to the next and from one country to another, since a flat in Cairo in August and a house in Amman in January have very different needs. The pattern underneath is the same, though: anything that makes heat or cold is expensive.

| Appliance | Why it uses so much |
| --- | --- |
| Air conditioner and electric heater | Run for hours at high power; often the biggest item on the bill in summer and winter |
| Electric water heater | Heating water takes a lot of energy, and many heaters are left on all day |
| Fridge and freezer | Run 24 hours a day, all year |
| Washer, dryer, iron and electric oven | High power, even if used for short periods |
| Lights, screens and chargers | Small individually, but they add up |

That table tells you where to spend your effort. Cooling, heating and hot water first; everything else after.

## How to save electricity with the AC and heating

Start with the thermostat. Set the air conditioner to 24–26 °C in summer. India's Bureau of Energy Efficiency estimates that each degree you raise the setting cuts its electricity use by about 6%. A lot of people drop it to 18 °C hoping the room will cool faster, but it won't. The unit cools at the same rate; it simply runs longer to chase a lower target. Running a fan alongside it makes a higher setting feel cooler, and switching it off when you leave the room is free.

Second, clean the AC filter every two weeks to a month during the heavy-use season. A filter packed with dust is like breathing through a thick scarf: the machine has to strain to push the same amount of air.

Third, keep the heat out in summer and in during winter. Close doors and windows, draw heavy curtains on sunny days, and seal the gaps around doors and window frames when it turns cold. The less outside air leaks in, the less the AC or heater has to do. In winter, a warmer jumper and a slightly lower heater setting make a real difference too.

## Lighting and standby power

The fourth habit is switching to LED bulbs. They use around 75% less energy, or more, than old incandescent bulbs and last many years longer, which is why the higher price usually pays for itself.

Fifth, deal with the devices that only look switched off. TVs, set-top boxes, chargers and games consoles keep drawing power on **standby**, when they've been turned off with the remote but are still plugged in. The US Department of Energy estimates this hidden load can make up around 5–10% of a home's electricity use. A switched power strip solves most of it: one click at bedtime and the whole TV corner is off. Phone chargers use very little, but one left plugged in with nothing attached still wastes a bit, so pull it out when you're done.

Sixth, and simplest, use daylight and turn off lights in empty rooms. People sometimes worry that switching things off and on again costs more than leaving them running. For lights and ordinary household devices it doesn't; turning something off when you don't need it almost always saves electricity.

## Kitchen, laundry and hot water

Number seven is the fridge, the one appliance that never gets a break. Set it to around 3–5 °C and the freezer to around −18 °C. Keep it away from the oven and out of direct sun, leave space behind it so the coils can breathe, and check that the door seal closes tightly. Let hot food cool before it goes in, otherwise the fridge has to pull all that heat out itself.

Eighth, the washing machine. Run full loads and use cold or warm water where you can, because most of a washer's energy goes into heating the water rather than spinning the drum.

Ninth, the dryer and the iron. Air-dry clothes whenever the weather allows, which in most Arab cities is much of the year, and iron in one big batch instead of heating the iron every morning for a single school shirt.

Tenth, the water heater. Don't leave it on all day if nobody needs hot water. Switch it on in good time before use or fit a timer, turn the temperature down to a moderate setting, and fix dripping hot taps, since every drop lost is water you already paid to heat.

> **Did you know?** The electricity wasted by devices on standby is often called "vampire power", because it's quietly drained by appliances you think are switched off.

## Buying appliances and reading your bill

The eleventh saving happens in the shop. When you buy an air conditioner, fridge or washer, look for the energy label or star rating. **Inverter** air conditioners usually use less power than conventional ones, because they adjust the compressor's speed instead of constantly switching it on and off. They often cost more up front, but the difference can come back through your bills over a few years.

Twelfth, understand **tiered pricing**. In many countries the price per kilowatt-hour rises as your monthly use climbs. Trimming consumption a little can drop you into a cheaper tier, which shrinks the bill by more than the energy you actually saved. Tiers change, so check your electricity provider's website for current rates.

## Beware of "electricity saving devices"

Online ads promote small plug-in boxes that supposedly slash your bill. There's no reliable evidence that these gadgets save real electricity in homes, consumer protection groups have warned about them, and some are poorly made and potentially unsafe. Think about what the meter measures: the energy your appliances draw. A box in the socket doesn't make the AC or the water heater need any less. Real savings come from using less energy, through the habits above.

## Questions families ask about the electricity bill

### Which appliance uses the most electricity at home?

In most homes it's the air conditioner or an electric heater, followed by the electric water heater, then the fridge because it never stops running.

### Are LED bulbs worth the money?

Generally yes. They use a fraction of the energy of traditional bulbs and last for years, so the price difference comes back through your bills.
