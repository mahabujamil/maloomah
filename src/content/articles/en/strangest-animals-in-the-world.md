Ask a class to name the strangest animals in the world and someone will usually say the platypus. Fair enough: it has a duck's bill and a beaver's tail, it lays eggs, and it still feeds its babies on milk. But it has serious competition. Below are ten real animals that could pass for science-fiction props, starting with a quick table and then the biology that explains each one's oddness.

## 1. The axolotl: the salamander that regrows body parts

With its permanent grin and a frill of feathery gills around its head, the axolotl looks more like a cartoon than a Mexican amphibian. Most salamanders start life as larvae in water and then change into an adult form. The axolotl never finishes that change. It keeps its larval features for its whole life.

What really draws scientists to it, though, is **regeneration**. Lose a limb, and an axolotl grows it back. It can even repair parts of its heart and brain, so it has become a favourite research animal. In the wild the story is much sadder: in its natural home, the lakes near Mexico City, it is critically endangered.

## 2. The platypus: is this the strangest animal of all?

The platypus lives in the rivers of eastern Australia, and it looks like several animals stitched together: a duck-like bill, a beaver-like tail and webbed feet. The oddities go well beyond its looks. It is a mammal that **lays eggs**. Its bill works as a sensor, picking up the electrical signals that prey gives off underwater, so it can find food without seeing it.

Males carry a venomous spur on their back legs, which is a very unusual weapon for a mammal. And the platypus has no true stomach at all; its gullet connects straight to the intestine. No wonder it tops so many "strangest animal" lists: it mixes features of mammals, birds and reptiles.

So is it a bird, since it lays eggs? No. The platypus is a **mammal** because it feeds its young on milk and its body is covered in fur. Along with the echidnas, it belongs to a small group of egg-laying mammals called monotremes.

## 3. The aye-aye: the finger tapper

The aye-aye is a nocturnal lemur from the forests of Madagascar, and its middle finger is extremely long and thin, almost wire-like. It hunts a bit like someone knocking on a wall to find the hollow spot. It taps on a tree trunk, listens to the echo to work out where grubs are hiding in the wood, and then hooks them out with that same finger.

## 4. The blobfish: "the world's ugliest animal"

In a light-hearted 2013 poll, the blobfish was voted "the world's ugliest animal", and its saggy face spread everywhere. The fish deserves an apology.

It lives deep in the ocean off Australia and New Zealand, where the water pressure is enormous, and down there it looks like an ordinary fish. The droopy shape in the famous photos only appears when it is hauled up to the surface and the pressure around it suddenly drops.

## 5. The pangolin: the armoured mammal

The pangolin is the only mammal covered in hard scales, and those scales are made of keratin, the same material as your fingernails. When something threatens it, it curls up into a tight armoured ball that leaves a predator nothing soft to grab. That armour does nothing against people, sadly. The pangolin is among the most poached and illegally trafficked mammals in the world.

## 6. The mantis shrimp: the fastest punch

Small and brightly coloured, the mantis shrimp hardly looks dangerous. Yet it delivers one of the fastest strikes in the animal kingdom, hard enough to smash a snail's shell in an instant.

> **Did you know?** A mantis shrimp punches so fast that it creates tiny bubbles in the water that collapse with a second shockwave, so its prey gets hit twice!

Its eyes are among the most complex in nature, with about 12 types of colour-detecting cells, while human eyes have just 3. You might expect that to give it amazing colour vision. Experiments show otherwise: it is no better than we are at telling similar colours apart. It seems to recognise colours quickly rather than compare shades finely.

## 7. The tardigrade: the nearly indestructible animal

The tardigrade, or water bear, is a microscopic animal only about half a millimetre long, and it turns up almost everywhere, even in moss. Its survival skills are unmatched. It can enter a dormant state in which it loses most of its body water, and in that state it endures extreme drought, cold and radiation. In a 2007 experiment, some tardigrades even survived exposure to the vacuum of space.

Still, "indestructible" is a popular exaggeration. Tardigrades cope with those conditions while dormant, but they die if the exposure lasts too long or hits them while they are active, and their natural lifespan is usually short.

## 8. The naked mole-rat: the long-lived cancer fighter

Wrinkly pink skin with almost no fur, and teeth that stick out: the naked mole-rat is not winning any beauty contests. Scientists care about it for a different reason. It lives for more than 30 years, far longer than rodents of a similar size, and it very rarely gets cancer.

Its social life is closer to a beehive than to a family of mice. Naked mole-rats live in underground colonies in East Africa, and each colony has a single breeding "queen".

## 9. The star-nosed mole: the world's fastest eater

Around this mole's nose sit **22 fleshy tentacles** arranged like a star, each packed with extremely sensitive touch receptors. It lives in the wetlands of eastern North America and feels its way around with that star, identifying and swallowing food in less than a quarter of a second. That makes it one of the fastest eaters on Earth.

## 10. The leafy seadragon: a fish disguised as seaweed

Off the coast of southern Australia lives the leafy seadragon, whose body is covered in leaf-like flaps. Drifting through seaweed, it becomes very hard to pick out. It is a relative of the seahorse and shares one of its most surprising habits: the male carries the eggs until they hatch.

## Strangest sea creatures, and why animals end up so weird

From this list, three sea creatures stand out: the blobfish, the mantis shrimp and the leafy seadragon. Which is strangest is a matter of taste, just like the bigger question of the strangest animal in the world.

Why animals look this odd becomes clearer when you go back over the examples. The mole's star finds food by touch, the seadragon's flaps hide it from enemies, and the tardigrade's dormant state carries it through drought. Each strange feature is usually a clever adaptation to the animal's environment, whether for camouflage, hunting in the dark or surviving harsh conditions. Several of these animals, including the axolotl and the pangolin, are now under threat, and protecting them before they disappear is our responsibility.
