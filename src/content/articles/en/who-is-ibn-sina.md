Most medical textbooks go out of date within a few decades. One written around a thousand years ago stayed on European university reading lists for roughly five hundred years. Its author was Ibn Sina, known in the West as Avicenna. So who was Ibn Sina? **He was a physician, philosopher and polymath of the Islamic golden age, honoured with the title "al-Shaykh al-Ra'is" (the Chief Master)**, and his Canon of Medicine shaped how doctors were trained in both East and West.

## Who was Ibn Sina, and where was he from?

His full name was Abu Ali al-Husayn ibn Abd Allah ibn Sina. He was born around 980 AD in Afshana, a village near Bukhara in what is now Uzbekistan, and he died in 1037 AD in Hamadan, in modern-day Iran. He spent much of his life moving between cities and royal courts, yet he kept writing wherever he went.

He wrote mostly in Arabic. That wasn't because he lived in an Arab country; it was because Arabic was the shared language of science at the time, much as English is in research today. A book in Arabic could be read by scholars from Spain to Central Asia.

## A teenager who ran out of things to study

Ibn Sina's talent showed very early. As a boy he studied language, literature, logic and mathematics, then moved on to medicine and had mastered it while still in his teens. His autobiography says he had worked through most of the known sciences of his day by about the age of 18.

His big break came through a patient. Nuh ibn Mansur, the Samanid ruler of Bukhara, fell ill with something other doctors couldn't cure, and the young Ibn Sina treated him. His reward was access to the royal library, one of the richest collections of its time. For a reader like him, that was worth more than gold, and he read widely there.

> **Did you know?** Ibn Sina wrote that he read Aristotle's Metaphysics about 40 times, until he knew it by heart, without understanding it! Then he came across a commentary by the philosopher al-Farabi, and the book finally made sense.

That story is reassuring for any student stuck on a hard text. Memorising wasn't enough even for a genius; what helped was a good explanation.

## The Canon of Medicine and his other books

His best-known work is **The Canon of Medicine** (al-Qanun fi al-Tibb), an encyclopedia in five books. It gathered the medical knowledge of his age, added his own clinical experience, and arranged it all so a student could find a disease, its symptoms and its treatment in a logical place. That organisation is a big part of why it lasted. It was translated into Latin in the 12th century and remained a major textbook in European universities until about the 17th century.

He also wrote The Book of Healing (al-Shifa), a vast encyclopedia of philosophy and science covering logic, the natural sciences, mathematics and more, and The Book of Salvation (al-Najat), a shorter version of it for readers who needed the essentials.

Hundreds of works are attributed to him, and an estimated 240 or so have survived.

## Ibn Sina's main achievements

People often ask what Ibn Sina discovered. There is no single invention to point to; his contribution was a set of ideas that were well ahead of their time.

| Field | Achievement |
| --- | --- |
| Infectious disease | Observed that some diseases spread by contagion, possibly through water and soil |
| Quarantine | Discussed isolating the sick to limit epidemics |
| Testing medicines | Set out rules in the Canon for testing drugs and checking their effects, resembling the basics of modern drug trials |
| Organising medicine | Arranged diseases, symptoms and treatments into a clear system that served as a model for centuries |
| Philosophy | Became one of the leading philosophers of his era, influencing thinkers in both East and West |

Take **contagion**. Keeping a child with flu home from school seems like common sense now, but linking illness to something passed from one source to another, and then suggesting the sick be kept apart, was a real step forward. His rules for testing medicines carry the same spirit: try a remedy carefully and check what it actually does before trusting it.

## How he is remembered

A crater on the Moon is named after him. UNESCO awards the Avicenna Prize for Ethics in Science. Many hospitals, universities and schools in the Arab world and beyond carry his name, so there is a fair chance you have walked past one.

## Questions students ask about Ibn Sina

### Why was he called "al-Shaykh al-Ra'is"?

The title means the chief or foremost master. It reflects how highly he stood among the scholars of his time in medicine and philosophy.

### Is Ibn Sina the father of medicine?

Many call him the "father of early modern medicine" or the "prince of physicians" because of his huge influence. The title "father of medicine" itself traditionally goes to the Greek physician Hippocrates, which is worth remembering in a quiz.
