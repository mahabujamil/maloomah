A crocodile lying still on a riverbank can pass for a floating log, and that stillness is exactly what makes it so dangerous. Students looking up crocodile facts for a science project usually want the same few answers: how big they get, what they eat, and whether they lay eggs. We'll take those one at a time, starting with the animal's family tree, which reaches back to the age of the dinosaurs.

## Crocodile facts: what kind of animal is it?

A crocodile is a **reptile** in the order Crocodilia. The order also holds alligators, caimans and the gharial. Counts vary a little with how scientists classify them, but there are about 25 living crocodilian species, and roughly half are true crocodiles.

You'll find crocodiles in the warm parts of Africa, Asia, the Americas and Australia, in rivers, lakes and swamps, and a few species handle salt water well. Their bodies are built for a life half in water and half out. The skin is thick and stiffened by small bony plates under the scales, so it works almost like armour. The eyes and nostrils sit on top of the head, which lets a crocodile watch and breathe while nearly all of it stays hidden, rather like a snorkeller with only the tube above the surface. A heavy tail pushes it through the water the way an oar pushes a boat.

Crocodiles are **cold-blooded**: their body temperature follows their surroundings. That's why you see them basking in the morning sun and sliding back into the water once it gets hot. They manage their temperature by moving, not by producing heat the way we do.

## The largest crocodile species

Three species are bigger than the rest:

| Species | Where it lives | Typical adult male length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Saltwater crocodile | Southeast Asia and northern Australia | About 4–5 m, rarely over 6 m |
| Nile crocodile | Sub-Saharan Africa and the Nile valley | About 3.5–5 m |
| Orinoco crocodile | Venezuela and Colombia | About 4 m or more |

The saltwater crocodile is the largest living reptile on Earth. The very biggest males can approach a tonne, and a few top 6 metres, which is longer than a family car. The Nile crocodile is Africa's largest crocodilian. National Geographic gives its average lifespan in the wild as about 45 years, and it can live longer in captivity; other sources report longer lives for large individuals, especially in zoos.

## What do crocodiles eat, and how do they hunt?

Crocodiles are predators, and the menu includes fish, birds, reptiles and mammals. Hatchlings start small, with insects and little fish, and move on to bigger prey as they grow.

They hunt by **ambush**. Instead of chasing, a crocodile waits motionless just below the surface with only its eyes and nostrils showing, lets an animal come close to drink, and then lunges. The catch is that crocodiles can't chew. So they swallow food in large chunks, and they may spin their whole body in the water to tear prey into pieces small enough to get down.

They also swallow stones now and then. Scientists think the stones help grind food in the stomach, which makes sense for an animal that can't grind it with its teeth, and they may help with balance in the water too.

## Crocodile bite force

In a well-known 2012 study, Gregory Erickson and colleagues measured the bite force of many crocodilian species. A saltwater crocodile produced the strongest bite then recorded for a living animal: about **3,700 pounds per square inch** (around 16,000 newtons).

Oddly, the muscles that open the jaws are fairly weak, while the ones that close them are enormously strong. A crocodile's job is to clamp down and hold on; opening its mouth takes little effort, so those muscles never needed much power.

> **Did you know?** The sex of baby crocodiles is decided by the **temperature of the nest** during incubation, not by genetics as in humans. A difference of a few degrees can make most of a clutch male or female.

## Do crocodiles lay eggs?

**Yes, crocodiles lay eggs.** The female digs a nest in sand or piles up a mound of plants near the water, lays dozens of hard-shelled eggs, and stays to guard them through incubation. In the Nile crocodile that takes around three months.

When the babies are ready to hatch, they call out from inside their shells. The mother opens the nest and may carry the hatchlings gently in her mouth down to the water, so the same jaws behind one of nature's hardest bites become a safe cradle. Even with that care, many young crocodiles don't survive their first weeks. Birds of prey, large fish and other predators pick them off.

## Crocodile vs alligator: how to tell them apart

At first glance the two look almost the same, but the head usually gives it away:

| Feature | Crocodile | Alligator |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Snout | Longer and narrower, V-shaped | Broader and rounded, U-shaped |
| Teeth with mouth closed | Lower teeth visible, including the large fourth tooth | Lower teeth mostly hidden |
| Salt water | Some species tolerate it well | Prefers fresh water |
| Range | Africa, Asia, the Americas and Australia | Only the United States and China |

A wide, rounded snout with the lower teeth tucked away points to an alligator. A pointed snout with lower teeth showing even when the mouth is shut means a crocodile.

## Surprising facts about crocodiles

Some crocodile habits sound strange until you know the reason. A crocodile can't stick its tongue out, because a membrane holds the tongue to the floor of its mouth. It can also go months without eating when it has to; a cold-blooded body burns so little energy that it doesn't need regular meals the way a lion does.

It's a skilled diver as well. Most dives last a few minutes, but a crocodile resting quietly can stay under far longer. Its teeth keep getting replaced: when one falls out, a new one grows in its place, and this happens many times over its life, whereas we get just one replacement set. And for all its power, a crocodile is quicker in water than on land, though some species can manage a short dash on the ground.

## Questions students ask about crocodiles

### Are there still crocodiles in the Nile in Egypt?

Far fewer than there used to be. Today Egypt's crocodiles live mainly in Lake Nasser, south of the Aswan High Dam, while the species is still common in rivers across sub-Saharan Africa.

### Are crocodiles dangerous to people?

Yes. Large species such as the Nile and saltwater crocodiles are very dangerous, so keep well away from riverbanks where they live and follow local safety advice.
