Most students study the way they were never actually taught to: read the chapter, read it again, run a highlighter over the important bits, and hope it sticks. It often doesn't, and the reason has little to do with intelligence. The most effective study methods are built on **testing yourself and spacing out your review**, not on rereading. Psychologists and education researchers have looked at this question for a long time, and they have a fairly clear picture of what works and what only feels like it works.

## Popular methods that don't work well

In 2013, psychologist John Dunlosky and his colleagues published a well-known review in the journal *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*. They went through hundreds of studies on ten common study techniques and rated how useful each one was. Two of the student favourites came out near the bottom. Rereading many times has limited benefit, and highlighting or underlining feels productive but on its own does little to improve memory.

The problem is familiarity. By the third time you see a paragraph, it looks known, so you assume you know it. But you have never once tried to pull it out of your head without the page in front of you, and that is exactly what the exam asks you to do.

Here is how the review rated the techniques:

| Rating | Techniques |
| --- | --- |
| High usefulness | Practice testing (testing yourself), spaced practice |
| Moderate usefulness | Interleaving (mixing problem types), asking "why?" (elaborative interrogation), explaining to yourself |
| Low usefulness | Rereading, highlighting, summarising, keyword memory tricks, picturing the text |

"Low" doesn't mean useless. A good summary still helps you organise your ideas; it just isn't enough by itself, so pair it with testing yourself.

## 10 effective study methods proven to work

### 1. Active recall: test yourself

After reading a section, close the book and ask what you actually remember. Write everything you can on a blank page, answer the end-of-chapter questions, or make flashcards with the question on one side and the answer on the other. The effort of remembering is what strengthens the memory, which is why **active recall** is one of the most powerful study methods there is.

### 2. Spaced repetition

Five reviews in one evening do less than five reviews spread over two weeks. Go back to the material after a day, then three days, then a week. German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus showed back in the 19th century that we forget very quickly unless we review, and spaced review moves information into long-term memory. There's a ready-made schedule further down.

### 3. The Pomodoro technique

Italian Francesco Cirillo came up with this in the late 1980s and named it after his tomato-shaped kitchen timer ("pomodoro" is Italian for tomato). You study for 25 minutes with full focus, take a 5-minute break, and after four rounds take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes. Twenty-five minutes is short enough that starting doesn't feel like a big decision, and the regular breaks keep you from burning out. If you keep putting off studying, read our guide on [how to stop procrastinating](/en/articles/how-to-stop-procrastinating/).

### 4. Mix up problem types (interleaving)

In maths and science, it's tempting to do twenty problems of the same kind in a row. You get good at them in your notebook, then freeze in the exam, because the exam doesn't tell you which kind of problem you're looking at. Mixing types while you practise trains you to pick the right method for each question, rather than repeating the one you just learned.

### 5. The Feynman technique: explain it to a child

Take one idea and explain it in very plain words, as if to a younger brother or a classmate who missed the lesson. You'll quickly find the spot where you stumble, and that spot is the part you need to go back to.

### 6. Ask "why?" and "how?"

A fact on its own slips away; a fact attached to a reason stays put. Instead of memorising the date of a war, ask why it happened. Instead of memorising an equation, ask how it works. Understanding makes memorising easier and longer-lasting.

### 7. Summarise in your own words, and draw

Write summaries in your own words rather than copying sentences from the book, and turn information into mind maps, diagrams and tables. Words and pictures together give your memory two routes back to the same idea.

### 8. Put your phone away

Not face down on the desk, but in another room. Notifications break your focus, and studies suggest that just having your phone nearby can distract you even if you don't touch it.

### 9. Sleep well

It's easy to treat sleep as time stolen from revision, but it works the other way: sleep is when **your brain consolidates what you learned** during the day. That's why pulling an all-nighter before an exam weakens focus and memory instead of helping. To find out how many hours you need at your age, read [how much sleep do you need](/en/articles/how-much-sleep-do-you-need/).

### 10. Plan, and do past papers

Split the syllabus across the days you have left and give each session one small, specific goal, like "cell structure" rather than "biology". In the last two weeks, do past exam papers under timed conditions, as if you were sitting the real thing, so both the clock and the question style feel familiar.

> **Did you know?** The idea that each student has one "learning style", visual or auditory, and should only study that way isn't backed by strong scientific evidence. It's better to use all the effective methods and mix reading, drawing, explaining and problem-solving.

## A sample spaced-repetition plan

Suppose you study a new lesson on Sunday. You could review it like this:

| When | What to do | Time needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sunday (day 1) | Study the lesson and write a short summary | 45 minutes |
| Monday (day 2) | Write what you remember on a blank page, then check | 10 minutes |
| Wednesday (day 4) | Answer 5 to 10 questions on the lesson | 15 minutes |
| The next Sunday (day 8) | Explain the lesson out loud as if teaching it | 10 minutes |
| Two weeks later | A quick quiz, then review only your mistakes | 10 minutes |

After the first session, no review takes more than a quarter of an hour, yet together they fix the lesson in your memory far better than one long session. Notice, too, that every step asks you to recall something rather than reread it.

## How to focus while studying

Focus starts with where you sit. Pick a quiet, tidy spot and use the same one each day, so that sitting there starts to mean "study time". Then tackle the hardest subject first, while your energy is highest, and leave the easier work for when you start to tire.

Your body matters too. Drink water, and avoid studying when you're very hungry or straight after a heavy meal. If you like music in the background, choose something calm without lyrics, since words compete with the lesson for your attention.

## How to study the night before an exam

The night before is for reviewing, not learning from scratch. A simple order to follow:

1. Don't start big new topics; stick to what you've already covered.
2. Go over your summaries, key formulas and definitions.
3. Do a few quick practice questions to test yourself.
4. Get your pens, calculator and anything else you need ready before bed.
5. Sleep 7 to 8 hours; your brain needs it on exam morning.

## Questions students ask about studying

### How many hours should I study a day?

There's no single number for everyone, because quality counts more than length. Two hours of focused study with active recall beats five hours of distracted rereading.

### What's the best time to study?

Whenever you're most alert and can stick to it every day. Many people like mornings for a clear head, but sticking to a routine matters more than the hour itself.

### Is highlighting a waste of time?

Not completely, but on its own it does little for memory. If you highlight, keep it to key words, then close the book and try to recall what the highlighted parts said. That turns highlighting into a recall exercise.

### What is the single best study method?

Research points to testing yourself, especially when it's spread over several days with spaced repetition. Using the two together is one of the most effective ways to study. Try them on one subject for two weeks and compare the results with your old routine.
