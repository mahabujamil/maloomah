Have you ever spent hours rereading a textbook and highlighting lines, only to forget most of it in the exam? You're not alone. The problem usually isn't your intelligence; it's your **study method**. The good news is that psychologists and education researchers have studied this a lot, and they know which methods really work.

## First: popular methods that don't work well

In a well-known 2013 scientific review, researchers rated ten common study techniques and found that:

- **Rereading** many times has limited benefit.
- **Highlighting and underlining** feels productive, but on its own does little to improve memory.

That's because these methods make the material **familiar** to your eyes, so you think you know it, when you haven't actually practised **recalling** it.

## 10 study methods proven to work

### 1. Active recall (test yourself)

Instead of rereading, close the book and ask yourself: **what do I remember?** Answer questions, write everything you recall on a blank page, and use flashcards. This is one of the most powerful study methods there is.

### 2. Spaced repetition

Review material at increasing intervals: after a day, then three days, then a week. German psychologist **Hermann Ebbinghaus** showed back in the 19th century that we forget very quickly unless we review, and spaced review moves information into long-term memory.

### 3. The Pomodoro technique

Study for **25 minutes with full focus**, then take a **5-minute break**. After four sessions, take a longer break of 15–30 minutes. It prevents burnout and makes getting started easier.

### 4. Mix up problem types (interleaving)

In maths and science, don't solve 20 problems of the same type in a row. Mix them up, because it trains your brain to **choose the right method** for each question, just like in the exam.

### 5. The Feynman technique: explain it to a child

Pick an idea and explain it in very simple words, as if teaching a child or a friend. Where you stumble is exactly the part you need to review.

### 6. Ask "why?" and "how?"

Don't memorise facts in isolation; connect them to their causes. Why did the war happen? How does this equation work? Understanding makes memorising easier and longer-lasting.

### 7. Summarise in your own words, and draw

Write summaries in your own words, and turn information into **mind maps**, diagrams and tables. Combining words and pictures helps memory.

### 8. Put your phone away

Keep your phone in another room while studying. Notifications break your focus, and studies suggest that just having your phone nearby can distract you even if you don't touch it.

### 9. Sleep well

Sleep isn't time lost from studying; it's when **your brain consolidates what you learned**. Pulling an all-nighter before an exam weakens focus and memory.

### 10. Plan, and do past papers

Split the syllabus across days and set a small goal for each session. In the last two weeks, **do past exam papers** under timed conditions, as if it were the real exam.

> **Did you know?** The idea that each student has one "learning style", visual or auditory, and should only study that way isn't backed by strong scientific evidence. It's better to use all the effective methods and mix reading, drawing, explaining and problem-solving.

## How to focus while studying

- Choose a **quiet, consistent, tidy place** to study.
- Start with the **hardest subject** while your energy is highest.
- Drink water, and don't study very hungry or right after a heavy meal.
- If you like music, choose calm music without lyrics, since words compete for your attention.

## How to study the night before an exam

1. **Don't start big new topics**; focus on review.
2. Go over your **summaries**, key formulas and definitions.
3. Do some **quick practice questions** to test yourself.
4. Get your things ready the night before.
5. **Sleep 7–8 hours**; your brain needs it for the exam.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many hours should I study a day?

There's no single number for everyone. Two hours of focused study with active recall beats five hours of distracted rereading.

### What's the best time to study?

Whenever you're most alert and can stick to it every day. Many people prefer mornings for a clear head, but consistency matters most.

## Key takeaway

Effective studying means **testing yourself** instead of rereading, and **spacing out your review** instead of cramming the night before, with phone-free focus and enough sleep. Try these methods for two weeks and you'll notice the difference in your grades and confidence.
