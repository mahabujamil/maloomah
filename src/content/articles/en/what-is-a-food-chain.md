Every living thing needs energy to survive, and in nature energy passes from one living thing to another through food. That path is called a **food chain**. In this lesson we explain what a food chain is, its three main parts, simple food chain examples on land and in water, and how a food chain differs from a food web, with tables and questions to help you revise.

## What is a food chain?

A **food chain** is a sequence of living things in which each one feeds on the one before it, so food energy passes from one link to the next.

Almost every food chain starts with the **Sun**. Plants use sunlight to make their own food through photosynthesis, animals eat the plants, and other animals eat those animals.

We draw a food chain with arrows, and the arrow always points **from the thing being eaten to the thing that eats it**, in the direction the energy moves:

> Sun → grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk

To understand how a plant makes the food at the start of the chain, read our lesson on [photosynthesis](/en/articles/what-is-photosynthesis/).

## The parts of a food chain

Every food chain is made of three main groups:

| Part | What it is | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Producers** | Living things that make their own food from sunlight, water and carbon dioxide | Grass, trees, algae, phytoplankton |
| **Consumers** | Living things that can't make their own food, so they eat others | Rabbit, lion, owl, humans |
| **Decomposers** | Living things that break down dead organisms and return nutrients to the soil | Bacteria, fungi such as bread mould and mushrooms |

### Types of consumers

- **Primary consumers** eat plants directly. They are herbivores, such as rabbits, deer and grasshoppers.
- **Secondary consumers** eat primary consumers, such as a fox eating a rabbit or a frog eating a grasshopper.
- **Tertiary consumers** eat secondary consumers, such as a hawk or a large snake.
- **Omnivores** eat both plants and animals, such as bears and humans.

### Why do decomposers matter?

Without decomposers, fallen leaves and dead animals would pile up everywhere, and nutrients would never return to the soil for new plants to grow. Decomposers are nature's clean-up crew, and they close the circle of life.

## Simple food chain examples

### Food chains on land

1. Grass → rabbit → fox
2. Wheat → mouse → owl
3. Leaves → caterpillar → sparrow → wildcat
4. Grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk

### Food chains in water

1. Phytoplankton → zooplankton → small fish → large fish
2. Algae → snails → crab → seagull
3. Phytoplankton → krill → blue whale

> **Did you know?** The blue whale, the largest animal that has ever lived, feeds mainly on tiny shrimp-like crustaceans called krill. The biggest animal in the world sits in one of the shortest food chains!

## Energy in a food chain: the 10 percent rule

Not all the energy passes from one link to the next. Each living thing uses most of its energy to move, breathe and grow, and loses a lot of it as heat.

Scientists estimate that, on average, **only about 10%** of the energy passes from one feeding level to the next. If the plants hold 10,000 units of energy:

| Feeding level | Example | Approximate energy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Producers | Grass | 10,000 units |
| Primary consumer | Grasshopper | 1,000 units |
| Secondary consumer | Frog | 100 units |
| Tertiary consumer | Snake | 10 units |

That is why **food chains are usually short**, rarely more than 4 or 5 links, and why there are far fewer predators at the top than plants at the bottom. The African savanna has far fewer lions than gazelles, and far fewer gazelles than blades of grass.

## Food chain vs food web

In nature, an animal rarely eats just one kind of food. An owl eats mice and small birds, and a mouse might be eaten by an owl, a snake or a fox. When several food chains in one place overlap, they form a **food web**.

| Feature | Food chain | Food web |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | One straight line | Many overlapping chains |
| Energy paths | One path | Many paths |
| How realistic | A simplified model | Closer to real nature |
| Stability | Badly affected if one link disappears | More stable, because animals have other food |

## What happens if a link disappears?

Every link in a food chain matters. If the frogs disappeared from a lake, for example:

- the insects they used to eat could multiply out of control;
- the snakes and birds that eat frogs would have less food;
- and the **balance of the whole ecosystem** would be upset.

Links can disappear because of overhunting, cutting down forests, and polluted water and soil. People protect the environment partly because we are part of a huge food web ourselves. To learn more about these problems, read our [essay about pollution](/en/articles/essay-about-pollution/).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a food chain, explained for kids?
A food chain is "who eats whom" in nature: a plant makes its food from sunlight, an animal eats the plant, and another animal eats that animal, so energy passes from one to the next.

### What does every food chain start with?
It starts with producers, meaning plants and algae, which get their energy from the Sun. That is why the Sun is called the first source of energy in most food chains.

### What are decomposers? Give an example.
Decomposers are living things that break down dead organisms and waste and return nutrients to the soil, such as bacteria and fungi.

### Why does the arrow in a food chain point to the eater?
Because the arrow shows the direction energy moves, and energy moves from the thing being eaten to the thing that eats it.

### What is the difference between a primary and a secondary consumer?
A primary consumer eats plants, like a rabbit. A secondary consumer eats primary consumers, like a fox.

### Why are food chains short?
Because, on average, only about 10% of the energy passes from one level to the next, so there isn't enough energy left after 4 or 5 links.

## Key takeaway

A **food chain** is the path energy takes from the Sun to plants (producers), then to animals (consumers), while decomposers return nutrients to the soil. Only about 10% of the energy passes from one link to the next, so chains stay short, and in nature they overlap to form food webs.
