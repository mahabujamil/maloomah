A grasshopper chews on a blade of grass in the school garden, hops a little too close to a hungry frog, and becomes lunch. Behind that small moment is a big idea. The energy the grass captured from sunlight has moved into the grasshopper, and now into the frog. That path of energy from one living thing to another, through food, is called a **food chain**. Below you'll find what a food chain is, its three main parts, simple examples on land and in water, and how a food chain differs from a food web.

## What is a food chain?

A food chain is a sequence of living things in which each one feeds on the one before it, so food energy passes from one link to the next.

Almost every food chain starts with the Sun. Plants use sunlight to make their own food through photosynthesis. Animals eat the plants, and other animals eat those animals. Nothing eats the Sun, of course, but it is still where the energy comes from.

We draw a food chain with arrows, and the arrow always points **from the thing being eaten to the thing that eats it**. Students often draw it the other way round. An easy fix is to remember that the arrow follows the energy, and the energy goes into whoever did the eating:

> Sun → grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk

To understand how a plant makes the food at the start of the chain, read our lesson on [photosynthesis](/en/articles/what-is-photosynthesis/).

## The parts of a food chain

Every food chain is built from three groups, and each one does a job the others can't:

| Part | What it is | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Producers | Living things that make their own food from sunlight, water and carbon dioxide | Grass, trees, algae, phytoplankton |
| Consumers | Living things that can't make their own food, so they eat others | Rabbit, lion, owl, humans |
| Decomposers | Living things that break down dead organisms and return nutrients to the soil | Bacteria, fungi such as bread mould and mushrooms |

### Types of consumers

Consumers are sorted by what they eat. Primary consumers eat plants directly; they are herbivores, such as rabbits, deer and grasshoppers. Secondary consumers eat the primary consumers, the way a fox eats a rabbit or a frog snaps up a grasshopper. Tertiary consumers sit one step higher and eat secondary consumers, as a hawk or a large snake does.

Some animals refuse to stick to one menu. Omnivores eat both plants and animals, and bears and humans are both examples. When you eat a salad you're acting as a primary consumer; when you eat chicken, you've moved up to secondary.

### Why do decomposers matter?

Picture a forest where fallen leaves and dead animals never rot. They would pile up everywhere, and the nutrients locked inside them would never get back into the soil for new plants to use. Decomposers stop that from happening. They are nature's clean-up crew, and they close the circle of life: what a plant took from the soil eventually goes back. The mould that grows on old bread in your kitchen is a fungus doing exactly this job.

## Simple food chain examples

### Food chains on land

1. Grass → rabbit → fox
2. Wheat → mouse → owl
3. Leaves → caterpillar → sparrow → wildcat
4. Grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk

### Food chains in water

In seas and rivers, phytoplankton and algae take the place of grass. They are the producers at the bottom:

1. Phytoplankton → zooplankton → small fish → large fish
2. Algae → snails → crab → seagull
3. Phytoplankton → krill → blue whale

> **Did you know?** The blue whale, the largest animal that has ever lived, feeds mainly on tiny shrimp-like crustaceans called krill. The biggest animal in the world sits in one of the shortest food chains!

## Energy in a food chain: the 10 percent rule

You might expect the frog to get all the energy that was in the grasshopper. It doesn't. The grasshopper spent most of its energy hopping, breathing and growing, and lost a lot of it as heat, so only a little is left in its body to pass on.

Scientists estimate that, on average, **only about 10%** of the energy passes from one feeding level to the next. Start with plants holding 10,000 units of energy and watch it shrink:

| Feeding level | Example | Approximate energy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Producers | Grass | 10,000 units |
| Primary consumer | Grasshopper | 1,000 units |
| Secondary consumer | Frog | 100 units |
| Tertiary consumer | Snake | 10 units |

This is why food chains are usually short, rarely more than 4 or 5 links: by then there isn't enough energy left to support another level. It also explains why there are far fewer predators at the top than plants at the bottom. The African savanna has far fewer lions than gazelles, and far fewer gazelles than blades of grass.

## Food chain vs food web

A food chain is a useful simplification, but real nature is messier. An animal rarely eats just one kind of food. An owl hunts mice and small birds, and a mouse might end up eaten by an owl, a snake or a fox. When several food chains in one place overlap like this, they form a **food web**.

| Feature | Food chain | Food web |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | One straight line | Many overlapping chains |
| Energy paths | One path | Many paths |
| How realistic | A simplified model | Closer to real nature |
| Stability | Badly affected if one link disappears | More stable, because animals have other food |

## What happens if a link disappears?

Suppose the frogs vanished from a lake. The insects they used to eat would have nothing holding them back and could multiply out of control. At the same time, the snakes and birds that hunt frogs would go hungry, and their numbers would fall too. One missing link, and the balance of the whole ecosystem is upset.

Links disappear for reasons like overhunting, cutting down forests, and polluted water and soil. Humans sit inside a huge food web too, so protecting the environment also means protecting our own food. ## Questions students ask about food chains

### What is a food chain, explained for kids?
It's "who eats whom" in nature. A plant makes its food from sunlight, an animal eats the plant, another animal eats that animal, and the energy moves along each step.

### What are decomposers? Give an example.
Living things that break down dead organisms and waste and return nutrients to the soil. Bacteria and fungi are the main examples.

### What is the difference between a primary and a secondary consumer?
A primary consumer eats plants, like a rabbit. A secondary consumer eats primary consumers, like a fox.
