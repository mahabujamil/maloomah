Every autumn, as the olive harvest gets going across Jordan and the Levant, the mills run late into the night and families bring home tins of new oil, bright green and sharp on the tongue. Grandparents will tell you it cures almost anything. So what are the real **benefits of olive oil**? Olive oil is one of the most studied fats in nutrition science, and its **proven benefits for the heart** are solid. It sits at the centre of the Mediterranean diet. Some of the other claims, though, have no evidence behind them. Here's how to tell the two apart, how to pick a genuine oil and how much of it to use.

## What's in olive oil?

Olive oil is pure fat. One tablespoon (about 13.5 g) has roughly 119 calories, so it is not a "light" food. What makes it special is the kind of fat it contains, not the amount:

| Component | Approximate share or amount |
| --- | --- |
| Monounsaturated fat (mostly oleic acid) | about 73% |
| Polyunsaturated fat | about 10% |
| Saturated fat | about 14% |
| Vitamins E and K | useful amounts |
| Antioxidant polyphenols | mainly in extra virgin oil |

## Olive oil health benefits for the heart

The best evidence for olive oil is about the heart, and the table above explains why. When you cook with olive oil instead of butter or ghee, you're swapping saturated fat for unsaturated fat, and that helps improve LDL ("bad") cholesterol.

There's also strong evidence from a large trial. The Spanish PREDIMED study followed thousands of people at high risk of heart disease. Those on a Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra virgin olive oil had about 30% fewer major cardiovascular events than people on a reduced-fat diet.

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) also accepts that olive oil polyphenols help protect blood lipids from oxidative stress, as long as the oil contains enough of them. That condition matters, because it means the type of oil you buy makes a real difference.

## Other benefits for the body

Extra virgin oil contains a compound called oleocanthal, which causes the peppery sting at the back of your throat. Research suggests it has anti-inflammatory properties.

Olive oil also helps you get more out of your vegetables. Some vitamins, such as A and K, dissolve in fat, so a drizzle of oil on a salad helps your body absorb them. Traditional dishes like fattoush and tabbouleh have been doing this for generations.

Fat slows down how quickly your stomach empties, so a meal with some oil in it keeps you full for longer. It's calorie-dense, though, so portions matter. Some people find it has a mild softening effect on digestion, but it isn't a treatment for chronic constipation.

> **Did you know?** That slight burning feeling in your throat when you taste fresh extra virgin olive oil is a sign it's rich in polyphenols. Professional tasters actually look for it.

## Olive oil on an empty stomach: fact or hype?

Social media is full of claims that a spoonful of olive oil first thing in the morning, or mixed with lemon, "melts fat" or "detoxes" the body. There is no strong scientific evidence for that. The benefits come from eating olive oil regularly as part of a healthy diet, and breakfast is no better than dinner. If drinking it neat doesn't appeal, dipping bread in oil and za'atar gets you exactly the same benefit.

Plenty of people also use olive oil on hair and skin as a natural moisturiser. It may help dry hair and reduce breakage, but research is limited, and it may not suit oily skin that is prone to acne.

## Types of olive oil and how to tell if it's real

Not everything sold as olive oil is the same product. From the highest grade to the lowest:

- **Extra virgin:** extracted mechanically, without high heat or chemicals, with free acidity of no more than 0.8%. It has the most flavour and the most polyphenols.
- Virgin: slightly lower grade, with acidity up to 2%.
- Refined or "light" olive oil: processed oil that loses most of its polyphenols. "Light" refers to flavour, not calories.
- Olive pomace oil: extracted from the leftover pulp; the lowest grade.

To find a good oil, look for "extra virgin" and a harvest date on the label, buy from a trusted brand or mill, and be suspicious of prices that seem too good to be true. You may have heard of the fridge test, where real oil is supposed to turn cloudy. It's unreliable; only a lab test of acidity gives a definite answer.

Once you've bought it, light and heat are the enemies. Keep the oil in a dark glass bottle or a metal tin, away from the stove and the window, and use it within about one to two years of pressing.

## How much olive oil per day?

There's no single official amount. Many studies used roughly 1.5 to 4 tablespoons a day, replacing other fats rather than adding to them. If you're watching your weight, remember each tablespoon is about 120 calories.

Extra virgin olive oil is also more heat-stable than its reputation suggests, so it's fine for everyday home cooking. Repeated deep frying is a different matter and breaks down some of its beneficial compounds. Try it in dishes like [falafel](/en/articles/falafel-recipe/) or [lentil soup](/en/articles/lentil-soup-recipe/).

## Questions people ask about olive oil

### Does olive oil raise cholesterol?
No. It tends to improve cholesterol when it replaces saturated fats like butter and ghee.

### How can you tell if olive oil is real?
Check the label, acidity and harvest date, buy from a trusted source, and look for a fresh, slightly bitter and peppery taste. Lab testing is the only certain method, so don't rely on the fridge test alone.
