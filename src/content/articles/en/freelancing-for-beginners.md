A university student designs a logo for a small restaurant in another city. A languages graduate translates a contract for a company whose office she has never seen. Both work from their bedrooms and get paid online. That is freelancing, and for beginners with one solid skill and some patience it is one of the most realistic ways to [make money online](/en/articles/ways-to-earn-money-online/). You can start from zero, as long as you take the steps in the right order.

## What is freelancing?

Freelancing means selling your skills to different clients, paid per project or per hour, without being a permanent employee of any one organisation. In practice you're closer to a small shop owner than to a staff member: you decide what to sell, set the price and build your own reputation. Much of this work now runs through online platforms that connect clients with freelancers and protect both sides, so the client doesn't pay for work they haven't seen and the freelancer doesn't hand over work without a guarantee of payment.

## The most in-demand freelance skills

Before you think about platforms, think about what you're going to sell. These are the fields where clients most often hire freelancers:

| Field | Example services |
| --- | --- |
| Design | Logos, social media posts, brand identities |
| Writing and content | Articles, website copy, ad copywriting |
| Translation | Arabic–English translation, document translation |
| Programming | Websites, apps, online stores |
| Video and audio | Editing, motion graphics, voice-over |
| Digital marketing | Social media management, paid ads, SEO |
| Support and admin | Virtual assistance, data entry, customer service |

Don't try to cover the whole table. A client looking for a logo designer trusts someone who presents themselves as a logo designer far more than someone who claims to design, translate and code. Pick **one skill**, get genuinely good at it, and expand around it later, the way a logo designer might move on to full brand identities.

## The best-known freelancing platforms

Platforms work in one of two ways. On some, clients post a job, freelancers send proposals, and the client picks one. On others, freelancers list a ready-made service at a fixed price, and clients buy it straight away, much like buying from a shop.

Fiverr is the giant of the second type: you publish "gigs" that clients order directly. Upwork is the best-known example of the first, where you send proposals for posted jobs. Freelancer and PeoplePerHour are other well-known options. Many regions also have their own platforms; in the Arab world, for example, Mostaql and Khamsat are popular.

### How much do freelance platforms take?

Every platform takes a commission from your earnings, so read the terms before you start.

| Platform | Freelancer fee (at this article's last update) |
| --- | --- |
| Fiverr | A flat 20% of every order |
| Upwork | A variable fee of 0% to 15% per contract, shown to you when you submit a proposal |
| Regional platforms (e.g. Mostaql, Khamsat) | A percentage of each project or service, listed in each platform's terms |

Here's what that means in real money: sell a $100 service on Fiverr and $80 reaches your balance, before any withdrawal fees. People who price without counting the fee find out later that they've been working for less than they thought. Build it into your prices from day one, and check each platform's official fee page now and then, because fees can change.

## How to start freelancing, step by step

The order matters here. Each step makes the next one easier.

### 1. Choose a skill and learn it well

Start with something you already know, or learn an in-demand skill through free courses on YouTube and learning platforms. Watching isn't enough, though. Give yourself a few weeks of hands-on practice, because clients pay for what you can produce, not for what you've watched.

### 2. Build a portfolio

The first question in any client's mind is simple: what can this person actually do? You can answer it before you have a single client by making your own samples, such as logos for imaginary companies, a few sample articles or a simple website. Show them on Behance if you design or GitHub if you code, and put your best pieces on your platform profile.

### 3. Create a professional profile

Your profile is your shop window, and clients judge it in seconds. It needs a clear, professional photo and a specific headline like "Logo and brand identity designer" rather than "I do everything". Add a short bio that says what you offer and how it helps the client, then your work samples.

### 4. Send tailored proposals

A single job can attract dozens of proposals, most of them copied and pasted. That's exactly why one that mentions the details of the brief stands out. Read the job carefully, then write a short proposal showing that you understand what the client needs and how you'll deliver it.

### 5. Start with reasonable prices

Reasonable prices help you land your first projects and first reviews, which are the hardest things to get. As reviews build up, raise your rates gradually. Clients will pay more for someone others have already hired and been happy with.

### 6. Deliver quality on time

Good reviews are **your real capital** as a freelancer, and one bad review early on can set you back weeks. Deliver on time, communicate politely, and do what you promised plus a little more.

## Mistakes beginners make

The most dangerous one is moving a client off the platform early. It can break the platform's rules, it removes the protection the platform gives you, and it's how many scams start. Close behind is accepting a vague project before pinning down exactly what's needed, which tends to end in endless revisions and arguments about payment.

Then there's pricing. Low prices are a way in, but if you never raise them they'll wear you out without ever turning into real income. Patience is another trap: plenty of people quit after two weeks with no projects, when the start is simply the hardest stretch and things usually improve. And finally, don't stop learning. The market keeps changing, and a skill that stands still slowly loses value.

## How do you get paid?

Platforms offer several withdrawal methods, such as bank transfer, PayPal and Payoneer, and which ones work depends on your country. Check before you start; there's no point building up a balance you can't withdraw. Find out, too, if your country has registration or tax rules for freelance income.

## Questions beginners ask about freelancing

### Can I start freelancing with no experience?

Yes, if you learn an in-demand skill and build samples that show your level. Real experience comes with your first projects, not before them.

### How much can I earn freelancing?

There's no fixed figure. It depends on your skill, the quality of your work, the hours you put in and your reviews. Many people start small, and income grows with experience and reputation.

### Fiverr or Upwork: which is better for beginners?

They differ more in how they work than in quality. Fiverr suits clearly packaged services at set prices, while Upwork suits bigger, proposal-based projects. Many freelancers use both.

### Do you need money to start freelancing?

Usually not much. A decent computer or phone, an internet connection and an in-demand skill are enough to begin. Just remember platform fees and withdrawal costs when you set your prices. The first few weeks are often slow, and that's normal. One skill done well, an honest portfolio and a habit of delivering quality on time are what turn those first reviews into steady income.
