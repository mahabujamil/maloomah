You've probably heard it on the evening forecast: "a low is moving in tomorrow." By morning the sky is grey and rain is drumming on the classroom windows. So **what is a low pressure system**, and why does that one word predict rain so reliably? The answer starts with something that surprises a lot of students: the air around you has weight.

## What is a low pressure system?

Air really does weigh something. The weight of the column of air stretching up above any spot is called **air pressure**, and at sea level it averages about 1013 hectopascals (hPa).

**A low pressure system is an area where the air pressure is lower than in the air around it.** Notice that the definition is a comparison. There's no magic number that makes somewhere a "low". A region at 1005 hPa, or at 990 hPa, counts as one as long as its neighbours are higher. Lows are also called depressions, and in the broad scientific sense cyclones, which doesn't mean every low is a destructive storm.

## How does a low pressure system form?

Everything begins with rising air. At the centre of a low, air moves upward, so the column of air pressing down on the surface gets lighter and the pressure drops. Air from the higher-pressure surroundings then flows in to fill the gap, and that flow is what we feel as wind.

You might expect the air to rush straight to the centre, but it doesn't. The Earth's rotation deflects it, an effect known as the **Coriolis effect**. As a result, winds swirl around a low anticlockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere.

What gets the air rising in the first place? Usually one of three things. Cold air from the north can meet warm, moist air and force the warm air upward. Strong sunshine can heat the ground so the air touching it rises. Or warm seas can do the same job, which is how tropical cyclones form.

## Low pressure system weather: why rain, wind and cold?

Once you know that air rises in a low, the rain makes sense. Rising air expands and cools, and cool air can't hold as much water vapour as warm air. The extra vapour condenses into cloud, and the cloud can produce rain. We walk through that process in [how rain forms](/en/articles/how-does-rain-form/).

Wind depends on the pressure difference. The steeper the drop from the surroundings to the centre, the faster air pours in, and the stronger the wind.

The cold usually comes along for the ride. Many winter lows over the Levant drag a cold air mass down from the north behind them, so temperatures fall sharply and snow can settle on high ground. And when the rising air is especially vigorous, [thunderstorms](/en/articles/what-is-a-thunderstorm/) can develop, bringing lightning and sometimes hail.

## Low vs high pressure: what's the difference?

A high pressure system is the mirror image of a low. Its pressure is higher than its surroundings, and air sinks towards the ground. Sinking air gets compressed, which warms and dries it, so clouds tend to clear. Almost every feature flips between the two:

| Feature | Low pressure | High pressure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pressure | Lower than surroundings | Higher than surroundings |
| Air movement | Rising | Sinking |
| Wind rotation (Northern Hemisphere) | Anticlockwise | Clockwise |
| Typical weather | Cloud, rain, wind | Fair and settled |
| Map symbol | L | H |

This is why farmers in Jordan, Syria and Lebanon are glad to hear about winter lows: they mean water for the fields. Highs bring calm, sunny days instead. In winter, though, a high can also mean frosty nights, because with no cloud cover the ground loses its heat quickly after dark.

> **Did you know?** The lowest sea-level pressure ever measured was inside Typhoon Tip in the Pacific on 12 October 1979: about 870 hPa, more than 140 hPa below average.

## Types of lows that affect the Middle East

The lows that reach the region aren't all alike. Each has its own season and its own kind of weather:

| Type | When it's common | What it brings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cyprus low | Winter | Centred near Cyprus in the eastern Mediterranean; the main source of winter rain in the Levant |
| Low with a polar air mass | Mid-winter | Severe cold and rain, sometimes snow on high ground |
| Red Sea trough | Mainly autumn, sometimes spring | A tongue of low pressure reaching up from the south; usually dry, but when "active" it can trigger heavy thunderstorms and flash floods |
| Khamsin low | Spring | Moves east from North Africa, preceded by hot, dry, dusty winds |

Jordanian forecasts often mention "a low accompanied by a cold air mass". That phrase actually describes two things working together: the low supplies the rain, and the cold air mass supplies the sudden drop in temperature. A khamsin low is a different story. It's linked far more to dust storms and sudden heat than to rain.

## How is pressure measured, and how do you read a low pressure system map?

Air pressure is measured with a barometer. Meteorologists use the hectopascal (hPa), which is exactly equal to the older millibar (mb), so a reading in an old textbook and one in a current forecast mean the same thing.

When you look at a weather map, these are the things to find:

1. Isobars: curved lines joining places with equal pressure, much like contour lines on a relief map.
2. The low pressure symbol: the letter L, often printed in red, sitting inside closed isobars whose values fall towards the centre. Highs are marked H.
3. Spacing: isobars packed tightly around a low mean strong winds.
4. Fronts, which often trail from the centre of a low: blue lines with triangles are cold fronts, and red lines with semicircles are warm fronts.

Forecasters put these maps together with satellite images and computer models to predict where a low will head next, as explained in [how weather forecasts are made](/en/articles/how-are-weather-forecasts-made/). So whenever you hear that a low is on the way, your national meteorological department's warnings are the ones to follow.

## Questions students ask about low pressure

### What is a low pressure system called?

It's called a low, a depression, or, in the broad scientific sense, a cyclone.

### What are the characteristics of a low pressure system?

Lower pressure than its surroundings, rising air, winds circling it (anticlockwise in the Northern Hemisphere), and usually cloud, wind and a chance of rain.

### Does every low pressure system bring rain?

No. Rain needs enough moisture in the air. Some lows, such as a khamsin low or an inactive Red Sea trough, can pass with dust and wind but little or no rain.

### How long does a low pressure system last?

Most lows affecting the Middle East last roughly one to three days, though some linger if they slow down or another low follows close behind.
