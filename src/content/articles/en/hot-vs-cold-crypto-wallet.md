The hot vs cold wallet question comes down to one thing: is the key that controls your coins sitting on a device connected to the internet? **A hot wallet keeps its keys online; a cold wallet keeps them offline.** People tend to start asking about this the week a big exchange gets hacked, when "where is my crypto actually safe?" suddenly feels urgent. Knowing the difference makes it easier to choose and to avoid the most common mistakes.

> This article is for education only and is not investment advice. Cryptocurrencies are high-risk and can lose much or all of their value.

## What does a crypto wallet actually hold?

The name is a little misleading. Your coins aren't stored inside the wallet; they stay recorded on the **blockchain**, a shared public ledger that tracks the balance of every address. What the wallet holds is the thing that lets you spend that balance: your **private key**, a secret code that proves ownership and authorises transfers.

A bank account is a useful comparison. Your account number is something you hand out so people can pay you; in crypto that's your public address, and sharing it is harmless. Your password and signature are what let money leave the account; that's the private key, and anyone who gets hold of it can move your coins as if they were you.

Then there's the seed phrase, also called a recovery phrase. It's usually a list of 12 or 24 words that can rebuild your keys on any other device, which makes it every bit as valuable as the keys themselves. If all of this is new, it helps to read [what is cryptocurrency](/en/articles/what-is-cryptocurrency/) first.

## What is a hot wallet?

A hot wallet is any wallet whose keys live on an internet-connected device. You may already be using one without calling it that:

- Mobile wallet apps.
- Browser extension wallets on a computer.
- Your account on a crypto exchange, where the exchange holds the keys for you.

Hot wallets are popular for good reasons. They're usually free, easy to set up, and quick for sending, receiving and trading. The catch is that their convenience comes from being online all the time, and that same connection exposes them to hacking, malware and phishing links.

## What is a cold wallet?

A cold wallet keeps your private keys offline. The most common kind is a **hardware wallet**, a small device often about the size of a USB stick. It generates the keys and never lets them out. When you send crypto, you approve the transaction on the device itself, so the private key never leaves it.

Why does that matter so much? Because a hacker who breaks into your computer remotely finds no key there to steal. That gives cold wallets far stronger protection against remote attacks and makes them a natural fit for holding coins over the long term.

There are trade-offs. You have to buy the device, every transaction takes an extra step, and the responsibility for guarding your seed phrase rests entirely with you.

## Hot vs cold wallet at a glance

| Feature | Hot wallet | Cold wallet |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Internet connection | Always online | Keys kept offline |
| Security | Lower | Higher |
| Ease of use | Quick and convenient | Slower, more steps |
| Cost | Usually free | Requires buying a device |
| Best for | Small amounts, daily use | Larger amounts, long-term holding |

The table shows that this isn't a choice between a good wallet and a bad one. It's a trade between convenience and security, which is why plenty of people use both: a hot wallet with a small amount for everyday use, and a cold wallet for the rest. It's the same logic as carrying a day's cash in your pocket and leaving your savings somewhere safer.

> **Did you know?** Crypto has a famous saying: "Not your keys, not your coins." It means that when you leave coins on an exchange, you are trusting the exchange to protect them. Many people learned this the hard way when the Mt. Gox exchange collapsed in 2014.

## How to protect your crypto

Most losses don't come from brilliant hackers. They come from one small slip by the owner. These habits close the usual gaps:

1. Never share your seed phrase with anyone, even someone claiming to be tech support. No legitimate service will ever ask for it.
2. Write it on paper or a metal plate and store it somewhere safe. Don't photograph it or keep it in your phone, email or cloud storage.
3. Buy a hardware wallet from the manufacturer or an authorised reseller. If the box contains a pre-written seed phrase, treat the device as compromised.
4. Turn on two-factor authentication for exchange accounts, preferably with an authenticator app rather than SMS.
5. Watch out for phishing. Check the website address before logging in, and don't install apps from unknown sources.
6. Double-check the address before sending. Blockchain transfers usually can't be reversed.

A lot of these attacks rely on software that slips onto a device unnoticed. To see how that works, read [virus vs worm vs Trojan](/en/articles/difference-between-virus-worm-trojan/).

## Questions beginners ask about cold wallets

### Is a cold wallet 100% safe?

Nothing is completely safe. A cold wallet protects you against remote hacking, but not if someone steals your seed phrase or talks you into approving a fraudulent transaction yourself.

### What happens if I lose my hardware wallet?

Your coins aren't lost as long as you have your seed phrase, because it's enough to restore them on a new device. If you lose both the device and the phrase, you may lose access for good.

### Can I turn a hot wallet into a cold wallet?

Not the app itself, but you can send your coins from the hot wallet to your cold wallet's address.

### Do I need a cold wallet for a small amount?

For small amounts, a reputable hot wallet with strong security settings may be enough. The larger the amount, the more cold storage matters. Whichever you pick, one rule never changes: protect your seed phrase and never share it with anyone.
