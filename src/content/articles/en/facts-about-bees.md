Here is a number to start with: a single worker honey bee makes only about one-twelfth of a teaspoon of honey in her whole life. Every spoonful in your kitchen is the life's work of a small crowd of bees. That tells you the most important of all honey bee facts. A bee on its own gets very little done. A hive, working as one, can do remarkable things.

## Who lives in a honey bee hive?

There are more than 20,000 species of bee, and most of them live alone. Honey bees are different. They live in large colonies, and every colony has three kinds of member:

| Member | How many | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Queen | One | Lays the eggs; she is the mother of the whole colony |
| Workers | Tens of thousands in summer | Everything else: cleaning, nursing, building, guarding and foraging |
| Drones | A few hundred in spring and summer | Mating with new queens |

All the workers are female. Drones are male, they have no sting, and they don't collect food. When autumn comes and food gets scarce, the workers push the drones out of the hive. A colony heading into winter can't afford to feed bees that don't work.

## Queen bee facts

**The queen is the only bee in the hive that lays eggs.** At the height of the season she can lay somewhere around 1,500 to 2,000 a day. She is longer than the workers and lives for several years, while a summer worker lives for weeks.

So how does one larva become a queen and another a worker, when they hatch from the same kind of egg? The difference is food. A larva chosen to be a queen is fed **royal jelly**, a substance made by glands in the heads of young workers, for her entire development. Worker larvae get royal jelly only for their first few days and then switch to a mix of pollen and honey. Diet alone decides whether a bee lives for weeks or for years.

## Worker bee facts: a job for every age

A new worker doesn't fly straight out to the flowers. Her life is more like moving up through school, with a new job as she gets older:

1. First she cleans out the wax cells so the queen can lay in them again.
2. Next she becomes a nurse, feeding the larvae.
3. Then she produces wax from glands on the underside of her abdomen and builds comb.
4. After that she guards the entrance, turning away intruders.
5. In her final weeks she becomes a forager, collecting nectar, pollen and water.

In summer, when the work is hardest, a worker usually lives only five or six weeks. Workers born just before winter can live for months, because they spend the cold season clustered around the queen keeping the hive warm.

## How do bees make honey?

A forager sucks nectar, the sugary liquid in flowers, into a special pouch called the honey stomach. Enzymes added there start breaking down the sugar. Back at the hive she passes the nectar to other workers, who add more enzymes and place it in the cells.

At this stage it is still far too watery and would spoil. So the workers fan their wings over the cells like tiny electric fans until much of the water evaporates and the liquid thickens. Once the honey is ready, they seal each cell with a thin cap of wax.

Estimates suggest that a pound of honey, about 450 grams, needs nectar from roughly two million flowers. No wonder beekeepers treat it with respect.

The six-sided shape of the cells is no accident either. Hexagons fit together with no gaps, and they hold a lot of honey for a small amount of wax, which costs the bees a great deal of energy to make.

## How bees talk: the waggle dance

When a forager finds a field full of flowers, how does she tell the others? She dances. The Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch showed that honey bees use a round dance when food is close to the hive and a waggle dance, in which the bee shakes her abdomen as she walks in a straight line, when food is farther away. The waggle dance gives both the distance and the direction, using the angle of the run relative to the sun.

Von Frisch shared the 1973 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his work on animal behaviour, including these discoveries about bees.

> **Did you know?** A honey bee has five eyes: two large compound eyes on the sides of its head and three small simple eyes on top. Bees can also see ultraviolet light, which we can't, so many flowers show them hidden markings that point the way to the nectar.

## Why do we need bees?

As a bee moves from flower to flower, pollen sticks to the hairs on her body and gets carried to the next bloom. That transfer, called **pollination**, is what lets many plants form fruit and seeds. The UN's Food and Agriculture Organization says that around three-quarters of the world's food crop types depend at least partly on pollinators, and bees are the most important of them. Apples, almonds, cucumbers and melons all produce far less without them.

That is why the United Nations marks World Bee Day every year on 20 May, to draw attention to bees and the threats they face, such as heavy pesticide use and the loss of wildflower habitat. Bees are also a link in the [food chain](/en/articles/what-is-a-food-chain/): they feed on flowers, and birds and other animals feed on them.

## Questions kids ask about bees

### Why does a honey bee die after it stings?

A worker's sting has tiny backward-facing barbs, like the teeth of a saw. When she stings a person or another mammal, the sting gets stuck in the skin. As she pulls away, part of her abdomen tears off with it, and she dies. The queen's sting is smooth, and she very rarely stings people.

### What makes the buzzing sound?

Her wings. A honey bee beats them roughly 200 times a second, fast enough to make the familiar hum.

### Can bees see every colour?

No. They see blue, yellow and ultraviolet well, but red looks dark to them. That's one reason bees visit blue and yellow flowers so often.

### Do all bees make honey?

No. Most bee species don't store honey in large amounts. Honey bees are the ones beekeepers keep for it.
