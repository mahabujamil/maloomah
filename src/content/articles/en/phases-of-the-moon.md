The Moon never actually changes shape. It's a ball of rock that stays round all month, yet one evening it looks like a thin sliver, a week later like half a disc, and a week after that like a bright full circle. Those changing appearances are the **phases of the Moon**. They're a standard part of school science, and long before calendars were printed, people used them to keep track of the months. Once you understand one idea about light and angles, the whole sequence makes sense.

## Why does the Moon change shape?

The Moon gives off no light of its own. What we see is **reflected sunlight**. At any moment the half of the Moon facing the Sun is lit and the other half is in darkness, just as one half of Earth has daytime while the other has night.

What changes over the month is our view of that lit half. The Moon travels around Earth, and as it moves we look at it from a different angle. When it sits between us and the Sun, its sunlit side points away from us and we can barely see it. When Earth sits between the Moon and the Sun, the whole sunlit side faces us and we get a full moon. Everywhere in between, we catch part of the lit half, which is why we see crescents, half discs and the bulging in-between shapes.

You can test this at home with a lamp and a ball. Put the lamp on one side of a dark room, hold the ball at arm's length and turn slowly on the spot. The lit part of the ball grows and shrinks as you turn, even though the ball itself never changes.

This also clears up a common mix-up: phases are **not caused by Earth's shadow**. Earth's shadow only reaches the Moon during a lunar eclipse, which is fairly rare. Phases happen every single month.

## The 8 phases of the Moon in order

| Order | Phase | What you see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | New Moon | the Moon is almost invisible |
| 2 | Waxing Crescent | a thin, growing crescent after sunset |
| 3 | First Quarter | half the disc is lit |
| 4 | Waxing Gibbous | more than half lit, still growing |
| 5 | Full Moon | the whole disc is lit |
| 6 | Waning Gibbous | more than half lit, shrinking |
| 7 | Third (Last) Quarter | the other half is lit |
| 8 | Waning Crescent | a thin crescent before dawn |

The second half of the list mirrors the first. "Waxing" means the lit part grows a little each night, "waning" means it shrinks, and the full moon is the turning point. Four of the eight are really single moments (new moon, the two quarters and full moon), while the other four are stretches of time between them. Since the whole cycle lasts about 29.5 days, each phase covers roughly three to four days.

## Waxing and waning: how the phases look from Earth

If you're in the Northern Hemisphere, there's a quick way to tell which way the Moon is heading. A lit right side means it's waxing; a lit left side means it's waning. South of the equator the rule flips.

The phases also keep different hours, which is why some feel more familiar than others. A waxing crescent hangs low in the west shortly after sunset, so it's easy to spot on the way home in the early evening. A full moon rises around sunset and stays up all night. A waning crescent is the shy one: it only appears in the east before sunrise, so most people who see it are early risers.

> **Did you know?** We always see the same face of the Moon, because it spins once on its axis in exactly the time it takes to orbit Earth. Its far side stayed a mystery until a Soviet probe photographed it in 1959.

## How long is a lunar cycle?

From one new moon to the next takes **about 29.5 days**, a period called the synodic month. That can seem odd at first, because the Moon completes one orbit of Earth, measured against the stars, in only about 27.3 days.

The extra two days come from Earth's own motion. While the Moon circles us, Earth is also moving along its path around the Sun. By the time the Moon is back in the same spot relative to the stars, the Sun appears in a slightly different direction, so the Moon has to travel for roughly two more days before it lines up with the Sun again and the same phase returns.

Lunar calendars follow this cycle. Months in the Islamic Hijri calendar, for example, have 29 or 30 days, and a lunar year ends up about 11 days shorter than a solar year.

## Moon phases, eclipses and tides

Eclipses depend on the same Sun, Earth and Moon line-up that creates the phases. A lunar eclipse can only happen at full moon, when Earth lies directly between the Sun and the Moon and its shadow falls across the Moon. A solar eclipse can only happen at new moon, when the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun and blocks its light.

So why isn't there an eclipse every month? The Moon's orbit is tilted slightly compared with Earth's, so most months the Moon slips above or below the shadow instead of passing straight through it.

The ocean feels the phases too. Tides are strongest around new and full moon, when the pull of the Sun and the pull of the Moon line up and add together.
