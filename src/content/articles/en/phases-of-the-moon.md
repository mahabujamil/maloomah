One night the Moon is a thin crescent, a week later it's a half disc, then a full, bright circle, before it shrinks away again. These changes are the **phases of the Moon**, a classic school science topic and one of the oldest natural clocks people have used to count months. Here are the moon phases in order, their names, and the science behind them.

## Why does the Moon change shape?

The Moon doesn't make its own light. It **reflects sunlight**. At any moment, the half facing the Sun is lit and the other half is dark. As the Moon orbits Earth, the angle from which we see that lit half keeps changing:

- When the Moon is between Earth and the Sun, its lit side faces away from us and we barely see it.
- When Earth is between the Moon and the Sun, we see the whole lit side: a full moon.
- In between, we see different portions of the lit half.

So the phases are **not caused by Earth's shadow**, a very common misconception. Earth's shadow only falls on the Moon during a lunar eclipse.

## The 8 phases of the Moon in order

| Order | Phase | What you see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | New Moon | the Moon is almost invisible |
| 2 | Waxing Crescent | a thin, growing crescent after sunset |
| 3 | First Quarter | half the disc is lit |
| 4 | Waxing Gibbous | more than half lit, still growing |
| 5 | Full Moon | the whole disc is lit |
| 6 | Waning Gibbous | more than half lit, shrinking |
| 7 | Third (Last) Quarter | the other half is lit |
| 8 | Waning Crescent | a thin crescent before dawn |

**Waxing** means the lit part is growing each night; **waning** means it's shrinking.

## How the phases look from Earth

A simple rule for the Northern Hemisphere: if the right side is lit, the Moon is waxing; if the left side is lit, it's waning. In the Southern Hemisphere it's the other way round.

The phases also rise at different times:

- A **waxing crescent** appears in the west shortly after sunset.
- A **full moon** rises around sunset and stays up all night.
- A **waning crescent** shows up in the east before sunrise.

> **Did you know?** We always see the same face of the Moon, because it spins once on its axis in exactly the time it takes to orbit Earth. Its far side stayed a mystery until a Soviet probe photographed it in 1959.

## How long is a lunar cycle?

A full cycle of phases, from one new moon to the next, takes **about 29.5 days**. This is called the synodic month. One orbit of Earth measured against the stars takes only about 27.3 days. The difference exists because Earth is also moving around the Sun, so the Moon needs roughly two extra days to line up with the Sun again.

That's why months in lunar calendars, such as the Islamic Hijri calendar, have 29 or 30 days, and a lunar year is about 11 days shorter than a solar year.

## Moon phases, eclipses and tides

- A **lunar eclipse** can only happen at **full moon**, when Earth lies directly between the Sun and the Moon.
- A **solar eclipse** can only happen at **new moon**, when the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun.
- Eclipses don't happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted slightly relative to Earth's.
- **Tides** are strongest around new and full moon, when the Sun's and Moon's pull line up.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the phases of the Moon in order?
New moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full moon, waning gibbous, third quarter and waning crescent.

### What causes the phases of the Moon?
The Moon's orbit around Earth, which changes the angle from which we see its sunlit half.

### How many phases does the Moon have?
Eight main phases: four instant points (new moon, the two quarters and full moon) and four stages in between.

### Is Earth's shadow the cause of moon phases?
No. Earth's shadow causes lunar eclipses only. Phases are about viewing angle.

### How long does each moon phase last?
The full cycle takes about 29.5 days, so each of the eight phases spans roughly 3 to 4 days, although the new, quarter and full moons are technically single moments.

## Key takeaway

There are eight **phases of the Moon**, from new moon to waning crescent, repeating about every 29.5 days. They happen because we see different parts of the Moon's sunlit half as it orbits Earth, not because of Earth's shadow. Understanding them explains lunar calendars, eclipses and tides.
