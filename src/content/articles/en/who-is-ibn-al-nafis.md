Most students meet William Harvey in science class as the man who discovered blood circulation. Fewer hear about a doctor in thirteenth-century Cairo who worked out one key piece of the puzzle long before him. So who was Ibn al-Nafis? He was **Ala al-Din Ali ibn Abi al-Hazm al-Qurashi, an Arab physician who described the pulmonary circulation, the flow of blood from the heart to the lungs and back, roughly 300 years before European anatomists did**.

## Who was Ibn al-Nafis? A short biography

He was born near Damascus in the early 1200s. Sources disagree on the exact year, giving dates between about 1210 and 1213. He trained in medicine at the Nuri hospital in Damascus, one of the best-known hospitals of the age. Hospitals of that kind, called *bimaristans*, were also teaching schools, so a student learned at the bedside as well as from books.

Around 1236 he moved to Cairo. He worked at the Nasiri hospital and rose to become one of the leading physicians in Egypt, eventually holding the title of chief of physicians. When Sultan Qalawun founded the Mansuri hospital in about 1284, Ibn al-Nafis served there. He died in Cairo in 1288 and left his house and his library to that hospital, so his books stayed with the patients and students who needed them.

Medicine was only part of his learning. He also studied and wrote on law, grammar and logic, which was normal for scholars of his time. [Ibn Sina](/en/articles/who-is-ibn-sina/), two centuries earlier, had been the same kind of all-round scholar.

## What did Ibn al-Nafis discover? Pulmonary circulation

To see why his idea mattered, you need to know what doctors believed before him. The Greek physician Galen, writing in the second century, taught that blood passed from the right side of the heart to the left through tiny invisible pores in the wall between the two ventricles, the septum. That view lasted for over a thousand years. Ibn Sina repeated it in his famous *Canon of Medicine*.

Ibn al-Nafis disagreed. The septum, he wrote, is solid, with no visible or invisible openings. That raised an obvious question: if blood can't cross the wall, how does it reach the left side? His answer was that blood leaves the right ventricle through the pulmonary artery, travels to the lungs, mixes with air there, and returns through the pulmonary vein to the left side of the heart. That loop is exactly what textbooks now call **pulmonary circulation**.

He set this out in his *Commentary on Anatomy in Avicenna's Canon* (*Sharh Tashrih al-Qanun*), reportedly written when he was only about 29. It's worth pausing on that. He was writing a commentary on the most respected medical book of his day, and he still corrected both its author and Galen when his reasoning led somewhere else.

## Other ideas ahead of their time

The same book contains several more insights:

- He argued that the heart muscle is fed by blood vessels running through its wall, not by the blood sitting inside its chambers. That is close to what we now call coronary circulation.
- He suggested there must be fine passages in the lungs linking the pulmonary artery to the pulmonary vein. Centuries later, microscopes revealed these as capillaries.
- He linked breathing and circulation correctly in principle, explaining that blood goes to the lungs to meet the air.

He reached all of this without a microscope, using careful observation and logic.

> **Did you know?** Modern scholars had no idea about Ibn al-Nafis's discovery until 1924. That year an Egyptian doctor, Muhyi al-Din al-Tatawi, found a manuscript of the *Commentary* in a library in Berlin and wrote about it in his doctoral thesis in Germany. Since then Ibn al-Nafis has appeared in histories of medicine around the world.

## Ibn al-Nafis, Harvey and the European anatomists

A common question in class is whether this means Harvey gets too much credit. Not exactly. They described different things, as the timeline shows:

| Scientist | Approximate date | What they described |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ibn al-Nafis | 13th century | Pulmonary circulation (heart to lungs and back) |
| Michael Servetus | 1553 | Pulmonary circulation, in Europe |
| Realdo Colombo | 1559 | Pulmonary circulation, with anatomical detail |
| William Harvey | 1628 | Full circulation of blood around the whole body |

Harvey showed that the heart pumps blood around the entire body in a continuous circuit. The smaller loop between the heart and lungs had already been described by Ibn al-Nafis about three centuries earlier. Historians still debate whether his writings reached Europe at the time or whether European scientists arrived at the same idea on their own; nobody knows for certain.

## His best-known books

- *Commentary on Anatomy in Avicenna's Canon*, which contains the description of pulmonary circulation.
- *Al-Shamil fi al-Sina'a al-Tibbiyya* ("The Comprehensive Book on Medicine"), a huge encyclopedia he never finished. Sources give different figures for its planned size, and only part of it survives.
- *Al-Mujaz fi al-Tibb*, a summary of the *Canon* that became popular with medical students.
- *Al-Muhadhdhab fi al-Kuhl*, a book on eye diseases.
- *Al-Risala al-Kamiliyya*, a philosophical story about a man who grows up alone on an island and learns about the world through reason.

## Questions students ask about Ibn al-Nafis

### Where was Ibn al-Nafis from?

He was born and educated in Damascus, then spent the rest of his working life in Cairo, where he died.

### Why is he called the discoverer of pulmonary circulation?

Because he was the first known writer to describe clearly how blood travels from the right ventricle to the lungs and back to the left side of the heart, and to reject Galen's idea of pores in the septum.

### What can students learn from him?

Mostly a habit of mind. He read Galen and Ibn Sina carefully and respected them, yet he trusted evidence over reputation. That is the same thing a good science teacher asks of you in the lab today: observe, ask why, and don't just memorise.
