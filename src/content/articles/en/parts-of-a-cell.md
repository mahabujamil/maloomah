A classic first biology practical goes like this. You peel a see-through layer from an onion, lay it on a glass slide with a drop of iodine stain, and look down the microscope. Suddenly there are hundreds of little boxes packed side by side like floor tiles, many with a dark dot near the middle. Each box is a cell, and each dot is a nucleus. That view is the best way into the parts of a cell and their functions, because every living thing, from an onion to an elephant to you, is built from units like these.

## What is a cell?

**A cell is the smallest unit of life: the basic building block of every living thing.** It's the smallest thing we can call alive, because it takes in food, releases energy, grows, reproduces and gets rid of waste. Some organisms, such as bacteria and amoebas, are a single cell. Plants, animals and people are made of enormous numbers of them.

The word itself goes back to the English scientist Robert Hooke. In 1665 he looked at a thin slice of cork through a microscope he had built and saw rows of tiny empty compartments that reminded him of small rooms, so he called them cells. In the nineteenth century the German biologists Schleiden and Schwann showed that all plants and animals are made of cells, and Rudolf Virchow added that every cell comes from an existing cell. Together these ideas form the **cell theory** you meet in your science book.

> **Did you know?** The cells Robert Hooke saw in cork were dead. Only their empty walls were left, so the first cells anyone ever described had no nucleus and none of the other parts in this lesson.

## The main parts of a cell and what they do

A handy way to remember them is to picture the cell as a school: a fence and gate around it, an office giving instructions, rooms where the work happens, and a power room keeping the lights on.

The cell membrane is the fence and the gate at once. It's a thin, flexible layer around the cell that decides what comes in and what goes out, letting water, food and oxygen in and sending waste out. Because it only lets certain things through, it's called **partially permeable**.

The cytoplasm is the playground and corridors. It's a jelly-like fluid that fills the cell, holds the other parts, and is where many chemical reactions take place.

The nucleus is the head teacher's office. It contains the genetic material, DNA, which carries the instructions for building and running the cell, and it controls the cell's activities and division. The colour of your eyes is written in that DNA.

Mitochondria are the power room, often called the powerhouse of the cell. This is where respiration happens: food is broken down using oxygen to release energy. Muscle cells, which work hard, are packed with them.

Ribosomes are tiny workshops that make proteins, following the instructions sent out from the nucleus.

The endoplasmic reticulum is a network of channels that moves materials around the cell. Where ribosomes stick to it, it looks rough under a powerful microscope.

The Golgi apparatus is the packing and dispatch department. It receives proteins, modifies them, wraps them up and sends them where they're needed, inside the cell or out of it.

Vacuoles are sacs that store water, food and waste. Lysosomes, found mainly in animal cells, are the cleaners: they contain enzymes that break down worn-out parts and anything the cell no longer needs.

## What plant cells have that animal cells don't

A plant cell has nearly everything above, plus three things of its own.

The first is the cell wall, a stiff layer of cellulose outside the membrane. It gives the cell a fixed shape and protects it, which is why the onion cells looked like neat tiles while human cheek cells look rounder and floppier.

The second is chloroplasts. They contain chlorophyll, the green pigment that absorbs sunlight, and they're where photosynthesis happens, the process plants use to make their own food. Our lesson on [what photosynthesis is](/en/articles/what-is-photosynthesis/) explains it step by step. Interestingly, the onion cells from the practical have no chloroplasts at all: an onion bulb grows underground, out of the light.

The third is a large permanent vacuole, which can take up most of the cell. It's filled with cell sap, and the pressure it puts on the wall keeps the plant firm and upright. When a houseplant wilts because someone forgot to water it, those vacuoles have lost much of their water.

## Plant and animal cells compared

| Feature | Animal cell | Plant cell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cell wall | No | Yes, made of cellulose |
| Chloroplasts | No | Yes, in green parts of the plant |
| Vacuoles | Small, often several | Usually one large permanent vacuole |
| Shape | Irregular, often rounded | Regular, often box-like |
| Centrioles | Present, help in cell division | Absent in most plants |
| Food | Takes in ready-made food | Makes its own by photosynthesis |

Both types share the membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria, ribosomes, endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus. Watch out for a common exam mistake: students sometimes write that plant cells have no mitochondria because they have chloroplasts. That's wrong. Plants need energy just like animals do, and they release it through respiration in their mitochondria.

## Shape follows function

Cells in the same body can look very different. A human red blood cell is small and dimpled on both sides so it can squeeze through narrow vessels, and it loses its nucleus as it matures, leaving more room for haemoglobin, the molecule that carries oxygen. A nerve cell is long and branched because its job is to carry signals over long distances; there's more on that in our lesson on the [nervous system](/en/articles/human-nervous-system/). Muscle cells are long and can contract and relax. Again and again in biology, a cell's shape suits the work it does.

Similar cells doing the same job group together into a tissue. Tissues form an organ, such as the heart or a leaf. Organs work together in a system, such as the digestive system, and systems make up the whole organism.

## Questions students ask about cells

### Which is the most important part of a cell?

Every part has a job the cell can't do without, but the nucleus is the control centre because it holds the DNA and directs the cell's activity. Even so, a mature human red blood cell manages about 120 days without one, because its job is limited to carrying gases.

### Can you see a cell without a microscope?

Most cells are far too small, measured in fractions of a millimetre. There are a few exceptions, such as some large single-celled algae.

### What's the difference between the cell membrane and the cell wall?

Every cell has a membrane: a thin, flexible layer that controls what enters and leaves. A cell wall is a rigid layer outside the membrane, found in plant cells and some other organisms, and its job is support and protection.

### How can I remember the parts of a cell?

Use the school picture: fence and gate for the membrane, office for the nucleus, power room for the mitochondria, workshops for the ribosomes, dispatch for the Golgi apparatus and cleaners for the lysosomes. Then draw an animal cell and a plant cell side by side and colour the plant-only parts in a different colour.
