**Who was Ibn Battuta?** He was a Moroccan traveller from Tangier who left home alone at 21 to make the Hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca, and didn't settle down again for almost thirty years. By the time he stopped he had covered about **120,000 kilometres**, through lands stretching from Morocco and al-Andalus to India, China and Mali. He later dictated what he had seen, and the book that came out of it is still one of the best windows we have onto the 14th-century world.

## Who was Ibn Battuta? A short answer

If you need the basics for a class presentation or a quick revision session, here they are in one place:

| Fact | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Full name | Abu Abdullah Muhammad ibn Abdullah ibn Muhammad al-Lawati al-Tanji |
| Known as | Ibn Battuta |
| Born | 24 February 1304 CE (703 AH) in Tangier, Morocco |
| Country | Morocco |
| Years of travel | About 29 (roughly 1325–1354 CE) |
| Approximate distance | About 120,000 km |
| Famous book | *A Gift to Those Who Contemplate the Wonders of Cities and the Marvels of Travelling*, known as the *Rihla* |
| Died | In Morocco, most often dated to around 1368 or 1369 CE |

He grew up in a family that had produced several judges, and he belonged to the Lawata, a Berber (Amazigh) tribe. As a young man he studied Islamic law in the Maliki school. That might sound like a detail, but it shaped his whole life on the road: a trained legal scholar could find respected work almost anywhere in the Muslim world, and Ibn Battuta did exactly that, serving as a judge in several of the places he stayed.

## Why was he called Ibn Battuta?

A lot of students assume the name has something to do with travelling. It doesn't. "Ibn Battuta" was a **family name** that his relatives in Tangier were already known by, passed down the way surnames are today. Where the word "Battuta" itself comes from is another matter: reliable historical sources give no confirmed origin, so treat the colourful stories you may find online with suspicion, since none of them comes with evidence.

## Leaving Tangier

He set out in June **1325**, alone, with Mecca as his goal. In his book he described saying goodbye to his family as feeling like a bird leaving its nest, an image anyone who has left home for the first time will recognise.

The route took him overland across North Africa to Egypt, then north into Syria, and from there south to the Hijaz, where he performed the pilgrimage. That would have been the natural moment to head home. Ibn Battuta didn't. What he had seen so far only made him want to see more, so he kept going, year after year, and made the Hajj more than once along the way.

## The main stops on Ibn Battuta's travels

His route was never a neat line on a map. He doubled back, stayed years in one place and only weeks in another. This table gives his main stops in rough order:

| Stop | What he did there |
| --- | --- |
| North Africa, Egypt and Syria | The start of his travels, visiting Alexandria, Cairo and Damascus |
| The Hijaz | Performed the Hajj and stayed in Mecca for a while |
| Iraq and Persia | Visited Basra, Baghdad and Persian cities |
| The East African coast | Visited Mogadishu and Kilwa on the Indian Ocean |
| Anatolia and the Central Asian steppe | Travelled through Anatolia and the lands of the Golden Horde, and visited Constantinople |
| India | Lived in Delhi for about eight years, working as a judge for Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq |
| The Maldives | Served as a judge for roughly a year and a half |
| China | Reached its southern ports and described its ships and cities |
| Al-Andalus | Visited Granada after returning to Morocco |
| The Mali Empire | His last great trip, around 1352–1353, including a visit to Timbuktu |

India stands out. He spent about eight years in Delhi as a judge for Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq, then another year and a half or so as a judge in the Maldives, which is where that early legal training really paid off.

He first got back to Morocco around 1349, but he wasn't finished. Two shorter trips followed, one north to al-Andalus and one south across the Sahara to Mali, before he finally settled in Fez around 1354.

> **Did you know?** The roughly 120,000 km Ibn Battuta travelled is about three times around the Earth, since the planet's circumference at the equator is about 40,000 km. He covered all of it by camel, horse and sailing ship, centuries before trains or planes existed.

## Ibn Battuta's book: the Rihla

Once he was back in Fez, the Marinid sultan Abu Inan Faris ordered that his travels be written down. Ibn Battuta didn't write the book himself. He dictated it to the scholar and writer **Ibn Juzayy al-Kalbi**, who shaped and edited the text. It was finished around 756 AH (1355 CE) under a long title, *Tuhfat al-Nuzzar fi Ghara'ib al-Amsar wa 'Aja'ib al-Asfar* (*A Gift to Those Who Contemplate the Wonders of Cities and the Marvels of Travelling*), though almost everyone just calls it the ***Rihla***, "the Journey". In English it is often published as *The Travels of Ibn Battuta*.

Open it and you'll find people's customs, clothes, food and markets, the scholars and rulers he met, and the cities, ports and roads he passed through. Historians read it carefully, though. Some details may be wrong, and it's easy to see why: Ibn Battuta was recalling events from memory years after they happened, and Ibn Juzayy added material from other sources. None of that makes the book worthless. It remains an important historical source.

## Why his travels still matter

For historians, the *Rihla* is rare evidence. It gives a vivid picture of 14th-century life in places that very few other writers described, such as the Maldives and the Mali Empire. The sheer distance matters too: he is one of the most widely travelled people before modern times, and went further than the Italian traveller Marco Polo.

For students, his route makes a good geography exercise. Trace it on a world map and you start to see how the old trade routes, over land and by sea, tied distant regions together. And there's a simpler lesson in his story: he left home with one goal, and curiosity carried him into the cultures of much of the known world.

## Questions students ask about Ibn Battuta

### Where was Ibn Battuta from?

Morocco. He was born in Tangier, in the far north of the country on the Strait of Gibraltar.

### How many years did he travel?

About 29 years, from leaving Tangier in 1325 to settling in Fez around 1354.

### When did Ibn Battuta die?

Sources disagree. Most say he died in Morocco around 1368 or 1369, while some give 779 AH (1377 CE).
