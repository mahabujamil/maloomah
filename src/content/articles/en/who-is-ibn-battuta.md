**Who was Ibn Battuta?** In short, he was a Moroccan traveller from the city of Tangier who left home at 21 to perform the Hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca. That one journey grew into nearly thirty years on the road, covering about **120,000 kilometres** through lands stretching from Morocco and al-Andalus to India, China and Mali. Later he dictated his story in a famous book that is still one of the best sources on the 14th-century world. In this lesson we look at his life, his travels and his book, with a table of his main stops.

## Who was Ibn Battuta? A short answer

| Fact | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Full name | Abu Abdullah Muhammad ibn Abdullah ibn Muhammad al-Lawati al-Tanji |
| Known as | Ibn Battuta |
| Born | 24 February 1304 CE (703 AH) in Tangier, Morocco |
| Country | Morocco |
| Years of travel | About 29 (roughly 1325–1354 CE) |
| Approximate distance | About 120,000 km |
| Famous book | *A Gift to Those Who Contemplate the Wonders of Cities and the Marvels of Travelling*, known as the *Rihla* |
| Died | In Morocco, most often dated to around 1368 or 1369 CE |

Ibn Battuta was born into a family that included several judges, and he belonged to the Lawata, a Berber (Amazigh) tribe. As a young man he studied Islamic law in the Maliki school, and that training later opened doors for him to work as a judge in some of the lands he visited.

## Why was he called Ibn Battuta?

Students often ask where the name comes from. "Ibn Battuta" was a **family name** that his family in Tangier was known by. It wasn't a title he earned for travelling. Reliable historical sources give no confirmed origin for the word "Battuta", so it's best not to trust the popular stories that link the name to tales with no evidence behind them.

## How Ibn Battuta's journey began

In June **1325** Ibn Battuta set out from Tangier alone, heading for Mecca to perform the Hajj. In his book he wrote that leaving his family felt like birds leaving their nests.

He travelled overland across North Africa to Egypt, then on to Syria, and from there to the Hijaz, where he performed the pilgrimage. But he didn't go home afterwards. Eager to see more of the world, he kept travelling for many years and performed the Hajj more than once along the way.

## The main stops on Ibn Battuta's travels

| Stop | What he did there |
| --- | --- |
| North Africa, Egypt and Syria | The start of his journey, visiting Alexandria, Cairo and Damascus |
| The Hijaz | Performed the Hajj and stayed in Mecca for a while |
| Iraq and Persia | Visited Basra, Baghdad and Persian cities |
| The East African coast | Visited Mogadishu and Kilwa on the Indian Ocean |
| Anatolia and the Central Asian steppe | Travelled through Anatolia and the lands of the Golden Horde, and visited Constantinople |
| India | Lived in Delhi for about eight years, working as a judge for Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq |
| The Maldives | Served as a judge for roughly a year and a half |
| China | Reached its southern ports and described its ships and cities |
| Al-Andalus | Visited Granada after returning to Morocco |
| The Mali Empire | His last great journey, around 1352–1353, including a visit to Timbuktu |

Ibn Battuta first returned to Morocco around 1349. He then made two shorter journeys, to al-Andalus and across the Sahara to Mali, before finally settling in the city of Fez around 1354.

> **Did you know?** The roughly **120,000 km** Ibn Battuta travelled is about **three times** around the Earth, since the planet's circumference at the equator is about 40,000 km. He covered all of it by camel, horse and sailing ship, centuries before trains or planes existed.

## Ibn Battuta's book: the Rihla

After he returned to Fez, the Marinid sultan **Abu Inan Faris** ordered that his travels be written down. Ibn Battuta dictated them to the scholar and writer **Ibn Juzayy al-Kalbi**, who shaped and edited the text. The book was finished around **756 AH (1355 CE)** and given the title:

***Tuhfat al-Nuzzar fi Ghara'ib al-Amsar wa 'Aja'ib al-Asfar*** (*A Gift to Those Who Contemplate the Wonders of Cities and the Marvels of Travelling*), usually just called the *Rihla*, "the Journey".

The book describes people's customs, clothes, food and markets, the scholars and rulers he met, and the cities, ports and roads he passed through. Historians think some details may be inaccurate, because Ibn Battuta dictated it from memory years later and Ibn Juzayy added material from other sources. Even so, it remains an important historical source.

## Why Ibn Battuta's travels matter

1. **A rare historical source:** the *Rihla* gives a vivid picture of 14th-century life in places little else was written about, such as the Maldives and the Mali Empire.
2. **An extraordinary distance:** he is one of the most widely travelled people before modern times, going further than the Italian traveller Marco Polo.
3. **A geography lesson:** a map of his journeys helps students understand the old trade routes over land and sea.
4. **A model of curiosity:** he set out with one goal, then let his love of learning lead him to the cultures of the world.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where was Ibn Battuta from?

He was from **Morocco**. He was born in Tangier, in the north of the country on the Strait of Gibraltar.

### How many years did Ibn Battuta travel?

About **29 years**, from leaving Tangier in 1325 to settling in Fez around 1354.

### What is the name of Ibn Battuta's book?

*Tuhfat al-Nuzzar fi Ghara'ib al-Amsar wa 'Aja'ib al-Asfar*, better known as the *Rihla* or "The Travels of Ibn Battuta".

### Who wrote the Rihla?

Ibn Battuta dictated it from memory, and **Ibn Juzayy al-Kalbi** wrote it down and edited it, on the orders of the Marinid sultan Abu Inan in Fez.

### When did Ibn Battuta die?

Sources disagree. Most say he died in Morocco around **1368 or 1369**, while some give 779 AH (1377 CE).

## Key takeaway

**Ibn Battuta** was a Moroccan traveller from Tangier who set out for the Hajj in 1325 and became one of the most famous travellers in history. He covered about 120,000 km in nearly 29 years and served as a judge in India and the Maldives. He left us the *Rihla*, dictated to Ibn Juzayy, which is still a window onto the world as it was about seven centuries ago.
