You brush your hand against a hot teapot and snatch it away before you even feel the burn. Something decided to move that hand, and it did so faster than you could think. That something is the **nervous system**: the body's communication network. It collects information from your senses, works out what it means and sends instructions to muscles and glands. Your heart, lungs and stomach all run under its supervision, which is why understanding it makes a lot of other biology lessons click into place.

## What does the nervous system do?

Think of it as a busy school. The head teacher's office is the brain, the corridors are the nerves, and the notes passed along those corridors are tiny electrical and chemical signals.

The job comes in three linked steps. First, sensing: picking up information from the eyes, ears, skin, nose and tongue, and from inside the body too, such as hunger or thirst. Second, processing: the brain compares that information with what it already knows and decides what it means. Third, responding: a signal goes out to a muscle to move or a gland to release something. When the bell rings and you close your book and stand up, you've run through all three in a second or two.

## Nervous system divisions: central and peripheral

Scientists split the nervous system into two main parts that work together all the time.

The **central nervous system (CNS)** is the brain and the spinal cord. This is where decisions are made. The skull protects the brain and the backbone protects the spinal cord. Between bone and nerve tissue sit protective membranes called the meninges and a cushioning fluid that absorbs knocks.

The **peripheral nervous system (PNS)** is every nerve that branches out from the CNS into the rest of the body. Twelve pairs of cranial nerves leave the brain directly and mostly serve the head and face; the optic nerve, which carries signals from the eye, is one of them. Another 31 pairs of spinal nerves leave the spinal cord and reach the arms, trunk and legs.

The spinal cord runs down inside the backbone and is roughly 45 cm long in an adult man. Students are often surprised that it's shorter than the backbone itself. It stops at about the top of the lower back, and a bundle of nerves carries on below it.

## The brain and its main parts

An adult brain weighs about 1.3 to 1.4 kg, roughly 2% of body weight, yet it uses around a fifth of the oxygen you breathe in. That's one reason concentration slips so quickly after a short night's sleep or a skipped breakfast.

| Part | Where it is | Main jobs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cerebrum | The large upper part | Thinking, memory, language, making sense of the senses, voluntary movement |
| Cerebellum | Below and behind the cerebrum | Balance and coordination: walking, cycling, handwriting |
| Brainstem | Joins the brain to the spinal cord | Breathing, heartbeat and swallowing, the things you never have to think about |

The cerebrum has a left and a right half. Oddly, the left half mostly controls the right side of the body and the right half controls the left, because many nerve fibres cross over on their way down.

## How a neuron sends a message

The building block of the system is the nerve cell, or **neuron**. A 2009 study that counted them estimated the human brain holds about 86 billion.

A neuron has three main parts. The cell body contains the nucleus. Dendrites are short branches that pick up signals from neighbouring cells. The axon is a long fibre that carries the signal away; some axons running down to the foot are close to a metre long.

Many axons are wrapped in a fatty layer called the **myelin sheath**. It works a bit like the plastic coating on an electric wire and makes signals travel much faster. In the fastest fibres a signal can move at around 120 metres per second, which is over 400 km/h.

Neurons don't quite touch each other, though. Between one cell and the next is a tiny gap called a synapse. When the electrical signal reaches the end of an axon, the cell releases chemicals called neurotransmitters. These cross the gap and pass the message on. So the message is electrical inside a cell and chemical between cells.

> **Did you know?** Brain tissue itself has no pain receptors, even though it's where every pain you feel is understood. A headache comes from the membranes, blood vessels and muscles around the brain, not from the neurons inside it.

## Reflexes: moving before you think

Back to the teapot. If the signal had to travel all the way to the brain and wait for a decision, your hand would stay put a fraction of a second longer and the burn would be worse. So the body has a shortcut called a **reflex arc**:

1. Heat receptors in the skin of your finger detect the burning temperature.
2. A sensory nerve carries the signal to the spinal cord.
3. The spinal cord sends an instant command back along a motor nerve.
4. Arm muscles contract and pull your hand away.
5. Only then does the signal reach the brain, and you feel the pain and realise what happened.

Blinking when something flies towards your eye, sneezing, and the knee jerk when a doctor taps just below the kneecap are reflexes too. They protect you without waiting for permission.

## The autonomic nervous system

You've never told your heart to beat or your stomach to digest lunch. Those jobs belong to the **autonomic nervous system**, a part of the peripheral system that runs on its own and controls the heart, blood vessels, gut and glands.

It has two branches that balance each other like the two sides of a see-saw. The sympathetic branch gets you ready to face danger or run from it, the "fight or flight" response. If a dog suddenly barks at you, or you step up to read in front of the whole school assembly, your heart speeds up, you breathe faster, your pupils widen and digestion slows down for a while.

The parasympathetic branch takes over when you're calm, "rest and digest". It slows the heart, gets digestion and saliva going again and returns the body to normal once the danger has passed. That's why your heartbeat settles once you've finished your reading and sat back down.

The gut even has its own web of nerves, the enteric nervous system, with hundreds of millions of neurons that manage much of the work of digestion. You can follow food's route through the body in our lesson on the [human digestive system](/en/articles/human-digestive-system/).

## Looking after your nervous system

Your nervous system is tough and well protected, but everyday habits help it work at its best. Sleep matters most: while you sleep, the brain sorts and stores what you learned during the day. Revising in the evening and then getting a full night's rest beats staying up until dawn. The brain also needs a balanced diet, enough water and regular exercise, which keeps blood and oxygen flowing to it.

Protection matters too. Wear a helmet on a bike and a seatbelt in the car, because injuries to the head and back can harm the brain and spinal cord. And keep your brain busy with reading, puzzles and new skills, which strengthens the connections between neurons.

## Questions students ask about the nervous system

### What is the difference between the central and peripheral nervous system?

The central nervous system is the brain and spinal cord, where information is processed and decisions are made. The peripheral nervous system is the network of nerves connecting the CNS to the rest of the body, carrying news in and instructions out.

### Do nerve cells grow back?

Most neurons in the brain last a lifetime and aren't replaced the way skin cells are. Peripheral nerves can slowly repair themselves after some injuries, though, and the brain keeps building new connections between its cells every time you learn something.

### What runs from the base of the brain down the backbone?

The spinal cord. It's part of the central nervous system, acting as the main highway for messages between brain and body and as the control centre for many reflexes.
