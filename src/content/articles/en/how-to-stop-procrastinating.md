"I'll start tomorrow." We all say it, and then tomorrow arrives and we say it again. Students do it the week before an exam, employees do it with a report that's been waiting for days, and people running their own business do it too. If you want to know how to stop procrastinating, the most useful first step is understanding why you put things off, because the fix depends on the cause. After that come the practical strategies that help you start today rather than tomorrow.

## What procrastination is, and why we do it

Procrastination is **delaying an important task for no good reason, even though you know the delay will hurt you**. That makes it different from planned rest, and different from sensible prioritising, where you put one thing off because something else matters more. When you procrastinate, you know exactly what needs doing. You just slip away to something easier, like scrolling your phone.

Here's the surprising part. Many psychologists now see procrastination as a problem of **managing emotions** rather than managing time. We don't delay a task because there's no time for it. We delay it because it stirs up a feeling we'd rather avoid. Sometimes that feeling is fear of failure or criticism: "What if my work isn't good enough?" Sometimes it's perfectionism, the habit of waiting for the perfect time or the perfect mood, which never quite shows up.

Other causes are more ordinary. A big, vague task is intimidating, and when you don't know where to begin, escaping feels easier. A dull task will lose to instant fun almost every time. And the reward is far away: exam results arrive months from now, while the fun of a video arrives this second.

> **Did you know?** Our brains tend to prefer a small reward now over a bigger reward later. Behavioural economists call this "temporal discounting", and it's one of the keys to understanding procrastination.

## How to stop procrastinating: 9 practical strategies

Not every strategy suits everyone, so pick the ones that match your own reason for delaying. If big tasks scare you, start with the first two. If your phone is the problem, go straight to number four.

### 1. Break the task into tiny steps

"Study the whole chapter" feels heavy. "Read the first two pages" is something you could begin right now. The smaller and clearer the step, the less daunting it is to start.

### 2. Use the two-minute rule

The **two-minute rule** has two halves. If a task takes two minutes or less, do it now. If it's bigger, commit to working on it for just two minutes. Most of the time you'll keep going once you've started, because starting is the hardest part of almost any job.

### 3. Try the Pomodoro Technique

With the **Pomodoro Technique**, you work for 25 minutes with full focus, then take a 5-minute break. After four rounds, you take a longer one. Italian Francesco Cirillo created the method and named it after a tomato-shaped kitchen timer (pomodoro is Italian for tomato).

| Stage | Length |
| --- | --- |
| Focused work | 25 minutes |
| Short break | 5 minutes |
| Long break after 4 rounds | 15–30 minutes |

### 4. Remove distractions

Put your phone in another room or switch on "Do Not Disturb", and close browser tabs you don't need. The rule is simple: the harder a distraction is to reach, the less you'll procrastinate.

### 5. Set a time and place

"I'll do it later" commits you to nothing. Compare it with "I'll write the report at 5 pm at my desk". Plans tied to a specific time and place are carried out far more often than vague intentions.

### 6. Tackle the hardest task at your peak

If you're sharpest in the morning, open your day with the task you dread most. Once it's done, the weight is off your shoulders and the rest of the day feels lighter.

### 7. Forgive yourself

This one sounds backwards, but psychology research found that students who forgave themselves for procrastinating before one exam procrastinated less before the next. Beating yourself up adds more negative feelings, and negative feelings are exactly what you were running from, so the avoidance grows.

### 8. Reward yourself

Give yourself something small after each chunk of work: a cup of tea, a short walk, an episode of your favourite show. That pulls a distant reward a little closer, and if you keep it up, the routine gradually becomes [a lasting habit](/en/articles/how-long-does-it-take-to-form-a-habit/).

### 9. Look after your sleep and energy

When you're tired, every task feels heavier than it really is. Enough sleep and some daily movement improve your focus and self-control, which are the very things you need to resist putting work off.

## How to stop procrastinating when studying for exams

Procrastination tends to peak in exam season, when there are lots of subjects and plenty of worry. The first remedy is a realistic study timetable with specific hours. "I'll study a lot" is a vague promise nobody holds you to; "maths from 4 to 6" is a plan. Start with your hardest subject in the first session of the day, while your mind is fresh.

It also helps to study alongside a serious classmate in a library or another quiet place, since working next to someone who is focused makes it easier to focus yourself. And use [effective study methods](/en/articles/effective-study-methods/) such as active recall and spaced repetition.

## When is procrastination a sign of something bigger?

If procrastination is severe and constant, and comes with persistent sadness, strong anxiety or lifelong difficulty concentrating, it may be linked to something that needs professional assessment, such as depression, anxiety or ADHD. In that case, talking to a doctor or psychologist is a wise step, not a sign of weakness.

## Questions students ask about procrastination

### How do I stop procrastinating for good?

No method gets rid of it forever, because everyone procrastinates sometimes. But breaking tasks down, the two-minute rule and removing distractions reduce it a lot over time.

### What's the difference between procrastination and laziness?

Laziness is not wanting to make an effort at all. A procrastinator does want to get things done and feels guilty about delaying, but is escaping the negative feelings tied to the task.

### Is procrastination a mental illness?

On its own, no. It's a very common behaviour. When it's severe and persistent, though, it can be a symptom that accompanies some mental health conditions, as described in the section above.
