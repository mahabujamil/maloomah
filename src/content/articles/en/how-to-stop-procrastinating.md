"I'll start tomorrow." We all say it, and then tomorrow comes and we say it again. **Procrastination** isn't rare; students, employees and business owners all struggle with it. So how do you stop procrastinating? This article explains why we put things off in the first place, then gives you 9 practical strategies to start today, not tomorrow.

## What is procrastination?

Procrastination is **delaying an important task for no good reason, even though you know the delay will hurt you**. It's different from planned rest or sensible prioritising: when you procrastinate, you know what needs doing but escape to something easier, like scrolling your phone.

## Why do we procrastinate?

Many psychologists now see procrastination as **a problem of managing emotions rather than managing time**. We delay a task because it stirs up an unpleasant feeling. Common causes include:

- **Fear of failure or criticism:** "What if my work isn't good enough?"
- **Perfectionism:** waiting for the "perfect time" or "perfect mood" to start.
- **Big, vague tasks:** when you don't know where to begin, escaping feels easier.
- **Boredom:** dull tasks always lose to instant fun.
- **Distant rewards:** exam results arrive months later; the fun of a video arrives now.

> **Did you know?** Our brains tend to prefer a small reward now over a bigger reward later. Behavioural economists call this "temporal discounting", and it's one of the keys to understanding procrastination.

## How to stop procrastinating: 9 practical strategies

### 1. Break the task into tiny steps

Instead of "study the whole chapter", write "read the first two pages". A small, clear step makes starting far less daunting.

### 2. Use the two-minute rule

If a task takes two minutes or less, do it now. If it's bigger, commit to working on it for **just two minutes**. You'll usually keep going once you've started, because starting is the hardest part.

### 3. Try the Pomodoro Technique

Work for **25 minutes** with full focus, then take a **5-minute** break. After four rounds, take a longer break. Italian Francesco Cirillo created the technique and named it after a tomato-shaped kitchen timer (pomodoro is Italian for tomato).

| Stage | Length |
| --- | --- |
| Focused work | 25 minutes |
| Short break | 5 minutes |
| Long break after 4 rounds | 15–30 minutes |

### 4. Remove distractions

Put your phone in another room or switch on "Do Not Disturb", and close tabs you don't need. The harder a distraction is to reach, the less you'll procrastinate.

### 5. Set a time and place

Instead of "I'll do it later", say "I'll write the report at 5 pm at my desk". Plans tied to a specific time and place are carried out far more often than vague intentions.

### 6. Tackle the hardest task at your peak

If you're sharpest in the morning, start with the task you dread most. Getting it done early gives you peace of mind for the rest of the day.

### 7. Forgive yourself

Psychology research found that students who forgave themselves for procrastinating before one exam procrastinated less before the next. Beating yourself up adds negative feelings, which fuels more avoidance.

### 8. Reward yourself

Give yourself a small reward after each chunk of work: a cup of tea, a short walk, an episode of your favourite show. Build it up gradually until it becomes [a lasting habit](/en/articles/how-long-does-it-take-to-form-a-habit/).

### 9. Look after your sleep and energy

Tiredness makes every task feel heavier. Enough sleep and daily movement improve your focus and self-control.

## Procrastination and studying: tips before exams

- Make a realistic study timetable with specific hours, not "I'll study a lot".
- Start with your hardest subject in your first session of the day.
- Study with a serious classmate in a library or another quiet place.
- Use [effective study methods](/en/articles/effective-study-methods/) such as active recall and spaced repetition.

## When is procrastination a sign of something bigger?

If procrastination is severe and constant, and comes with persistent sadness, strong anxiety or lifelong difficulty concentrating, it may be linked to something that needs professional assessment, such as depression, anxiety or ADHD. In that case, talking to a doctor or psychologist is a wise step.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I stop procrastinating for good?

No method gets rid of it forever; everyone procrastinates sometimes. But breaking tasks down, the two-minute rule and removing distractions reduce it a lot over time.

### What's the difference between procrastination and laziness?

Laziness is not wanting to make an effort at all. A procrastinator wants to get things done and feels guilty about delaying, but is escaping the negative feelings tied to the task.

### Is procrastination a mental illness?

Procrastination on its own isn't a mental illness; it's a common behaviour. But when it's severe and persistent, it can be a symptom that accompanies some mental health conditions.

## Key takeaway

Procrastination is mainly an escape from unpleasant feelings tied to a task, not just poor time management. To beat it, break the task down, start with two minutes, try Pomodoro, move your phone away, set a clear time and place, and forgive yourself when you slip.
