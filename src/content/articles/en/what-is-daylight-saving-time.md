Twice a year, the oven clock and the phone in your pocket stop agreeing, and someone in the house has to work out which one is right. Behind that small annoyance is daylight saving time. So what is daylight saving time? **It means moving the official clock forward one hour in spring and summer, then back again in autumn.** The idea is more than a century old, and people are still arguing about whether it's worth it.

## What is daylight saving time?

In the UK and much of Europe it's simply called "summer time". When the clocks go back in autumn, the country returns to **standard time**, often nicknamed "winter time".

The reasoning goes like this. In summer the sun comes up very early, so an hour or more of morning light passes while most people are still asleep. Shift the clocks forward and that hour of daylight effectively moves to the evening, when people are awake, out of school or work, and able to use it.

It's worth being clear about what doesn't change. Moving the clock hands doesn't make the day any longer or shorter. Day length is decided by Earth's tilt and its path around the Sun. Clock time is just a human agreement, and changing it changes the numbers, not the sunlight.

## Who invented daylight saving time?

Benjamin Franklin often gets the credit, but his famous 1784 essay was a joke. He suggested Parisians simply get out of bed earlier to save on candles, and never proposed touching the clocks. The real idea took shape much later:

| Year | Milestone |
| --- | --- |
| 1784 | Franklin's satirical essay about rising early to save candles |
| 1895 | New Zealand scientist George Vernon Hudson proposes shifting clocks in summer |
| 1907 | British builder William Willett publishes his pamphlet *The Waste of Daylight* |
| 1916 | Germany becomes the first country to adopt DST, during World War I, to save fuel; Britain follows weeks later |
| 1918 | The United States adopts daylight saving time |

> **Did you know?** William Willett, the idea's best-known champion in Britain, died in 1915, just one year before his country finally put the clocks forward.

## Why do we change the clocks?

It's telling that the first country to adopt the scheme did so in the middle of a war. The original goal was saving energy: lighter evenings meant less artificial lighting, and so less coal and fuel. Over time other arguments joined it. Supporters point to more usable daylight after work for shopping, sport and time outdoors, fewer people travelling home in the dark, and keeping schedules lined up with trading partners that follow the same system.

The world has changed since 1916, though. Homes now run air conditioning, screens and all kinds of devices, and lighting is a much smaller share of electricity use than it once was. As a result, researchers disagree about how much energy DST really saves, and several countries and regions keep debating whether to drop the twice-yearly switch altogether.

## When do the clocks go back in 2026?

Not everyone changes on the same day. These are the main "fall back" dates this year:

| Region | Clocks go back one hour |
| --- | --- |
| European Union and UK | Early Sunday, 25 October 2026 (at 01:00 UTC) |
| Egypt | Midnight at the end of Thursday, 29 October 2026 (12:00 becomes 11:00 pm) |
| Most of the United States | 2:00 am on Sunday, 1 November 2026 |

Because of that staggered timing, for the week between 25 October and 1 November the gap between London and New York shrinks from five hours to four, which regularly catches out people with transatlantic calls.

In Egypt the rules come from Law No. 24 of 2023: summer time starts on the last Friday of April and ends at the end of the last Thursday of October. In the US, Hawaii and most of Arizona don't change their clocks at all.

As for that disagreement in the kitchen: most smartphones update themselves if automatic date and time is switched on. Oven clocks, wall clocks and older gadgets know nothing about the law and have to be changed by hand.

## Countries that don't change the clocks

Most countries near the equator don't bother with DST, because their day length barely changes through the year, so there's no "wasted" morning light to move. In the Arab world, **Saudi Arabia** and **Qatar** don't use it, and Jordan and Syria announced in October 2022 that they would stop switching twice a year and keep one time all year round.

## Does changing the clocks affect your health?

If you feel a bit groggy or sleep badly for a few days after the change, you're not imagining it. Your body runs on an internal clock that can't be reset with a button, and it takes a few days to catch up. A few habits make the adjustment easier:

- shift your bedtime by about 15 minutes a day in the days before the change
- get some morning sunlight, which helps reset your body clock
- keep regular sleeping and waking times

## Questions students ask about the clock change

### What's the difference between daylight saving time and standard time?

Daylight saving time puts the clock one hour ahead during the warmer months. Standard time is the normal clock, which comes back when the hour is taken away again in autumn.

### Do clocks go forward or back in winter?

Back, by one hour, so you get an extra hour in bed on the night of the change. The easy way to remember it is "spring forward, fall back".

### Which country used daylight saving time first?

Germany, on 30 April 1916, during World War I, to save coal and energy.

### Will my phone change the time automatically?

Usually, yes, as long as automatic time is turned on and the system is up to date, because phone operating systems carry the time-zone rules for every country. That's why, in the oven-versus-phone standoff, the phone is almost always the one telling the truth.
