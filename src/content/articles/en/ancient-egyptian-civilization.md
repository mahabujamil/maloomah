Every time you write on paper or flip to a new month in a 365-day calendar, you're using ideas that trace back to the banks of the Nile. **Ancient Egyptian civilization** was one of the oldest and longest-lasting in the world. It flourished for about three thousand years and left behind pyramids, temples, a writing system and knowledge we still study. Below you'll find how it began, how historians divide its history, what it gave the world, and a revision table for the night before a test.

## What was ancient Egyptian civilization?

Ancient Egyptian civilization, often called **Pharaonic civilization** in Arabic, grew up in the Nile Valley from about 3100 BC. That was when King Narmer (identified by many historians with the legendary Menes) united Upper and Lower Egypt into a single state. From then on it lasted for many centuries, until Egypt became a Roman province in 30 BC. That's roughly three thousand years, though exact dates vary slightly between sources.

The word "pharaoh" comes from an ancient Egyptian phrase meaning "great house", the royal palace. Over time the name for the building came to mean the person living in it, much as a news report today might say "the White House announced" when it means the president.

## The Nile: the secret behind the civilization

The Greek historian Herodotus is often quoted as saying that "Egypt is the gift of the Nile". It makes sense once you remember that most of Egypt is desert. Every summer, though, the Nile flooded the land along its banks and then fell back, leaving a layer of rich black **silt** that turned dry ground into farmland.

In that silt the Egyptians grew wheat, barley and flax. They didn't rely on the flood alone: they dug canals and used the shaduf, a counterweighted pole and bucket, to lift water from the river to their fields. The Nile was also their main highway, carrying people, goods and huge blocks of stone by boat. Because so much depended on the water level, they built Nilometers to measure it and plan the farming season.

Even their year followed the river. It had three seasons: the flood, the growing season and the harvest.

## The periods of ancient Egypt

Historians divide ancient Egyptian history into major periods, the best known being three "kingdoms". The dates are approximate and vary slightly between sources:

| Period | Approximate dates | What it's known for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early Dynastic Period | c. 3100–2686 BC | Unification and the first dynasties |
| Old Kingdom | c. 2686–2181 BC | The age of the pyramid builders, including Khufu |
| Middle Kingdom | c. 2055–1650 BC | Farming, irrigation projects and literature |
| New Kingdom | c. 1550–1070 BC | Empire and great temples; kings include Ramesses II and Tutankhamun |
| Late and Ptolemaic periods | until 30 BC | Ptolemaic rule after Alexander, ending with Cleopatra VII |

You may notice gaps between the dates of the kingdoms. Those were times of weakness and division, when central power broke down for a while. Historians call them "intermediate periods".

## Achievements of ancient Egyptian civilization

### Hieroglyphs and papyrus

The ancient Egyptians developed **hieroglyphic writing**, a script of picture symbols carved on the walls of temples and tombs. Carving stone is slow, though, so they also needed something lighter. They made writing sheets from the papyrus plant that grew along the Nile, and the English word "paper" comes from its name.

### The solar calendar

Farmers needed to know when the flood was coming, and a reliable calendar helped. The Egyptians created a civil calendar of 365 days: twelve months of 30 days each, plus five extra days at the end of the year. It influenced the calendar introduced by Julius Caesar, the ancestor of the one on your wall.

### Engineering and architecture

The most famous Egyptian buildings are the pyramids. The largest, the Great Pyramid of Khufu at Giza, originally stood about 146 metres tall and was the tallest human-made structure for thousands of years. The Egyptians also built vast temples such as Karnak in Luxor and Abu Simbel, which Ramesses II had carved into a cliff. If you want to know how they moved all that stone, read our lesson [How were the pyramids built?](/en/articles/how-were-the-pyramids-built/).

### Medicine

Egyptian doctors were among the most skilled in the ancient world, and we know this because their writing survived. The Edwin Smith Papyrus describes treating wounds and fractures, while the Ebers Papyrus contains hundreds of remedies made from herbs and natural materials.

### Mathematics and astronomy

Each flood washed away the boundaries between fields, so the Egyptians used arithmetic and geometry to re-measure the land once the water went down. They used the same skills to plan their buildings. The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus is one of the most famous ancient maths documents, full of problems on fractions, areas and volumes that wouldn't look out of place in a school textbook. They watched the sky as well, and linked the dawn rising of Sirius with the coming of the flood.

### Mummification

Because they believed in life after death, the Egyptians became experts at **mummification**, preserving bodies from decay. That skill is why mummies of kings who lived thousands of years ago still exist.

## How did we learn the secrets of ancient Egypt?

For centuries people could see hieroglyphs everywhere and read none of them. Then in 1799 the **Rosetta Stone** was found near the town of Rosetta (Rashid) in Egypt. It carries one text written in three scripts: hieroglyphic, demotic and Greek. Scholars could already read Greek, so the stone worked a bit like an answer key. By comparing the three versions, the French scholar Jean-François Champollion deciphered hieroglyphs in 1822, and Egypt's history could finally be read in its own words.

A century later, in 1922, the British archaeologist Howard Carter discovered the tomb of King Tutankhamun in the Valley of the Kings at Luxor, almost intact with its treasures. Today almost all of Tutankhamun's treasures are displayed together for the first time at the Grand Egyptian Museum near the Giza pyramids, which officially opened on 1 November 2025.

> **Did you know?** In the 1960s the whole Abu Simbel temple was cut into huge blocks, moved and rebuilt on higher ground to save it from being flooded by the lake behind the Aswan High Dam. It was part of an international campaign led by UNESCO.

## Questions students ask about ancient Egypt

### What were the main achievements of ancient Egypt?

Hieroglyphic writing, papyrus, a 365-day solar calendar, the pyramids and great temples, medicine, mathematics, mummification and well-organised farming and irrigation along the Nile.

### What is the difference between hieroglyphic and demotic?

Hieroglyphs are picture symbols used mainly in temples and tombs. Demotic is a faster, shorthand script used for everyday business in later periods. That's why both appear on the Rosetta Stone: one for formal religious text, the other for what ordinary people actually read.

### Where can I see ancient Egyptian monuments today?

At many sites in Egypt, such as the Giza pyramids, the temples of Luxor, Aswan and Abu Simbel, and in museums, most famously the Egyptian Museum in Tahrir Square and the Grand Egyptian Museum.
