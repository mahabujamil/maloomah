How many continents are there in the world? Most schools teach **seven**: Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe and Australia. Ask a student in Mexico or Russia, though, and you may hear six, and they wouldn't be wrong by their own textbooks. To see why both answers exist, it helps to start with what a continent actually is, then rank the seven by size, and finally sort out the Old World and the New World.

## What is a continent?

A **continent** is a very large mass of land, usually surrounded by oceans and seas. That sounds clear enough until you try to use it. How big does a landmass have to be? And where does one continent stop and the next begin when they're joined together? Science doesn't give a single fixed answer, so the way we divide up the land depends partly on physical geography and partly on history and culture.

For a sense of scale: land covers only about 29% of Earth's surface. All seven continents together come to roughly 149 million square kilometres. The other 71% or so of the planet is water.

## How many continents are there, and what are their names?

In the most widely used model there are 7 continents. Here they are from largest to smallest. Treat the areas as approximate, since sources draw the borders a little differently:

| Rank | Continent | Approx. area (million km²) | Highest peak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Asia | about 44.6 | Everest (about 8,849 m) |
| 2 | Africa | about 30.3 | Kilimanjaro (about 5,895 m) |
| 3 | North America | about 24.2 | Denali (about 6,190 m) |
| 4 | South America | about 17.8 | Aconcagua (about 6,961 m) |
| 5 | Antarctica | about 14.2 | Vinson (about 4,892 m) |
| 6 | Europe | about 10 | Elbrus (about 5,642 m) |
| 7 | Australia | about 7.7 | Kosciuszko (about 2,228 m) |

You don't need to memorise every number. Fix the two ends first: Asia is the biggest, Australia the smallest. Africa comes second, the two Americas follow one after the other, then the frozen continent, and Europe sits just above Australia.

## A short profile of each continent

**Asia** is the giant. It is the largest continent and the most populated, with about 6 in every 10 people on Earth living there, including in enormous countries such as China and India. Mount Everest, the highest point on the planet, is here too.

Africa is second in both area and population, but first in number of countries, with 54. The Nile runs through it, and it holds the Sahara, the largest hot desert in the world.

North America reaches from the Arctic Ocean down through Central America to the Caribbean islands, taking in Canada, the United States and Mexico. Greenland, the world's largest island, belongs to it geographically. South of it lies South America, with the Amazon (the river that carries more water than any other), the Amazon rainforest, and the Andes, the longest mountain range on land.

Antarctica is the odd one out. It is the coldest, driest and windiest continent, and ice covers about 98% of it. Nobody lives there permanently; scientists stay at research stations, and the Antarctic Treaty, signed in 1959, reserves the continent for peaceful and scientific use.

Europe is one of the smallest continents by area, yet densely populated. No sea separates it from Asia. The boundary is the Ural Mountains and the Ural River, which is why some geographers count the two as one continent, **Eurasia**.

Australia is the smallest. When the Pacific islands such as New Zealand and Papua New Guinea are included, the region is often called **Oceania**. Australia is also the only country that takes up an entire continent.

> **Did you know?** Scientists believe that about 300 million years ago the continents were joined in one giant landmass called Pangaea. It began to break apart about 200 million years ago, and the continents are still moving today, a few centimetres a year, roughly as fast as your fingernails grow.

## Why the answer varies: 7, 6 or 5 continents?

Remember that the definition doesn't settle where one continent ends. North and South America are linked by a thin strip of land, and Europe and Asia are really one continuous landmass. If you think the connection matters more than the dividing line, you merge them; if not, you keep them apart. Different countries have made different choices:

| Model | Number | What changes? | Where it's mostly used |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Seven-continent model | 7 | Every continent counted separately | Most Arab curricula, English-speaking countries and China |
| Six-continent model | 6 | North and South America combined as "America" | Much of Latin America and parts of Europe |
| Six-continent model (Eurasia) | 6 | Europe and Asia combined as "Eurasia" | Russia and parts of Eastern Europe |
| Inhabited-continents model | 5 | The Americas combined, Antarctica left out as uninhabited | Reflected in the five Olympic rings |

So on a test, write seven: that's the expected answer in most curricula. Knowing the other models is still useful, because a foreign atlas or a map from another country won't look wrong to you.

## Old World and New World continents

This one comes up in exams a lot, and it's really a history question. **The Old World is three continents: Asia, Africa and Europe.** Their peoples knew about one another and traded for thousands of years, and the oldest civilisations grew up there, such as [ancient Egypt](/en/articles/ancient-egyptian-civilization/), Mesopotamia and China.

The New World means North America and South America. "New" only meant new to Europeans: Europe learned of these lands after Christopher Columbus arrived in 1492, while Indigenous peoples had already lived there for thousands of years.

Australia is sometimes called the "Newest World", because Europeans reached it later still. Antarctica was the last continent to be discovered, first sighted in the 19th century.

## Continents and oceans: how water separates the land

Five oceans lie between the continents. From largest to smallest: the Pacific, the Atlantic, the Indian Ocean, the Southern Ocean and the Arctic Ocean. The Pacific alone is bigger than all the land on Earth combined.

Water doesn't cut every continent off completely, though. Some meet through narrow land bridges: Asia and Africa at the Sinai Peninsula, and the two Americas at the Isthmus of Panama. Those bridges are exactly what the six-or-seven argument is about.

## Questions students ask about continents

### What are the largest and smallest continents?

Asia is the largest, at about 44.6 million km², and also the most populated. Australia is the smallest, at about 7.7 million km².

### Which continent has no permanent population?

Antarctica. Scientists and researchers stay there for limited periods at research stations, and their numbers change between summer and winter.

### Which continents are Arab countries in?

Two: Asia (for example Saudi Arabia, Jordan, Iraq and Syria) and Africa (for example Egypt, Morocco, Algeria and Sudan). Egypt is one of the few countries that spans two continents, because the Sinai Peninsula is in Asia.

### Which continent has the most countries?

Africa, with 54, followed by Asia and then Europe.
