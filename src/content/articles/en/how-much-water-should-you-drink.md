Water is essential for life, and it makes up a large share of our body weight. But **how much water should you drink a day?** Is it the famous 8 glasses, 2 litres, or more? Here's what health authorities say, and how to tell whether you're drinking enough.

## How much water does your body need?

The **US National Academies of Sciences** set an approximate adequate daily intake of **total water** for healthy adults:

| Group | Approximate total daily water |
| --- | --- |
| Men | About 3.7 litres |
| Women | About 2.7 litres |

The key word is **total**. These amounts include **all drinks and foods**, not just plain water. About **20%** typically comes from food, especially fruit and vegetables such as cucumber and watermelon. The rest comes from water and other drinks such as milk, tea and juice.

## Is "8 glasses a day" right?

The **"8 glasses a day"** rule, about 2 litres, is easy to remember and a helpful reminder for many people. But it **isn't a precise scientific rule** for everyone. Your needs change with:

- **Climate:** in hot weather you lose a lot of water through sweat.
- **Physical activity:** exercise and hard physical work raise your needs considerably.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** both increase fluid needs.
- **Illness:** fever, vomiting or diarrhoea make the body lose extra fluid.

## How much water by body weight?

A common rule of thumb is **about 30 to 35 ml per kilogram of body weight** per day.

For example, a 70 kg person would need about **2.1 to 2.5 litres** of fluid a day by this rule.

It's only a **general estimate**, and it goes up with heat and exercise. People with kidney or heart conditions may be given different limits by their doctor and should follow that advice.

> **Did you know?** Water makes up about **50 to 60%** of an adult's body weight, and an even higher share in newborn babies.

## Signs you're not drinking enough

The simplest practical check is **urine colour**. **Pale yellow** usually means you're well hydrated, while **dark yellow** can mean you need more water. Other signs of dehydration include:

- Strong thirst and a dry mouth
- Urinating less often
- Tiredness and headaches
- Dizziness, especially when standing up

Thirst is a signal from your body, so don't ignore it. **Older adults**, however, may not feel thirsty easily, so they benefit from regular reminders to drink.

## Can you drink too much water?

Yes, but it's rare. Drinking **very large amounts** in a short time can lower the **sodium** level in your blood, which is dangerous. It sometimes happens to endurance athletes who drink huge volumes without replacing salts. For a healthy person drinking to thirst, it's not normally a risk.

## Tips to drink enough

- Carry a **water bottle** wherever you go.
- Start the day with **a glass of water** after waking up.
- Drink a glass with every meal.
- Eat plenty of **water-rich fruit and vegetables**.
- Drink more in **summer**, and before, during and after exercise.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many litres of water should I drink a day?

References put total daily water at about 3.7 litres for men and 2.7 litres for women, from all drinks and foods. That usually works out at roughly 2 to 3 litres of drinks.

### Do tea and coffee count?

Yes, they contribute to your daily fluids. Water is still the best choice, because it has no calories or sugar.

### How much water is in the human body?

Water is about 50 to 60% of an adult's weight. A 70 kg person carries roughly 40 litres of water.

### Does drinking water help with weight loss?

Swapping sugary drinks for water cuts calories, and drinking water before a meal may help you feel full. On its own it isn't a magic solution.

## Key takeaway

Adults generally need about **3.7 litres of total water for men and 2.7 for women** a day, from water, other drinks and food combined. Needs rise in **heat and with exercise**. The easiest check that you're drinking enough is **pale yellow urine**.
