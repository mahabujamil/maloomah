Get far enough from city lights on a clear, moonless night, give your eyes twenty minutes to adjust, and you'll see a faint, cloudy band stretching across the sky. That band is the **Milky Way galaxy**, the galaxy we live in. Here's the strange part: we never see it from the outside, the way it appears in textbook pictures. We see it from within, because the Sun and Earth are part of it. Its huge flat disc looks like a stripe to us for the same reason a row of classmates looks like a single line when you stand in the middle of it.

## What is the Milky Way galaxy?

A galaxy is an enormous collection of stars, gas and dust, held together by gravity and turning around a shared centre. Ours is one of billions of galaxies in the universe, and it's home: the Sun, the eight planets and everything else in the solar system belong to it.

Astronomers classify the Milky Way as a **barred spiral galaxy**. In the middle there's a long, bar-shaped crowd of stars, and spiral arms curl out from its ends and wind around the centre, a bit like water swirling down a sink. No spacecraft has ever travelled far enough to photograph our galaxy from outside, so every picture you see of the whole thing is either an artist's illustration built from measurements or a photo of a similar galaxy somewhere else.

## Where does the name "Milky Way" come from?

The ancient Greeks and Romans thought the pale band looked like spilled milk, so they called it the milky road. The Greek word for milk, *gala*, also gave us the word "galaxy".

Arabic has its own picture for the same sight. The usual Arabic name, *Darb al-Tabbana*, means roughly "the path of the straw carriers". The common explanation is that people compared the glowing band to the trail of straw that falls from animals carrying loads of it along a road. Two cultures looked at the same faint light and each saw something from everyday life.

## How big is the Milky Way, and how many stars does it have?

These distances are far too big for kilometres, so astronomers use the **light-year**: the distance light travels in one year, about 9.46 trillion kilometres.

| Measure | Approximate value |
| --- | --- |
| Width of the disc | About 100,000 light-years |
| Thickness of the disc | About 1,000 light-years |
| Number of stars | Roughly 100 to 400 billion |
| Sun's distance from the centre | About 26,000 light-years |
| Time for the Sun to orbit the centre | About 230 million years |

Notice that the star count is a wide range rather than one number. Nobody counts stars one by one. Scientists estimate the galaxy's mass and work out a likely number of stars from it, and many stars are too dim to see or are hidden behind clouds of dust.

How many planets are there? Nobody knows exactly. Astronomers have already found thousands of planets orbiting other stars and think most stars have planets of their own, which would put the Milky Way's total in the hundreds of billions.

## Where is Earth in the Milky Way?

The Sun isn't at the centre. It sits in a fairly quiet neighbourhood about 26,000 light-years out, inside a small partial arm called the Orion Arm, or Orion Spur, which lies between two larger arms. Being away from the crowded middle is good for us, since the region near the centre is packed with stars and harsh radiation.

The Sun isn't standing still either. It carries all the planets with it around the galactic centre, and one full lap takes about 230 million years, a stretch some astronomers call a "galactic year". The last time the Sun was where it is now, it was roughly the time the first dinosaurs appeared.

> **Did you know?** The light from some stars in the Milky Way's band left them thousands of years ago. When you look at them, you're seeing what they looked like back then, not what they look like tonight. Sunlight does the same thing on a much smaller scale, as explained in [how long sunlight takes to reach Earth](/en/articles/how-long-does-sunlight-take-to-reach-earth/).

## What is at the centre of the Milky Way?

Right at the heart of the galaxy sits a **supermassive black hole** called Sagittarius A* (say "Sagittarius A-star"). It has about 4 million times the mass of the Sun packed into a small space. Scientists first knew it was there because stars near the centre whip around a point where nothing can be seen. Then, on 12 May 2022, the Event Horizon Telescope team released the first image of it: a glowing ring around a dark shadow, made from data gathered in 2017 by linking radio telescopes on several continents.

There's nothing to worry about. It's extremely far away and isn't going to swallow the Sun or Earth. If you want to know what a black hole actually is and how one forms, read [what is a black hole](/en/articles/what-is-a-black-hole/).

## Our galactic neighbours, including Andromeda

The Milky Way belongs to a small family of galaxies called the Local Group. The biggest member is the **Andromeda Galaxy**, about 2.5 million light-years away. Under very dark skies you can spot it without a telescope as a small smudge of light, which makes it one of the most distant things a human eye can see unaided. Smaller satellite galaxies orbit the Milky Way too. The best known are the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, visible from the Southern Hemisphere.

For years, books said Andromeda would definitely crash into the Milky Way in a few billion years. A study published in 2025, using data from the Hubble Space Telescope and the Gaia spacecraft, found the chance of the two galaxies merging in the next 10 billion years is only about 50%. So it's closer to a coin toss than a certainty, and either way it's unimaginably far in the future.

## Questions students ask about the Milky Way

### Can you see the Milky Way from a city?

Usually not. Street and building lights drown out its faint glow. It shows up clearly in dark places far from towns, especially on nights without a Moon. In the Northern Hemisphere, its brightest part is best seen on summer nights.

### Is the Milky Way an elliptical galaxy?

No, it's a barred spiral. Elliptical galaxies are a different type: egg-shaped, with no arms, and mostly filled with old stars.

### What's the difference between a galaxy and the solar system?

The solar system is one star, the Sun, plus the planets, moons and asteroids that orbit it. A galaxy holds hundreds of billions of stars, and the Sun is just one of them. Think of the solar system as one small house in a huge city called the Milky Way.
