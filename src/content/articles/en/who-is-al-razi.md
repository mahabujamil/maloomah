Ask a class "Who was al-Razi?" and you may get several different answers, because more than one famous scholar carried that name. In science and history-of-medicine lessons, the one meant is **Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi**, a physician and chemist of the 9th and 10th centuries whom medieval Europe called Rhazes. He was one of the great doctors of his age, wrote the first book known to separate smallpox from measles clearly, and left behind an enormous medical encyclopedia called *al-Hawi*.

## His life in brief

Al-Razi was born around 864 CE (about 250 AH) in the city of Rayy, near present-day Tehran, and his name simply means "the man from Rayy". Biographers say he spent his youth on music and alchemy and came to medicine fairly late, then rose to become the leading physician of his time.

He ran the hospital in Rayy, and the Abbasid caliph al-Muktafi later called him to Baghdad to head the city's largest hospital. When al-Muktafi died in 907 he went home. He was also a teacher with many students. One account describes him sitting surrounded by pupils in rings: a new patient was seen first by the most junior, and passed inwards only when they were stuck, until the case reached al-Razi himself.

His eyesight failed badly in old age, and some accounts say he went blind. He died around 925 CE (313 AH), most likely in Rayy, although sources disagree on both the year and the place.

## What he did for medicine

What sets al-Razi apart is less the number of his books than the way he worked. He watched patients closely, recorded their symptoms day by day, compared cases and refused to accept an idea just because a famous name stood behind it.

### Telling smallpox from measles

His treatise *On Smallpox and Measles* is the oldest known book to describe both diseases carefully and explain how they differ. Doctors before him often confused them, since both begin with fever and a rash. The treatise was translated into Latin and other European languages and printed many times.

### Testing a treatment against no treatment

In *al-Hawi* he notes that he treated one group of patients in a certain way and deliberately left another group untreated, to see whether the treatment really helped. Comparing treated and untreated patients is the basic idea behind how medicines are tested today, even though the particular treatment he tried is no longer used.

### Spring colds and roses

A short treatise attributed to him asks why some people catch a cold in spring when they smell roses. Historians of medicine count it among the earliest descriptions of what we now call seasonal allergy, or hay fever.

### Children and medical ethics

He wrote on childhood illnesses, and also on how a good doctor should behave: honestly, carefully and in the patient's interest. He warned readers against quacks who cheat people in medicine's name.

> **Did you know?** A popular story says al-Razi chose the site of a Baghdad hospital by hanging pieces of meat in different districts and picking the one where the meat rotted slowest, because its air must be cleanest. Historians doubt it: the story is usually linked to the Adudi hospital, which was founded more than fifty years after his death.

## His books: al-Hawi and al-Mansuri

Al-Razi wrote dozens of books and treatises on medicine, chemistry and philosophy. Three stand out:

| Book | What it contains | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Al-Hawi* (The Comprehensive Book) | A vast collection of notes, readings and patient cases gathered over his whole career | Assembled by his students after his death; translated into Latin in 1279 as *Continens* |
| *Al-Mansuri* | A shorter, well-organised textbook | Dedicated around 903 to al-Mansur ibn Ishaq, governor of Rayy, hence the title |
| *On Smallpox and Measles* | A short treatise on the two diseases | One of his best-known works in Europe |

European medical schools taught from his books for centuries, alongside the *Canon* of [Ibn Sina](/en/articles/who-is-ibn-sina/), who came about a century after him.

## Al-Razi the chemist

Before medicine, al-Razi's passion was chemistry, which in his day was tangled up with alchemy, the dream of turning cheap metals into gold. He was far more interested in the practical side. In his *Book of Secrets* he described the equipment a laboratory needs, such as the alembic, flasks and furnaces, and explained processes like distillation, calcination and filtration. He listed the materials first, then the apparatus, then the method, which is close to how students write up a school experiment today.

He also sorted substances into mineral, vegetable and animal groups, plus derived substances that people prepare themselves. It sounds simple now, but it was a step towards treating materials according to their properties rather than the legends attached to them.

## Not to be confused with Fakhr al-Din al-Razi

Searching for "al-Razi" brings up several people:

- Abu Bakr al-Razi (about 864–925 CE): the physician and chemist in this lesson.
- Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (1149–1209 CE): a theologian and Quran commentator, author of the large commentary *Mafatih al-Ghayb*, who lived about three centuries later.
- Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr al-Razi: compiler of *Mukhtar al-Sihah*, an Arabic dictionary many students still use.

The reason is the name itself: "al-Razi" just means someone from Rayy, and the city produced scholars in many fields. If the question comes up in a science or health lesson, it almost certainly means Abu Bakr the physician.

## Questions students ask about al-Razi

### Why is he called a great physician?

Because he combined careful observation, experiment and clear writing. He separated smallpox from measles, compared treated and untreated patients, and left *al-Hawi*, which doctors in both the Islamic world and Europe used for centuries.

### How is he different from Ibn Sina?

Both were great Muslim physicians, but al-Razi came about a century earlier and was more of a hands-on clinician who recorded his own cases. Ibn Sina was more of an organiser who brought medicine together in one systematic book, the *Canon*. If you enjoy scientists' lives, [Ibn al-Haytham](/en/articles/ibn-al-haytham/) is another scholar who relied on experiment; he was born about forty years after al-Razi died.

### How should I structure a school project on him?

Introduce him and his birthplace, give one section to medicine and another to chemistry, add a table of his books, and finish with his influence in Europe. Short facts from the [inventions and scientists "Did you know?" page](/en/did-you-know/inventions/) make good extras.
