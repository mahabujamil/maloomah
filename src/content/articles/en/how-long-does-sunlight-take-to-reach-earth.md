Watch a sunset from a balcony or the school playground (without staring straight at the Sun) and you are looking at the past. The Sun you see isn't the Sun as it is right now. So how long does sunlight take to reach Earth? **About 8 minutes and 20 seconds.** Light is unimaginably fast, but space is very big, and even light needs time to cross it.

## How long does sunlight take to reach Earth? Doing the sum

You don't have to take this number on trust. It comes from a single division, and you only need two facts.

The first is distance. On average, the centre of the Earth is about 149.6 million kilometres from the centre of the Sun. Astronomers call this distance one **astronomical unit** (AU) and use it as a ruler for the solar system. The second is speed: light in a vacuum covers about 299,792 kilometres every second.

Divide one by the other:

149,600,000 ÷ 299,792 ≈ 499 seconds, which is roughly 8 minutes and 19 seconds.

Because Earth's distance from the Sun shifts during the year (more on that below), you'll often see the figure rounded to 8 minutes 20 seconds. Both are fair approximations.

## Sunlight travel time to each planet

The same sum works for every planet, and the further out you go, the longer the wait. These times use each planet's average distance from the Sun, so they're approximate and change a little as each planet moves along its orbit:

| Planet | Average distance from the Sun | Approx. sunlight travel time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mercury | 0.39 AU | 3.2 minutes |
| Venus | 0.72 AU | 6 minutes |
| Earth | 1 AU | 8.3 minutes |
| Mars | 1.52 AU | 12.7 minutes |
| Jupiter | 5.2 AU | 43 minutes |
| Saturn | 9.5 AU | 1 hour 19 minutes |
| Uranus | 19.2 AU | 2 hours 40 minutes |
| Neptune | 30.1 AU | 4 hours 10 minutes |

Look at the spread. Light that reaches Mercury in about three minutes needs more than four hours to get to Neptune, roughly the length of a school morning.

Pluto sits further out still. It's a dwarf planet rather than one of the eight, and sunlight takes about 5.5 hours on average to get there. If you're wondering why it lost its planet status, read [how many planets are in the solar system](/en/articles/how-many-planets-in-the-solar-system/).

## Why the number changes during the year

If Earth's orbit were a perfect circle, the answer would be the same every day. It isn't. The orbit is a slightly stretched ellipse, so we drift a little closer to the Sun for part of the year and a little further away for the rest, and the light's travel time changes by a few seconds:

| Position | Approximate date | Distance | Light travel time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Closest point (perihelion) | Early January | 147.1 million km | about 8 min 11 s |
| Average | — | 149.6 million km | about 8 min 19 s |
| Farthest point (aphelion) | Early July | 152.1 million km | about 8 min 27 s |

So the real figure swings between roughly 8 min 11 s in January and 8 min 27 s in July.

> **Did you know?** Earth is closest to the Sun in January, in the middle of the northern hemisphere's winter. Seasons are caused by the tilt of Earth's axis, not by our distance from the Sun.

## Everything in the sky is an old picture

This is where the idea gets strange. If light needs time to arrive, then nothing in the sky is seen as it is now, only as it was when the light set off. The Moon is close, so the delay is tiny: you see it as it was about 1.3 seconds ago. The Sun you see is about 8 minutes old. Proxima Centauri, the nearest star after the Sun, appears as it was about 4.2 years ago, so the starlight reaching you tonight left it around the time you were four school years younger. Distant stars and galaxies show up as they were millions or even billions of years ago.

That's why astronomers describe big telescopes as a kind of time machine. The further they look, the further back in time they see.

## What if the Sun suddenly disappeared?

Students love this one, and the answer tends to surprise them: for about 8 minutes, we wouldn't notice a thing. Light that had already left the Sun would keep arriving, and the day would carry on as normal. Earth would even keep following its orbit for those minutes, because according to relativity, changes in gravity also travel at the speed of light. Our planet wouldn't "feel" the Sun was gone until the last of its light arrived.

## Light spends far longer inside the Sun

Those eight minutes are only the final stretch. Energy is produced in the Sun's core by nuclear fusion, and getting out to the surface is painfully slow, because it is absorbed and re-emitted countless times in the dense inner layers. Picture someone trying to leave a packed hall where every person they bump into sends them off in a new direction. Studies estimate this trip from the core takes tens of thousands of years, possibly more.

Put another way, the light warming your face today started out in the heart of the Sun before any city had been built on Earth.

## Questions students ask about sunlight and the speed of light

### Does sunlight reach every planet at the same time?

No. It reaches Mercury, the closest planet, in about 3 minutes, but needs more than 4 hours to reach Neptune.

### What is a "light-year"?

The name tricks a lot of people into thinking it measures time. It's actually a distance: how far light travels in one year, about 9.46 trillion kilometres.

### What is the speed of light per second?

Light in a vacuum travels at exactly **299,792,458 metres per second**, about 300,000 km per second, which students usually write as 3 × 10⁸ m/s. At that speed, light could go around the Earth about 7.5 times in one second.

### How does the speed of light compare with the speed of sound?

Sound moves through air at about 343 metres per second (at 20 °C), which makes light roughly 874,000 times faster. That's why you see a lightning flash before you hear the thunder, and the longer the gap, the further away the storm. For more facts, visit the [space "Did you know?" page](/en/did-you-know/space/).
