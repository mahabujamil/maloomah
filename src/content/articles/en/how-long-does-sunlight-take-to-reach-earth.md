When you look at the setting Sun (without staring straight at it!), you are not seeing it as it is right now, but as it was about eight minutes ago. That is not a trick: light is incredibly fast, but it still needs time to cross the distance.

## The short answer: about 8 minutes and 20 seconds

On average, the centre of the Earth is about **149.6 million kilometres** from the centre of the Sun. Astronomers call this distance one "astronomical unit".

The speed of light in a vacuum is about **299,792 kilometres per second**. Divide the distance by the speed and you get:

149,600,000 ÷ 299,792 ≈ **499 seconds**, or roughly **8 minutes and 19 seconds**.

## Why does the number change during the year?

Earth's orbit is not a perfect circle but a slightly stretched ellipse, so our distance from the Sun changes over the year:

| Position | Approximate date | Distance | Light travel time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Closest point (perihelion) | Early January | 147.1 million km | about 8 min 11 s |
| Average | — | 149.6 million km | about 8 min 19 s |
| Farthest point (aphelion) | Early July | 152.1 million km | about 8 min 27 s |

> **Did you know?** Earth is closest to the Sun in January, in the middle of the northern hemisphere's winter. Seasons are caused by the tilt of Earth's axis, not by our distance from the Sun.

## We are always looking at the past

Because light takes time to travel, everything we see in the sky is a picture from the past:

- **The Moon:** as it was about **1.3 seconds** ago.
- **The Sun:** as it was about **8 minutes** ago.
- **The nearest star after the Sun (Proxima Centauri):** as it was about **4.2 years** ago.
- **Distant stars and galaxies:** as they were millions or even billions of years ago.

That is why astronomers say large telescopes are a kind of time machine.

## What if the Sun suddenly disappeared?

A fun question many people ask. The answer: we would not notice anything for about 8 minutes! Light that had already left the Sun would keep arriving, and Earth would keep following its orbit for that time too, because according to relativity, changes in gravity also travel at the speed of light.

## A much longer journey inside the Sun

Those eight minutes are only the last leg of light's trip. Energy is produced in the Sun's core by nuclear fusion, and it takes a very long time to work its way out to the surface, because it is absorbed and re-emitted countless times in the dense layers. Studies estimate this inner journey takes **tens of thousands of years**, possibly more.

In other words, the light warming your face today began its journey in the heart of the Sun before any city was built on Earth.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does sunlight reach every planet at the same time?

No. It reaches Mercury, the closest planet, in about 3 minutes, but needs more than 4 hours to reach Neptune.

### What is a "light-year"?

It is a unit of distance, not time: the distance light travels in one year, about 9.46 trillion kilometres.

## Key takeaway

Sunlight takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach Earth, ranging from roughly 8 min 11 s to 8 min 27 s depending on where Earth is in its orbit. Every time you look at the sky, remember you are seeing light from the past.
