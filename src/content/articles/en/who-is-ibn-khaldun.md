Ask a history teacher to name the greatest Arab historian and you will very often hear one name: **Ibn Khaldun**. So who was Ibn Khaldun? He was a fourteenth-century scholar born in Tunis who wrote the *Muqaddimah*, a book that many researchers believe laid the foundations of **sociology** centuries before the subject took shape in Europe. What set him apart was a simple habit of mind. Where other historians recorded what happened, he kept asking why.

## Who was Ibn Khaldun? Quick facts

## A childhood in Tunis, cut short by the plague

Ibn Khaldun was born in Tunis in 1332 into a family known for scholarship and public office. The family had already moved a long way to get there. Its roots were in Yemen, it had lived for centuries in Seville, in al-Andalus, and it crossed to North Africa before Seville fell to the Spanish kingdoms in the thirteenth century.

As a boy he studied with his father and with the scholars of Tunis. He memorised the Quran, learned hadith, law, Arabic and poetry, and went on to logic and philosophy. Then, when he was about seventeen, the Black Death swept across the Mediterranean in 1348. It killed both his parents and many of his teachers. Picture a student of secondary-school age losing his family and his teachers in the same year. That loss stayed with him, and it shaped the way he later thought about how states and societies rise, decline and change.

## Life between the Maghreb, al-Andalus and Egypt

Ibn Khaldun was not a scholar who stayed quietly among his books. He moved from court to court and served several rulers, which let him watch politics from the inside.

His career began in Fez, Morocco, where he worked as a secretary and adviser to the Marinid sultans. In Granada, the sultan sent him in 1364 as an envoy to the King of Castile in Seville, the very city his ancestors had once called home. After that he held administrative and political posts in Bejaia and the central Maghreb, in what is now Algeria.

The turning point came when he stepped away from politics. For about four years he lived at **Qal'at Ibn Salama**, also in modern Algeria, and there he wrote the *Muqaddimah*, finishing the first draft in 1377. In 1382 he moved to Cairo. He taught at al-Azhar and other colleges, and the Mamluk sultan Barquq appointed him chief Maliki judge, a post he held several times until his death. He kept revising and adding to the *Muqaddimah* in Egypt.

One episode reads almost like fiction. In 1401 he met the Mongol-Turkic conqueror Timur (Tamerlane) outside Damascus, while Timur's armies were besieging the city, and he recorded the meeting in detail in his autobiography.

## The Muqaddimah: the book that made Ibn Khaldun famous

His great work was a huge history of the world called *Kitab al-'Ibar* ("The Book of Lessons"). He opened it with a long introduction explaining his method for understanding history and society, and that introduction became so famous that it is now read as a book in its own right. Its name, *Muqaddimah*, simply means "Introduction".

Why was it new? Many earlier historians had written chronicles: who ruled, when, and who won which battle. Ibn Khaldun wanted to know why states rise and flourish, why they weaken and fall, and how the environment, the economy and customs shape the way people live. It's the difference between memorising the dates of wars and understanding what caused them. He called this field **'ilm al-'umran**, "the science of human civilisation", which roughly matches what we now call sociology. Because he studied human society this systematically, about four centuries before modern sociology appeared in Europe, he is often called one of its founders.

> **Did you know?** The British historian Arnold Toynbee called the *Muqaddimah* "a philosophy of history which is undoubtedly the greatest work of its kind that has ever yet been created by any mind in any time or place."

## Ibn Khaldun's key ideas made simple

The idea most linked to his name is ***asabiyyah***, usually translated as group solidarity. It is the bond of loyalty and cooperation that holds a group together and makes its members defend one another. Think of a school team that beats a more talented side because its players stick together. For Ibn Khaldun, this kind of bond was the force that builds states.

That leads to his view of the life cycle of states. A state, he argued, passes through stages much like a human life: birth, strength, luxury and decline, often over about three generations. He tied this to the contrast between desert and city life. Desert peoples, in his view, tend to be tougher and more united, while city dwellers have more learning and crafts. The danger is luxury, which slowly weakens a society.

He was also strict about evidence. A historian, he said, shouldn't believe every report that reaches him, but should test it against the nature of things and ask whether it could really have happened. That is good advice for anyone reading viral posts on a phone today. On economics, he saw human labour as the real source of wealth and earnings, and he warned that when taxes become too heavy people produce less, so in the end the state collects less, not more.

One of his most quoted lines, which many students look up, is the title of a chapter in the *Muqaddimah*: "The vanquished always want to imitate the victor in his distinctive marks, his dress, his beliefs and his other customs and habits." Put simply, defeated peoples tend to copy the stronger side, believing its strength comes from those habits.

He wrote about language as well, arguing that it is a skill acquired through practice and repetition, as you'll find among our [Did You Know facts about Arabic](/en/did-you-know/arabic/).

## His books and his place in the world now

Students are often asked to name his books. The best known are *Kitab al-'Ibar*, with its famous introduction, the *Muqaddimah*, and his autobiography, *al-Ta'rif bi-Ibn Khaldun*. Other works on law and logic are also attributed to him.

His ideas are still taught at universities around the world in history, sociology and economics, and the *Muqaddimah* has been translated into many languages, including English and French. Many researchers see him as a pioneer of sociology and one of the earliest writers on the philosophy of history and on economics. In Tunis his statue stands on the city's best-known avenue, and schools, universities and research centres in many countries carry his name.
