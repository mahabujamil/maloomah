Every hour on the classroom clock has 60 minutes, and every minute has 60 seconds. Nobody chose those numbers last century. They go back thousands of years to a flat, hot plain between two rivers, and that plain is today's subject: **Mesopotamian civilization**, one of the oldest in the world. The first known writing appeared there, along with some of the first large cities and one of the most famous written law codes in history. This lesson covers where Mesopotamia was, the peoples who ruled it in turn, and the achievements we still live with.

## Where was Mesopotamia, and what does the name mean?

"Mesopotamia" is a Greek word meaning "the land between the rivers". The two rivers are the **Tigris and the Euphrates**, and Arabic has the same idea in its name for the region, *Bilad al-Rafidayn*, "the land of the two rivers".

Most of that land lies in modern Iraq, with its edges reaching into parts of Syria and Turkey. Both rivers rise in the mountains of Anatolia to the north and flow south-east until they meet at the Shatt al-Arab and empty into the Gulf.

Why did civilization start here? Picture a flat, hot land with little rain, crossed by two big rivers that flood in spring and leave rich silt behind. People who learned to dig canals and steer that water onto their fields could grow wheat, barley and date palms, and produce more food than they needed. That surplus let them settle in villages, then cities, and let some people stop farming altogether to become craftsmen, traders, scribes and officials.

## Who ruled Mesopotamia, in order

Mesopotamia was never one country for its whole history. Peoples and kingdoms followed one another, and learning the order does half the work of the lesson:

| Order | People or state | Approximate dates | Best known for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Sumerians | from about 3500 BCE | the first cities, such as Uruk and Ur, and cuneiform writing |
| 2 | Akkadians | about 2334 BCE | Sargon of Akkad and the first large empire |
| 3 | Old Babylonians | about 1792–1750 BCE (Hammurabi's reign) | Hammurabi's laws |
| 4 | Assyrians | strongest from the 9th to the 7th century BCE | a powerful army and Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh |
| 5 | Neo-Babylonians (Chaldeans) | 612–539 BCE | Nebuchadnezzar II and the Ishtar Gate |

The **Sumerians** lived in the south and built independent city-states, each with its own ruler and a great temple. Uruk, which historians count among the world's first large cities, and Ur are the best known. These cities were rivals, and they fought one another for centuries.

Around 2334 BCE **Sargon of Akkad** brought the southern and northern cities under his rule, creating what many historians consider the first true empire. Centuries later the city of Babylon rose, reaching its first peak under King Hammurabi.

The Assyrians ruled from the north; their most famous capital, Nineveh, lies near today's Mosul. After Assyria fell in 612 BCE, Babylon became powerful again, until the Persian king Cyrus took the city in 539 BCE. That conquest ended rule by Mesopotamia's own kingdoms.

## Cuneiform: Mesopotamia's greatest gift

The oldest known writing appeared in southern Mesopotamia more than 5,000 years ago, towards the end of the fourth millennium BCE. It started as small pictures of things, such as a cow's head or an ear of wheat, which temple officials used to record grain and livestock. Over time the pictures turned into marks made of wedge-shaped strokes, and that is where the name **cuneiform** comes from: *cuneus* is Latin for "wedge".

A scribe pressed a cut reed into a tablet of soft clay, then left it to dry in the sun or baked it hard. That is exactly why hundreds of thousands of tablets have survived: baked clay lasts for millennia where paper rots.

Moving from a picture of a cow to signs that stand for sounds and words was a huge mental leap. Writing let people record sales, send letters, publish laws and keep stories, among them the **Epic of Gilgamesh**, one of the oldest known works of literature.

> **Did you know?** Splitting an hour into 60 minutes, a minute into 60 seconds and a circle into 360 degrees all trace back to the base-60 counting system used in Mesopotamia. Sixty is handy because it divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30.

## The laws of Hammurabi

Hammurabi was the sixth king of the First Dynasty of Babylon and ruled for about 42 years in the 18th century BCE. His best-known legacy is a set of laws carved on a black stone stele more than two metres tall, which scholars usually divide into about 282 laws.

At the top, a carving shows Hammurabi standing before the Babylonian god of justice; below it the laws run in cuneiform. They cover everyday life: buying and selling, wages, farming and irrigation, family and inheritance, and punishments. Penalties often depended on a person's social class, which is one big difference from how we think of justice today.

The stele was found at the site of ancient Susa, in Iran, in the winter of 1901–1902. It had been carried there as war plunder in ancient times, and it now stands in the Louvre in Paris. If a teacher asks why it matters, the answer is that it is one of the oldest and most complete law collections to survive, and it was put on public display so that people could know their rights and duties.

## Buildings, science and farming

The signature building of a Mesopotamian city was the **ziggurat**: a tall temple built in stepped layers, each smaller than the one below, from mud brick and baked brick. The remains of the Ziggurat of Ur still stand in southern Iraq.

Farming depended on a wide network of irrigation canals and earth dams, since the south gets too little rain to grow crops without river water. Archaeological evidence suggests the wheel was among the early inventions of the region, around the middle of the fourth millennium BCE, first for making pottery and later for carts.

Priests tracked the Moon and planets and recorded what they saw on tablets, built a lunar calendar, and solved arithmetic and geometry problems that were advanced for their time. The Assyrian king Ashurbanipal gathered a vast library of clay tablets at Nineveh, and thousands of them are now held in the British Museum.

The **Ishtar Gate** was built in Babylon by Nebuchadnezzar II and faced with glazed blue bricks showing lions, bulls and dragons. It has been reconstructed from its original pieces in the Pergamon Museum in Berlin.

## Questions students ask about Mesopotamia

### How was Mesopotamia different from ancient Egypt?

Both grew up along rivers at roughly the same time. But the Nile flooded on a regular schedule and Egypt was shielded by desert on both sides, so it stayed united for long stretches. The Tigris and Euphrates flooded less predictably and Mesopotamia was open on all sides, so one people after another moved in. Read the lesson on [ancient Egyptian civilization](/en/articles/ancient-egyptian-civilization/) and compare for yourself.

### Why did Mesopotamians write and build with clay?

Because clay was everywhere along the rivers, while stone and timber were scarce on the southern plain. They shaped it into bricks for houses and temples, and into tablets for writing.
