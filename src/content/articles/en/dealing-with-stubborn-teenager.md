Yesterday your little one ran to tell you everything; today they close the bedroom door, answer in one word and push back on every request. If that sounds like life with your son or daughter, take heart: **you're not alone, and this is a normal stage** most teenagers go through. What matters most is how you respond to it.

## Why do teenagers become stubborn?

1. **The brain is still developing:** the front part of the brain responsible for planning and controlling emotions keeps maturing until roughly the mid-twenties, while the emotional centres are very active. That's why teens can act impulsively.
2. **The search for independence:** they want to prove they're no longer children and that their opinions count.
3. **Building an identity:** they're asking "who am I?" and "what do I want?", and trying out new ideas.
4. **Physical and hormonal changes** that affect mood.
5. **Pressure from friends, school** and social media.

Understanding these reasons doesn't mean accepting any behaviour, but it helps you respond calmly instead of angrily.

## 10 tips for dealing with a stubborn teenager

### 1. Listen more than you talk

When your teen speaks, listen without interrupting or lecturing. Sometimes they need someone to hear them, not advise them, and listening opens the door to conversation.

### 2. Choose your battles

Not every disagreement is worth a fight. A haircut or clothing colour matters less than school, values and safety. Focus on the big things and let the small ones go.

### 3. Set clear rules with agreed consequences

Agree together on rules, such as curfew and phone time, and on what happens if they're broken. Clear rules agreed in advance reduce arguments.

### 4. Stay calm and don't shout

Shouting and insults increase stubbornness without changing behaviour, and studies suggest that ongoing harsh verbal discipline is linked to more behavioural and emotional problems in teens. If you're angry, take a break and come back to the conversation later.

### 5. Respect their privacy, within limits

Teenagers need personal space. Knock before entering and don't read their messages without a serious reason, while keeping safety non-negotiable.

### 6. Spend enjoyable time together

A simple shared activity, such as a drive, a match, cooking or a game, builds trust more than any advice. Family meals are a golden opportunity to talk.

### 7. Praise effort, not just results

Say "I noticed how hard you studied this week". Encouraging effort builds their confidence and motivation.

### 8. Offer choices and responsibilities

Instead of orders, offer options: "Do you want to study before or after dinner?" Give them real responsibilities at home; it makes them feel grown-up and trusted.

### 9. Be a role model

Don't ask them to put the phone down if you never do. Teenagers learn more from what they see than from what they hear.

### 10. Always keep the door open

Let them know they can bring you any problem without fear of punishment or mockery. That sense of safety is what makes them turn to you rather than to strangers.

> **Did you know?** The World Health Organization defines adolescence as ages **10 to 19**, so some of its signs can start as early as the end of primary school.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Comparing them** with siblings or other people's children.
- **Embarrassing them** in public or in front of friends.
- **Physical punishment**: it harms the relationship and the teen's mental health without teaching better behaviour.
- **Threatening punishments you won't carry out**, which makes your words lose their weight.

## When should you seek professional help?

Talk to a **school counsellor** or **psychologist** if you notice:

- Ongoing sadness or withdrawal lasting weeks.
- A sudden, sharp drop in school performance.
- Big changes in sleep or eating.
- Talk of self-harm or hopelessness, which needs help right away.
- Suspected smoking or substance use.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I take away my teen's phone as a punishment?

It's better to agree on clear phone rules in advance than to confiscate it suddenly. If the agreement is broken, make the consequence one you agreed on, with a set time limit.

### My teenager never talks to me. What should I do?

Don't push with questions. Choose calm moments, such as during a car ride, and share something about your own day first. Conversation comes with time and trust.

## Key takeaway

A teenager's stubbornness is usually a normal part of their journey towards independence. The best approach combines listening, calm, clear rules and time together, while avoiding shouting, comparisons and humiliation. Remember: your relationship with your child matters more than winning any argument.
