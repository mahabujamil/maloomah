Not long ago your child came running out of the school gate to tell you about their day. Now they walk in, answer "fine", and shut the bedroom door. Every small request, from making the bed to putting the phone down, turns into an argument. If you're searching for how to deal with a stubborn teenager, start here: **this is normal, and most parents go through it**. How you respond makes a big difference to whether it settles down or gets worse.

## Why do teenagers become stubborn?

Patience comes more easily once you know what's going on in a teenager's head. The front part of the brain, which handles planning and self-control, keeps maturing until roughly the mid-twenties, while the emotional centres are very active during the teenage years. The brakes aren't finished yet but the engine is running at full power, which is why teens can act on impulse and regret it later.

On top of that, they want to prove they're no longer children and that their opinions deserve to be heard. They're also working out who they are and what they want, trying on ideas that may well annoy their parents. Add the physical and hormonal changes that affect mood, plus pressure from friends, school and social media, and the pushback starts to make sense.

None of this means you have to accept any behaviour. It does help you see stubbornness as a bid for independence rather than a personal attack, so you can answer calmly instead of angrily.

## 10 tips for dealing with a stubborn teenager

### 1. Listen more than you talk

When your teen speaks, listen without interrupting or lecturing. Sometimes they need to be heard, not advised. Listening now is what opens the door to real conversations later.

### 2. Choose your battles

Not every disagreement is worth a fight. A haircut or the colour of a hoodie matters far less than school, values and safety. Focus on the big things and let the small ones go.

### 3. Set clear rules with agreed consequences

Sit down together and agree on rules, such as curfew and phone time, and on what happens if they're broken. A rule a teenager helped write is hard to argue with, and a consequence everyone knew about in advance doesn't feel unfair when it's applied.

### 4. Stay calm and don't shout

Shouting and insults make stubbornness worse without changing behaviour. Studies suggest that ongoing harsh verbal discipline is linked to more behavioural and emotional problems in teens. If you feel your temper rising, say "Let's finish this in a bit", step away, and come back when you've cooled down.

### 5. Respect their privacy, within limits

Teenagers need a space of their own where they feel grown-up. Knock before going in, and don't read their messages without a serious reason. Safety still comes first, though: if there are real warning signs, their wellbeing outweighs their privacy.

### 6. Spend enjoyable time together

A simple shared activity, such as a drive, a match, cooking or a game, builds more trust than any amount of advice. Family meals remain one of the easiest chances to talk without it feeling planned.

### 7. Praise effort, not just results

Try "I noticed how hard you studied this week." Praising effort shows teens that what they control is what counts, which builds confidence and keeps them trying even when the grade isn't what they hoped.

### 8. Offer choices and responsibilities

Swap orders for options: "Do you want to study before dinner or after?" The outcome is the same, but the decision feels like theirs. Real responsibilities at home make them feel grown-up and trusted.

### 9. Be a role model

Don't ask them to put the phone away at dinner while yours is in your hand. Teenagers learn far more from what they see than from what they're told,.

### 10. Always keep the door open

Tell them they can bring you any problem without fear of punishment or mockery, then prove it when they do. That sense of safety is what makes a teen turn to you, rather than to strangers, when they're in trouble.

> **Did you know?** The World Health Organization defines adolescence as ages 10 to 19, so some of its signs can show up in the last years of primary school.

## Common mistakes to avoid

Some reactions feel firm in the moment but undo all the good work above. Comparing your teen with siblings or other people's children rarely motivates them; it mostly makes them feel they don't measure up. Embarrassing them in public or in front of friends leaves a mark they remember for a long time.

Physical punishment harms both the relationship and the teen's mental health, and it doesn't teach better behaviour. What it mostly teaches is how to hide mistakes. Finally, avoid threatening punishments you won't carry out. Empty threats make your words lose their weight.

## When should you seek professional help?

Most stubbornness fades with time and patience, but some signs go beyond normal teenage moodiness. Talk to a **school counsellor** or a psychologist if you notice:

- Ongoing sadness or withdrawal lasting weeks.
- A sudden, sharp drop in school performance.
- Big changes in sleep or eating.
- Talk of self-harm or hopelessness, which needs help right away.
- Suspected smoking or substance use.

## Questions parents ask about stubborn teens

### Should I take away my teen's phone as a punishment?

It works better to agree on clear phone rules in advance than to confiscate it out of the blue. If the agreement is broken, use the consequence you agreed on, with a set time limit, so it doesn't turn into an open-ended standoff.

### My teenager never talks to me. What should I do?

Don't push with questions. Pick calm moments, such as a car ride, where you sit side by side rather than face to face, and share something about your own day first. Conversation comes with time and trust, and your relationship with your child matters more than winning any argument.
